Lesson 5 · ~16 min · Level 2-3: Strategic Games

Matching Pennies, Mixed Strategies & Expected Value

A game with no stable answer at all — until you allow players to randomize on purpose.

Recap: Lesson 3 had one pure equilibrium (via dominant strategies). Lesson 4 had two. This lesson has zero — in pure strategies, at least — which forces a genuinely new idea: sometimes the smartest thing a rational player can do is be unpredictable, on purpose, in a precise and calculable way.

The game

Two players each secretly place a coin showing Heads or Tails, then reveal simultaneously. If the coins match (both Heads or both Tails), Player 1 ("Even") keeps both coins. If they don't match, Player 2 ("Odd") keeps both. Purely zero-sum — Odd's gain is always exactly Even's loss.

Even's payoff (amber) first, Odd's (blue) second. In coins, so +1/−1.
Odd: Heads Odd: Tails
Even: Heads +1, −1 −1, +1
Even: Tails −1, +1 +1, −1

Why no pure strategy is ever stable

Try to find a Nash equilibrium the way Lessons 3 and 4 did — pick a cell, check if either player wants to switch. Take (Heads, Heads): Even is winning (+1), so Even's happy — but Odd is losing, and Odd can flip to Tails and win instead. So Odd moves. Now you're at (Heads, Tails) — but now Even is losing, and Even can flip to Tails to win. That sends you to (Tails, Tails) — and now Odd wants to move again. Every single cell has someone who wants to unilaterally switch. There is no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium in this game at all — the first time in this course that's happened.

The deeper reason, not just the mechanical one Any predictable pure strategy is exploitable here. If Even always plays Heads, Odd learns that and always plays Tails to win every round. The instant your strategy is knowable in advance, in a purely zero-sum, match/mismatch game, the other player can exploit it perfectly. The fix isn't a smarter fixed choice — there isn't one — it's to stop being predictable at all.

Expected value, in plain English first

Before the fix makes sense, you need one piece of math: expected value is just the average outcome you'd get if a random event happened many times, weighted by how likely each result is. If a bet pays +₹10 with 50% chance and −₹6 with 50% chance, the expected value is 0.5 × 10 + 0.5 × (−6) = 5 − 3 = 2 — meaning if you took this exact bet over and over, you'd average +₹2 per round, even though any single round is either +10 or −6, never +2.

Why you need it here: once a player randomizes, "their payoff" isn't one number anymore — it's a range of possible outcomes depending on how the coin lands. Expected value is how you compare two randomized strategies on equal footing.

The mixed-strategy equilibrium

A mixed strategy is a plan to randomize over your available actions with specific probabilities, rather than committing to one action for certain. Here's the key insight for finding the right probability: you should randomize in exactly the way that makes your opponent indifferent between their own options — if they'd still prefer one action over the other even against your randomization, you're still exploitable, just less obviously.

Let Even play Heads with probability p (and Tails with probability 1−p). Work out Odd's expected payoff from each of Odd's own pure choices, against that mix:

  • Odd plays Heads: gets −1 when Even plays Heads (prob p), +1 when Even plays Tails (prob 1−p). Expected value: −1(p) + 1(1−p) = 1 − 2p
  • Odd plays Tails: gets +1 when Even plays Heads (prob p), −1 when Even plays Tails (prob 1−p). Expected value: 1(p) − 1(1−p) = 2p − 1

For Even's mix to leave Odd with no exploitable preference, these must be equal: 1 − 2p = 2p − 1 → 2 = 4p → p = 0.5. Even should play Heads exactly half the time. By the matrix's symmetry, the same logic makes Odd's equilibrium mix 50/50 too. Real-life interpretation: in a game this perfectly symmetric, "flip a fair coin" isn't a cop-out — it's the mathematically optimal, unexploitable strategy, and it's the only stable one.

The general recipe, once you see it You don't always land on 50/50 — that's specific to this symmetric game. The method generalizes: to find your own equilibrium mixing probability, set your opponent's two expected payoffs equal to each other and solve. It feels backwards the first time (why am I solving for their indifference to find my own move?) — but it's correct: your randomization exists specifically to deny them an exploitable pattern, so it's calibrated to their payoffs, not yours.

Recognizing it outside a coin game

  • Penalty kicks in football. The kicker picks a side, the goalkeeper dives a side, simultaneously. If either side is predictable, the other exploits it perfectly — economists who studied professional penalty kicks (Ignacio Palacios-Huerta's work is the well-known version) found real players' left/right splits land remarkably close to the game-theoretic mixed-strategy prediction, not 50/50 exactly (their payoffs aren't perfectly symmetric — some players are stronger kicking one side), but close to what the math predicts for their actual payoff structure.
  • Audits and spot-checks. A manager deciding whether to review an employee's work, and an employee deciding whether to cut corners, form the same shape: if the manager always checks on Fridays, the employee learns to be careful only on Fridays. The unexploitable move is a genuinely randomized inspection schedule, calibrated (per the recipe above) to make cutting corners not worth the expected risk.
  • Pricing and promotions. A retailer deciding when to run surprise discounts, against shoppers deciding when to buy, has the same tension — fully predictable sale dates get gamed by shoppers who just wait for them.
Go deeper (optional — this lesson is complete on its own) William Spaniel's Game Theory 101 has a dedicated mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium walkthrough using this exact game, if you want to see the algebra done a second way. Ben Polak's Yale course covers the penalty-kick evidence directly.

Your turn — reason first, then reveal
A manager can spot-check an employee's report (costs the manager 1 hour) or not check. The employee can submit carefully (safe, no bonus either way) or cut corners to save time. Payoffs: if the manager checks and the employee cut corners, the employee is caught (employee: −4, manager: +2, since the problem gets fixed before it ships). If the manager checks and the employee was careful, the manager wasted an hour for nothing (employee: 0, manager: −1). If the manager doesn't check and the employee cut corners, the employee saves time and gets away with it (employee: +3, manager: −3, since a bad report ships). If the manager doesn't check and the employee was careful, both are fine (employee: 0, manager: +1).

Build the matrix. Confirm there's no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (check all four cells the way this lesson did for the coin game). Then set up the equation to find the manager's equilibrium checking probability — remember: you solve for the manager's mix by setting the employee's two expected payoffs equal.
No pure equilibrium: Same instability check as the coin game — if the manager never checks, the employee's best move is always cut corners (+3 beats 0); but if the employee always cuts corners, the manager's best move is always check (+2 beats −3); but if the manager always checks, the employee's best move is always be careful (0 beats −4); but if the employee is always careful, the manager's best move is never check (+1 beats −1) — which brings you back to the start. Every cell has someone wanting to switch, exactly like the coin game.

Setting up the manager's mix: Let the manager check with probability p. The employee's expected payoff from cutting corners: p(−4) + (1−p)(3) = 3 − 7p. The employee's expected payoff from being careful: p(0) + (1−p)(0) = 0. Setting them equal: 3 − 7p = 0 → p = 3/7 ≈ 43%. The manager should check a little under half the time — not because "check often" or "check rarely" is inherently right, but because that's the exact rate that leaves the employee with no incentive to cut corners at all.

If you noticed the answer isn't 50% and wondered why — that's the right instinct. 50/50 was specific to Matching Pennies' perfectly symmetric payoffs. Here the payoffs are lopsided (getting away with cutting corners is worth more to the employee than the manager's cost of a wasted check), and the equilibrium probability shifts accordingly. That asymmetry-sensitivity is exactly why the "set the opponent's payoffs equal and solve" recipe matters more than memorizing 50/50.
