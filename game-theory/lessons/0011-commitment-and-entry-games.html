Lesson 11 · ~16 min · Level 4: Sequential Games (closing)

Commitment, Deterrence & Market Entry

Your own example from the start of this course, finally worked properly: should a company enter a competitor's market?

Recap: Lesson 10 gave you backward induction and the credibility test (subgame perfect equilibrium). This lesson applies both to the exact situation named in this workspace's mission — market entry — and shows precisely how a threat that looks non-credible can be made real through investment made in advance.

The entry game

An Entrant is deciding whether to enter a market currently held by an Incumbent monopolist. If the Entrant Stays Out, nothing changes. If the Entrant Enters, the Incumbent then chooses whether to Fight (a price war, expensive for both) or Accommodate (share the market peacefully).

Entrant moves first: Stay Out, or Enter
Stay Out →
Payoffs: Entrant = 0, Incumbent = 5 (kept monopoly)
Enter →
Incumbent responds: Fight, or Accommodate
Fight →
Payoffs: Entrant = −3, Incumbent = −1
Accommodate →
Payoffs: Entrant = 2, Incumbent = 2

Backward induction says the incumbent's threat is empty

Solve from the last decision: if entry has already happened, the Incumbent compares Fight (−1) to Accommodate (2) — and Accommodate wins. Fighting is genuinely worse for the Incumbent too, once entry is a done deal; the price war costs the Incumbent more than just quietly sharing the market. So a rational Incumbent accommodates.

Knowing this, the Entrant compares Stay Out (0) to Enter (which now actually leads to 2, since Fight was never really on the table). Entering wins. The predicted outcome is (2, 2), and the Incumbent's implicit "if you enter, I'll fight" threat is exactly the non-credible kind Lesson 7 and Lesson 10 taught you to distrust — it sounds intimidating stated in advance, but fails the subgame-perfection test: it's not actually the Incumbent's best move once entry happens.

Making the threat real: strategic commitment

The Incumbent clearly prefers keeping its monopoly (5) to sharing the market (2) — deterring entry entirely would be worth a lot. The problem is purely that "I'll fight" isn't believable as stated. Three real ways to fix that, each of which works by changing the Incumbent's actual future payoffs, not by saying the threat louder:

  • Sunk capacity investment. If the Incumbent builds significant excess production capacity before the Entrant decides — capacity that only makes sense if it's later used to flood the market with supply and crush prices — fighting becomes genuinely cheap for the Incumbent once entry happens (that capacity is a sunk cost either way, so using it barely lowers the Incumbent's payoff). This can flip Fight from −1 to, say, +3 in the tree above — Fight now actually beats Accommodate, making the threat credible for real, and a rational Entrant who sees this evidence should predict Fight and Stay Out.
  • Reputation across multiple markets. An Incumbent facing entrants in many separate markets over time has an incentive to actually fight in one market — even at a short-term loss — specifically to build a reputation that deters entrants everywhere else. This only works as a repeated-game argument (full treatment next lesson), but the intuition matters now: a threat that's individually irrational in one isolated market can become genuinely credible once it's serving a reputation that pays off across many.
  • Contractual commitments. Publicly signed exclusive-supplier or price-matching agreements can bind the Incumbent's own hands in a way that makes aggressive response automatic and visible — the same "burn the steering wheel" logic from Lesson 7, applied to a business rather than a car.
First-mover advantage, precisely defined This is what "first-mover advantage" actually means in game-theoretic terms: moving first lets you make a commitment — capacity, contracts, sunk investment — that changes the game your opponent then has to respond to, rather than reacting to theirs. It's not an advantage from being first in a calendar sense; it's an advantage from being able to commit before the other side has to decide. Second-mover advantage is the mirror case: sometimes waiting to see what the first mover actually does — and how the market responds — lets you react with better information, avoiding a costly commitment made too early. Neither is universally better; which one wins depends on whether commitment power or information matters more in the specific game.

Now your own example

This is the entry game named in this workspace's mission from the very first lesson: "should my company enter a competitor's market." Here's the actual checklist this lesson gives you, not a generic pep talk:

1. Map the tree
Your move (Enter/Stay Out) first, their response (Fight/Accommodate) second — get real rough numbers for all four/three outcomes, even approximate ones.
2. Solve backward
Is Fight actually better for them than Accommodate, once entry has happened — given their real current cost structure, not just their stated posture?
3. Look for commitment evidence
Have they made sunk investments (capacity, exclusive contracts) that would make fighting cheap for them? Do they have a track record of fighting entrants elsewhere (reputation)?
4. Predict, don't assume
If the evidence for credible commitment is weak or absent, backward induction says Accommodate is their real best response — the "they'll crush us" fear may itself be a non-credible threat you're reacting to.
Go deeper (optional — this lesson is complete on its own) This entry-deterrence model traces to work by Avinash Dixit and others in the late 1970s-80s on strategic commitment; it's covered directly in Dixit & Nalebuff's The Art of Strategy (see RESOURCES.md) with several more real corporate examples.

Game Theory Detective — and your real situation

Your turn — reason first, then reveal
A mid-sized SaaS company is deciding whether to launch a product competing directly with a larger, established incumbent's flagship offering. The incumbent has publicly stated it will "aggressively defend its market position." The incumbent has not made any visible new infrastructure investment recently, has a history of quietly acquiring or ignoring smaller competitors rather than price-warring them, and its cost structure means an actual price war would cut deeply into its own profitable core business.

Using the four-step checklist above: is the incumbent's public threat credible? What should the smaller company predict, and why? Then — this is the part that matters most — try the same four steps on your actual market-entry situation, if you have one in mind, and bring me the real numbers and evidence you come up with.
Credibility check: The evidence points toward non-credible. A public statement alone is cheap talk (Lesson 7's non-credible threat, restated). The absence of new infrastructure investment means there's no sunk-cost logic making Fight cheap. A history of acquiring or ignoring rather than price-warring smaller entrants is direct behavioral evidence against a fighting reputation. And a price war genuinely hurting the incumbent's own profitable core business means Fight likely isn't even their own best move once entry happens — the same structural gap as the base entry game.

Prediction: Backward induction says Accommodate is the incumbent's real best response despite the public posture, and the smaller company's rational move is to Enter — the threat is very likely bluster rather than a credible deterrent, though it's worth staying alert for any of the three commitment signals (capacity, reputation, contracts) appearing later, since a threat's credibility can change.
