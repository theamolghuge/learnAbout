Lesson 4 · ~13 min · Level 2-3: Strategic Games

Battle of the Sexes

Two Nash equilibria, no dominant strategy anywhere — the coordination problem Lesson 3 promised was coming.

Recap: Lesson 3's Prisoner's Dilemma had one Nash equilibrium, reached through dominant strategies for both players. This game has neither — two stable outcomes, and no single "always-best" move for anyone. That gap is the entire lesson.

The story

Meera and Karan are close friends who'd rather spend the evening together than apart — that part isn't in dispute. But they disagree on what to do: Meera wants to go to a concert, Karan wants to go to a cricket match. They have to choose without talking first (phones are dead, assume no coordination).

Meera's payoff (amber) first, Karan's (blue) second. Both circled cells are Nash equilibria.
Karan: Concert Karan: Cricket
Meera: Concert 2, 1 0, 0
Meera: Cricket 0, 0 1, 2

Why there's no dominant strategy here

Run Lesson 3's test on Meera: hold Karan's action fixed and check both cases.

  • If Karan picks Concert: Meera gets 2 for Concert, 0 for Cricket. Concert is better.
  • If Karan picks Cricket: Meera gets 0 for Concert, 1 for Cricket. Cricket is better.

Her best move flips depending on what Karan does — that's the definition of not having a dominant strategy. Whatever she should do depends entirely on a correct guess about him, and vice versa. Neither player can plan in isolation the way Anjali and Bilal could in Lesson 3.

Two Nash equilibria, not zero, not one

Check (Concert, Concert): if Meera switches to Cricket alone, she goes from 2 to 0 — worse. If Karan switches to Cricket alone, he goes from 1 to 0 — worse. Neither wants to move unilaterally, so it's a Nash equilibrium, by the exact same definition from Lesson 3. Now check (Cricket, Cricket): same logic, same conclusion. Both outcomes are equally valid Nash equilibria — the concept doesn't pick a winner between them on its own.

This is a genuinely different kind of problem Lesson 3's dilemma was: both players know exactly what they should do (defect), and the tragedy is that it's individually correct but jointly bad. This game's problem is the opposite: both players agree that together beats apart — there's no temptation to betray anyone — but they still might fail to coordinate, purely from not knowing which of two good outcomes the other is aiming for. Same tool (Nash equilibrium), completely different real-world failure mode.

Retrieval check

1. Why does Meera not have a dominant strategy in this game?
2. How many Nash equilibria does this particular game have (in pure strategies)?

Solving it: focal points

If both equilibria are equally "valid" by the theory, how do real people actually coordinate? Thomas Schelling's answer, from the same book that gave this course its negotiation lessons later: people converge on a focal point (also called a Schelling point) — an option that stands out from the others for reasons the formal payoffs don't capture, but that both players can guess the other will also notice.

Schelling's original example Told to meet a stranger in New York City tomorrow, with no way to communicate where or when, most people independently pick the information booth at Grand Central Terminal, at noon. Nothing in the payoffs says "Grand Central" — it works because it's the choice each person expects the other to expect them to make. That's a focal point.

For Meera and Karan, a focal point might be "whoever bought tickets first, in advance, effectively pre-commits the other" (this borders on Level 4's idea of commitment), or a standing convention like "we always default to whichever event is time-sensitive" (the concert has a fixed start time; the cricket match doesn't). Notice neither of these came from the payoff matrix — they came from context outside it, which is exactly what a focal point is.

Recognizing it outside picking an evening plan

  • Tech stack choice between co-founders. Both prefer building the same product together over splitting effort on separate stacks — but one prefers React, the other Vue. Either choice, made together, beats going separate ways; the friction is purely about which.
  • Long-distance relationships deciding whose city to move to. Both clearly prefer living together over apart; the coordination problem is entirely about which city, and it's easy to mistake this for the same fight as the Prisoner's Dilemma when it isn't — nobody here has an incentive to "defect" and live apart.
  • Two departments standardizing on one internal tool. Everyone's better off with one shared tool than two competing ones; the fight is only over which one becomes the default — and often gets resolved by a focal point like "whichever team already has more users" rather than a formal vote.
Go deeper (optional — this lesson is complete on its own) Ben Polak's Yale lectures on coordination games (search "Open Yale Econ 159 coordination") cover this alongside several other focal-point examples. Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (see RESOURCES.md) is the primary source for focal points themselves.

Game Theory Detective

Your turn — reason first, then reveal
Two coworkers, on a shared but flexible project, both strongly prefer working in the same tool over duplicating effort in two — a genuine Battle-of-the-Sexes setup, not a Prisoner's Dilemma (nobody benefits from splitting). One prefers Notion, the other prefers Google Docs.

Sketch the payoff matrix (rough numbers are fine). Confirm there's no dominant strategy and two Nash equilibria. Then — this is the actual point of the exercise — propose a real focal point that could resolve it without either of them having to "win" an argument, and explain why it would work as a focal point (what makes it obvious to both sides, not just convenient to one).
Matrix shape: Same structure as Meera/Karan — (Notion, Notion) and (Docs, Docs) both beat any split combination for both coworkers, and each coworker's preferred one gives them a slightly higher payoff than the other's preferred one. No dominant strategy, two Nash equilibria, exactly as expected.

A focal point that actually works: "Whichever tool the rest of the team/project already uses" is a strong focal point — it's not arbitrary to either coworker (so it doesn't feel like one side winning), and it's genuinely obvious to both of them independently, which is the actual test of a focal point (not "is it a good idea" but "would both of us have guessed the other would land here too"). A weaker, common wrong answer: "whoever suggests first should just decide" — that's not a focal point, it's just deference, and it breaks down the moment both people are equally assertive.

If your focal point was something like "flip a coin" — that's a real mechanism, but it's not a focal point in Schelling's sense; a coin flip works through randomization, not through both players independently recognizing the same obvious answer. Worth noticing the difference: focal points exploit shared context, randomization doesn't need any.
Bring me your actual answer Tell me what focal point you proposed and your reasoning for why it's focal rather than just "a fair-sounding rule." That distinction is genuinely subtle on a first pass — most people's first instinct is a fairness mechanism (coin flip, alternate weeks), not a true focal point, and both can work in practice, but only one is what this lesson is teaching.
What you should be able to do now Recognize when a disagreement is actually a coordination problem (everyone better off together, fighting only over which shared outcome) rather than a Prisoner's-Dilemma-shaped conflict of interest — and reach for a focal point, not a negotiation tactic, to resolve it.
