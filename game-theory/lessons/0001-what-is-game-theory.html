What Is Game Theory?

Not a branch of math about board games — a way of reasoning about any decision where the outcome depends on what someone else does too.

Tied to your mission: this lesson gives you the five-part vocabulary you'll use to take apart every real situation from here on — including the ₹25 LPA salary offer and the market-entry decision you mentioned. Nothing here yet tells you what to do in those situations; that comes once you have equilibrium (Lesson 3+) and negotiation (Level 8). Today's job is narrower: learn to see the pieces.

Why this field exists

Start with a decision that is not game theory: whether to bring an umbrella tomorrow. You look at a forecast, weigh "get wet" against "carry an umbrella for nothing," and pick. It's a real decision, it involves uncertainty, but there's no opponent in it. The weather doesn't check what you're planning and adjust. This is the domain of ordinary decision theory — one decision-maker against nature.

Now take a decision that looks similar on the surface but isn't: whether to lowball your opening ask in a salary negotiation. The moment you introduce another party who is also choosing a move, watching yours, and anticipating your response to theirs — the problem changes shape entirely. Your best move now depends on what they'll do. Their best move depends on what they think you'll do. And what they think you'll do depends on what they think you think they'll do. There's no fixed "forecast" to consult, because the other side is actively reasoning about you, the same way you're reasoning about them.

The one-sentence definition Game theory is the study of decisions where your best choice depends on someone else's choice, and theirs depends on yours. Wherever you find that loop — negotiation, pricing, hiring, dating, office politics, war, traffic — game theory applies. Wherever that loop is absent, it doesn't, no matter how complex or uncertain the decision is.

This is also why the field matters practically, not just academically: most of the decisions that actually shape your career, income, and relationships have another reasoning party inside them. A weather forecast can't out-think you. A hiring manager, a competitor, or a negotiating counterpart can — and does.

The anatomy of a game

Every strategic situation, no matter how complicated, is built from the same five parts. We'll build them up through one running example: you and a friend are choosing where to eat — Italian or Sushi.

1. Players

Whoever is making a decision that affects the outcome, and whose decision is affected by others' decisions in turn. In the restaurant example: you and your friend. Not the restaurant, not the waiter — they're not choosing strategically here, they're just the setting.

2. Actions (and Strategies)

An action is a single choice available at a given moment — here, each of you picks "Italian" or "Sushi." A strategy is bigger than that: it's a complete plan covering every situation you might face, including ones that depend on what the other player does. Right now, with one simultaneous choice, action and strategy are the same thing. They come apart once decisions happen in sequence (Level 4) — worth knowing the distinction exists now so it doesn't feel like a new idea later.

3. Payoffs (built from preferences + incentives)

What each player actually gets from each possible combination of choices — usually expressed as a number representing how good that outcome is for that player. Payoffs are downstream of two things: preferences (what you actually want — maybe you prefer Sushi to Italian) and incentives (what's pushing you toward or away from an option beyond raw preference — maybe Italian is walking distance and Sushi requires a cab). Payoffs bundle both into one number per outcome, which is what makes the game analyzable at all.

4. Information

What each player knows when they choose — about the game, about the other player's preferences, and (in sequential situations) about what the other player has already done. In the restaurant example, you probably know your friend hates raw fish; that's information shaping your prediction of their move. In a salary negotiation, you almost never know the company's actual ceiling — that gap is information asymmetry, and it will get its own module (Level 6) because of how much it changes optimal play.

5. Rules of the game

Everything that structures how play happens: who moves when, what's allowed, what each player can observe, when the game ends. Two friends picking a restaurant typically choose simultaneously (you both blurt out a preference) rather than sequentially (one commits first, the other responds) — and which of those two rules is actually in force changes what the smart move is, even though the players and payoffs are identical. You'll see exactly how in Level 4.

Players
You, your friend
Actions
Say "Italian" or "Sushi"
Payoffs
Own preference + convenience (distance, cost, mood)
Information
Each of you has a rough guess at the other's preference
Rules
Simultaneous, one round, plain conversation (no binding commitment)

Second worked example: the salary offer

You mentioned reasoning through a ₹25 LPA offer — here's the same five-part anatomy applied to it, in miniature. This isn't the negotiation lesson (that's Level 8, and it needs equilibrium and information asymmetry under it first) — it's just proof that the same five questions apply to a real, high-stakes situation exactly as they applied to picking a restaurant.

Players
You, the company (represented by a recruiter/hiring manager with their own incentives)
Actions
Accept, counter (and by how much), walk away, stall for time
Payoffs
You: income, growth, risk of losing the offer. Them: hiring you cheaply, filling the role fast, not overpaying relative to internal bands
Information
You don't know their budget ceiling or how badly they need this role filled. They don't know your other offers or your true walk-away point
Rules
Usually sequential (they offer, you respond), often with a real or implied deadline, and typically more than one round is allowed

Notice what's already visible just from naming the parts, before any equilibrium math: the two information gaps are where most of the real leverage in this situation lives. That's not a coincidence — it previews the entire Level 6/8 arc of this course (asymmetric information, signaling, negotiation).

Game Theory Detective

Your turn — reason first, then reveal
Two coworkers, Priya and Raj, are both up for the same single promotion. Their manager will decide based partly on visible output over the next quarter, and partly on who seems more "ready" in conversations. Priya and Raj are also friendly and occasionally cover for each other's mistakes.

Using the five parts from this lesson, write out: who the players are, what actions are actually available to them (not just "work hard" — be specific), what their payoffs look like, what information is asymmetric here, and what the rules of this particular game are (timing, who's watching, how many rounds).
Players: Priya, Raj — and arguably the manager, who isn't competing but whose evaluation rule shapes both players' best moves, which makes them a strategic third party even though they're not "in the running."

Actions: More concrete than "work hard" — take on the highest-visibility project vs. the highest-impact-but-invisible one; speak up first in meetings or let the other go first; keep covering for the other's mistakes or stop; actively highlight your own wins to the manager or stay quiet and let work speak for itself.

Payoffs: The promotion itself (money, title, trajectory) for the winner; for the loser, the promotion lost plus whatever the covering-for-each-other relationship is now worth (or costs) going forward. This is the detail worth noticing: the payoff isn't just "get the promotion" — it includes what happens to the friendship and future cooperation, win or lose.

Information: Neither Priya nor Raj can fully see how the manager weighs "visible output" against "seeming ready," or how the manager is currently leaning. Each also doesn't fully know how hard the other is actually pushing behind the scenes.

Rules: One quarter, one promotion (not splittable), manager observes some things directly and infers others, and — this is the detail that makes it interesting — the "covering for mistakes" behavior is itself an ongoing side-game layered on top of the competition, not separate from it.

If your answer named the friendship/covering-for-each-other dynamic as part of the payoffs or rules, that's the sharp catch — it's what turns this from a plain competition into something closer to a repeated game with mixed cooperation and competition, which is exactly the territory Level 5 (repeated games) is built for.

What's next

This lesson deliberately stopped short of a few Level-1 ideas — rationality, common knowledge, zero-sum vs. non-zero-sum, and cooperative vs. non-cooperative games — because five new terms plus two worked examples is already a full working-memory load for one sitting. Those finish Level 1 in Lesson 2, and they matter: the promotion scenario above, for instance, is a mostly non-zero-sum game wearing a zero-sum costume (only one person gets the title, but the total payoff — including the relationship — isn't fixed), which is exactly the kind of distinction Lesson 2 will train you to catch automatically.

What you should be able to do now Take any real situation involving another person and immediately ask: who are the players, what can each actually do, what does each want, who knows what, and what are the rules of engagement (timing, rounds, observability)? That's it — that's the whole win for today. Equilibrium, "the right move," and negotiation tactics all come later and all build on this.
