# Mission: Game Theory Through Real-Life Application

## Why
Not academic curiosity — you want a working lens you can drop onto any real situation involving other people with their own incentives (a salary negotiation, a promotion cycle, a company deciding whether to enter a competitor's market, a coworker you don't fully trust) and immediately see the structure underneath: who's playing, what they want, what they know, what they'll do next. The explicit bar you set yourself: not "I know game theory" but **"I can see the game."** Two of your own examples anchor this — reasoning through a ₹25 LPA salary offer, and analyzing whether "my company should enter a competitor's market" as a strategic-form problem, not a gut call.

## Success looks like
- Given any real situation you're currently in, you can name the players, their actions, their payoffs, and what information each side has — without being told to.
- You can walk into a negotiation (salary, freelance rate, a deal) having explicitly reasoned about your BATNA, their BATNA, the ZOPA, and what you should and shouldn't reveal — not just "ask for more and see what happens."
- You can look at a classic strategic pattern (Prisoner's Dilemma, Chicken, a signaling game, an auction) playing out in your career, a business, or the news, and name it correctly instead of just sensing "something competitive is happening."
- You can distinguish a one-shot interaction from a repeated one and explain why the rational move differs between them — and you use this distinction deliberately (e.g., with a manager, a recurring vendor, a repeat customer).
- You can read a costly signal (a warranty, a portfolio, a credential, someone burning a bridge) and separate it from cheap talk.
- You know when game theory is the wrong tool — where real humans violate rationality, common knowledge, or stable-preference assumptions — so you don't over-apply it.
- You've used the one-page Game Theory Decision Framework (Level 15 of your own brief) on at least one live decision, not just a hypothetical.

## Constraints
- Feynman technique, explicitly requested: intuition and "why" before formalism, minimal jargon, math introduced only when it earns its keep and always explained in plain English first.
- Interactive learning cycle, explicitly requested: **Explain → Example → Test → Scenario → Your Answer → Feedback → Deeper Concept → New Scenario.** Lessons should ask you questions and let you reason before revealing answers — not just narrate at you.
- No time budget confirmed yet — assume short, self-contained lessons (one tangible win, ~10-20 min) until you say otherwise, consistent with how the other workspaces in this repo are paced.
- You want your own real-life situations analyzed as they come up, using: Players → Incentives → Information → Strategies → Payoffs → Timing → Repeated Interaction → Equilibrium → Alternatives → Best Response. Bring them any time — this isn't limited to the queued lesson plan.
- Self-contained guide, per this repo's established convention (see [[NOTES.md]]): lessons teach the full concept on their own; external sources are for trust and optional depth, not prerequisites.

## Out of scope
- Graduate-level formal proofs and solver-grade mathematics (this was explicitly de-prioritized in favor of intuition — math is introduced only as far as it sharpens real decisions).
- Deep behavioral tell-reading, body language, and manipulation-resistance tradecraft — that's already owned by your [[reading-people-influence-strategy]] workspace, which explicitly deferred *formal* game theory back to here. This workspace owns the strategic-structure side (incentives, equilibria, signaling-as-information); that one owns the behavioral/nonverbal side. Cross-link rather than duplicate.
- Predicting specific market movements or giving investment advice — investing is covered only for its strategic-reasoning content (expectations, herd behavior, coordination), never as a forecasting tool.
