The Game Theory Decision Framework

Twenty questions, in the order it actually pays to ask them. Applies to any real situation involving other people with their own incentives — this is the whole point of this workspace, compressed onto one page.

1. Map the game (Lesson 1)

  1. Who are the players? Include anyone whose choice affects the outcome and is affected by others' choices — not just the obvious two sides.
  2. What does each player want? Their real preferences, not the stated position.
  3. What actions can each player take? Be concrete — not "work hard," but the specific real moves available.
  4. What information does each player have? What they know about the game, and about each other.
  5. What does each player believe about what the others will do, and about what the others believe?
  6. What are the incentives pushing each player toward or away from each action?
  7. What are the possible outcomes, and what does each player's payoff look like under each one?

2. Solve it (Lessons 2-6)

  1. What is each player's best response to each of the other player's possible moves?
  2. Is there a dominant strategy — a move that's best regardless of what anyone else does?
  3. Is there a Nash equilibrium — a combination where nobody wants to switch unilaterally? Check for more than one.
  4. Is the equilibrium actually good for everyone, or is there a Pareto-better outcome nobody can reach alone (Prisoner's Dilemma, Public Goods, Tragedy of the Commons)?

3. Check the dynamics (Lessons 4, 10-12)

  1. Is this a one-shot game or a repeated one — and if repeated, is the end known and finite (cooperation unravels) or genuinely uncertain (cooperation can be sustained)?
  2. Who moves first, and does that let them commit to something before the other side decides?
  3. What commitments are possible, and are they actually credible — visible and costly to reverse — or just stated?
  4. What threats are credible? Check: is this still the threatening player's own best move once the moment to carry it out actually arrives (subgame perfection)?

4. Check the information (Lessons 13-14)

  1. What is being signaled, and is it a credible, differentially-costly signal or just cheap talk?
  2. Is there asymmetric information — and is it a hidden-type problem (adverse selection, fix with signaling/screening) or a hidden-action problem (moral hazard, fix with monitoring/aligned incentives)?

5. Decide what to do (Lessons 5, 15, 17)

  1. What happens if someone defects — and can that be punished credibly (Grim Trigger, reputation, a real consequence)?
  2. What happens if cooperation continues — is there a real, sustainable path to the better equilibrium (small trust-building steps, staged commitments)?
  3. How can the rules of the game be changed? Before accepting a bad equilibrium as fixed, ask whether the game itself can be redesigned (mechanism design) — new incentives, a different contract structure, a different process entirely — rather than just playing better within the existing rules.
The one-sentence reminder to keep nearby Game theory is a framework for reasoning about strategic interactions, not a magic formula for predicting human behavior — treat the equilibrium prediction as a floor to reason from, then adjust for fairness, loss aversion, bounded rationality, and overconfidence where they're likely to bite (Lesson 17).
