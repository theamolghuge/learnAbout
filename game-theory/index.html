Teaching workspace

Game Theory Through Real-Life Application

Not the academic subject — a working lens for career, negotiation, business, relationships, and everyday decisions involving other people with their own incentives.

Mission: reach the point where you can look at a real situation and say "I can see the game" — name the players, their incentives, their information, and their likely best response, without being prompted. See MISSION.md. Everything below is self-contained: no lesson requires reading an external book first, though primary sources are linked throughout for depth and trust.

How this works

Each lesson follows the same loop: Explain → Example → Test → Scenario → Your Answer → Feedback → Deeper Concept. You'll get quizzes to check retrieval and "Game Theory Detective" scenarios where you reason first, in writing, before anything is revealed. Bring your own real situations any time — negotiations, career decisions, anything with another person's incentives in it — and I'll run them through the same framework live, not just the queued lessons below.

Roadmap — complete

All ten levels are now built, 17 lessons in total, from the five-part anatomy of a game through mechanism design and behavioral corrections. None of it has been checked against your actual answers yet — see the note in NOTES.md before assuming everything landed.

Level 1 · Lesson 1 · ~12 min
What Is Game Theory?
The five-part anatomy of any strategic situation — players, actions/strategies, payoffs, information, rules — via a restaurant choice and your own ₹25 LPA offer example. The foundation everything else builds on.
Level 1 · Lesson 2 · ~13 min
Rationality, Common Knowledge & the Shape of the Game
Finishes the foundations: what "rational" actually means here (and where it breaks), why common knowledge is stronger than "everyone knows," zero-sum vs. non-zero-sum, cooperative vs. non-cooperative games.
Level 2–3 · Lesson 3 · ~15 min
The Prisoner's Dilemma
Your first payoff matrix: dominant strategy, Nash equilibrium, and Pareto efficiency, via the classic interrogation story and a ride-hailing price-war detective scenario.
Level 2–3 · Lesson 4 · ~13 min
Battle of the Sexes
Two Nash equilibria, no dominant strategy, and Schelling's focal points as the real-world fix for pure coordination problems.
Level 2–3 · Lesson 5 · ~16 min
Matching Pennies, Mixed Strategies & Expected Value
A game with zero pure-strategy equilibria — why deliberate randomization is the rational fix, with the algebra for calculating your equilibrium mixing probability (penalty kicks, audits, spot-checks).
Level 2–3 · Lesson 6 · ~13 min
Stag Hunt: Trust & Risk-Dominant Equilibria
Two Nash equilibria where everyone agrees which is better, and groups still get stuck on the worse one — payoff-dominant vs. risk-dominant, and how small trust-building steps shift the outcome.
Level 2–3 · Lesson 7 · ~15 min
Chicken: Credible Threats & Brinkmanship
Anti-coordination, Schelling's "burn the steering wheel," and what makes a commitment credible rather than just loudly stated — plus the mixed-strategy math applied to a non-zero-sum game.
Level 2–3 · Lesson 8 · ~14 min
Ultimatum & Dictator Games
Where the naive rational prediction is confidently wrong — real Responders reject unfair offers, real Proposers offer far more than the minimum, and the Dictator Game isolates genuine fairness from fear of rejection.
Level 2–3 · Lesson 9 · ~14 min
Public Goods, Free-Riding & the Tragedy of the Commons
The Prisoner's Dilemma at group scale — under-giving to shared benefits and over-taking from shared resources — plus three real structural fixes, including Elinor Ostrom's Nobel-winning counter-evidence that "tragedy" isn't inevitable.
Level 4 · Lesson 10 · ~15 min
Game Trees, Backward Induction & the Trust Game
Formal backward induction and subgame perfect equilibrium, via the Trust Game — Stag Hunt's trust problem played sequentially, and why the mutually-better outcome goes unreached absent a credible commitment.
Level 4 · Lesson 11 · ~16 min
Commitment, Deterrence & Market Entry
The entry-deterrence game, first/second-mover advantage, and this workspace's own mission example finally worked properly: should a company enter a competitor's market, and is the incumbent's threat actually credible?
Level 5 · Lesson 12 · ~16 min
Repeated Games, Reputation & Tit-for-Tat
Why finite, known-end repetition still unravels to defection (backward induction again) while indefinite repetition can sustain cooperation — Grim Trigger, Tit-for-Tat, and Axelrod's tournament results.
Level 6 · Lesson 13 · ~15 min
Asymmetric Information, Adverse Selection & the Market for Lemons
Adverse selection vs. moral hazard, precisely distinguished, and Akerlof's Market for Lemons — how an information gap alone can unravel an entire market.
Level 7 · Lesson 14 · ~14 min
Signaling, Cheap Talk & Costly Signals
The differential-cost test that separates a credible signal from cheap talk — education, warranties, GitHub portfolios — plus the skeptic's follow-up on when a real signal can still be gamed.
Level 8 · Lesson 15 · ~18 min
Negotiation: BATNA, ZOPA & the ₹25 LPA Offer
Every earlier tool aimed at one practical skill — the ₹25 LPA salary offer from this workspace's mission, worked fully: BATNA, reservation value, ZOPA, anchoring, and what to reveal versus protect.
Level 9 · Lesson 16 · ~18 min
Business Competition & Auctions
Differentiation, network effects, and the platform cold-start problem as a real Stag Hunt — plus first-price, second-price/Vickrey, English and Dutch auctions, and the winner's curse. Includes named case studies: Apple, Netflix, Amazon Marketplace, and Uber/Lyft, each analyzed as a game.
Level 10 · Lesson 17 · ~18 min
Mechanism Design, Evolutionary & Behavioral Game Theory
Flipping the question to "how do we design the rules" (incentive compatibility, principal-agent problems), Hawk-Dove and evolutionarily stable strategies, and where loss aversion, bounded rationality, and fairness make the classical prediction wrong. The capstone.
Level 6 (deepening) · Lesson 18 · ~16 min
Bayesian Reasoning, Beliefs & Risk vs. Uncertainty
The actual math for what a credible signal (Lesson 14) should do to a belief, worked as real numbers — plus the base rate fallacy and Knight's risk-vs-uncertainty distinction.
