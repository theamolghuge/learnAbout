Lesson 7 · ~15 min, interactive

Simulations I: Interpersonal Heat

Six moments where someone else's words or absence land hard. Write your real answer before you read the analysis — that's the entire exercise.

For each scenario: read it as if it's happening to you right now, then actually write what you notice and what you'd do — not what you know you're "supposed" to say. Then reveal. The reveal never tells you that you were wrong; it separates emotionally understandable (the reaction makes complete sense given what you felt) from strategically wise (the reaction gets you what you actually want, a week later). Those overlap less often than it feels like in the moment.

1. Someone insults you during an argument

You're mid-disagreement about something that actually matters to you. They say something personal and cutting — not about the topic, about you.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do in the next 10 seconds?

Matching the insult is emotionally understandable — it's the fastest way to make the sting stop. It's rarely strategically wise: it hands them a new argument (about the insult) that replaces the original one, and it's the exact moment the topic that mattered gets lost. The 10-second protocol exists precisely for this gap: label it ("that landed, noted"), one physiological sigh, "I don't have to respond to that part yet" — then you can choose whether to name what they did ("that was personal — I want to get back to the actual issue") instead of reflexively returning fire.
2. Someone you love becomes distant

Over the last week or two, a partner or close friend has been shorter with you, slower to respond, less present. Nothing was said. You've noticed.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

This one is mostly a story-generation problem, not an action problem — ambiguity is uncomfortable enough that the mind fills it in fast, usually with the worst available explanation, and then reacts to the story as if it were confirmed. The urge is either to demand reassurance immediately or to withdraw first so you're not the one who got left. Lesson 4's checklist question "what information am I actually missing" does most of the work here: you don't yet know what's true. A strategically wise move names the observation without the story attached — "I've noticed some distance the last couple weeks, is everything okay?" — and then actually waits for the answer instead of pre-loading a reaction to the one you imagined.
3. Your boss criticizes you unfairly

In front of two colleagues, your manager attributes a delay to you that was actually caused by someone else's late input.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Correcting the record immediately, in front of the same audience, is emotionally understandable — the unfairness is public, so the urge to fix it publicly is proportionate to how it happened. It's often not strategically wise: contesting a manager in front of peers usually reads as defensive regardless of how right you are, and can cost more socially than the original misattribution did. The "what must be decided right now" question from Lesson 4 is doing the work: correcting the facts almost never has to happen in that exact meeting. A brief, calm note afterward ("wanted to flag — that delay was actually on [X]'s side, happy to walk through the timeline") gets the facts corrected without the public-defensiveness cost.
4. Someone sends you an angry message

A text or email arrives that's clearly written while the sender was upset — sharp, maybe unfair, sent fast.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Firing back at the same speed and heat is understandable — matching energy feels like the only way to not be steamrolled. It's rarely wise, mostly because you're now making two people's worst-written message the record of the conversation. The "I had to respond immediately" rationalization from Lesson 5 is worth running here specifically: what's the actual cost of an hour? Usually nothing. A message written after the 2-minute (or 20-minute, if it's serious) protocol reads completely differently than one written in the same minute you received it — and you can always send something fast and short ("got this, want to think before I respond properly") without sending the reactive version.
5. You feel ignored or disrespected

In a group setting — a meeting, a family dinner — you make a point and it's talked over or waved off, then someone else makes the same point minutes later and gets credit for it.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Going quiet for the rest of the meeting (withdrawal) or pointedly announcing "as I said earlier" (a jab dressed as a correction) are both understandable — the feeling of being erased is genuinely unpleasant. Neither reliably gets you what you actually want, which is usually to be heard next time, not to win this specific moment. A toward-move, from the reflex/opposite/delay structure in Lesson 3: name it plainly and immediately, without the edge — "yeah, that's what I was getting at a minute ago" — said flatly, once, then move on. It corrects the record without spending the rest of the meeting on it.
6. You receive a rejection

An application, pitch, or ask you cared about comes back as a clear no — with little or no explanation attached.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Two common reflexes: immediately asking for detailed reasons (an attempt to regain control by understanding), or immediately deciding the whole effort was a waste (catastrophizing to pre-empt further disappointment). Both are understandable ways to make "no" feel less unresolved. The "delay" option from Lesson 3 tends to be strategically wiser than either: let the no sit for a day before deciding what it means or what to do next — same-day interpretations of a rejection are usually the most distorted ones you'll ever have about it, precisely because they're made at peak intensity.
