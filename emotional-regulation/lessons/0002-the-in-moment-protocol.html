The In-Moment Protocol

One route through activation, in four lengths — from a single breath to walking away and coming back.

Lesson 1 established the failure mode: activation degrades the deliberate system's bandwidth, so the action step collapses straight into the feeling with nothing in between. The fix isn't willpower in the moment — willpower is exactly the resource that's degraded. The fix is a rehearsed sequence you can run on autopilot, so it doesn't need the bandwidth you don't have yet:

Trigger → activation → labeling → pause → regulation → perspective → options → consequences → deliberate action

You never do all nine steps consciously in real time — that's what makes it a rehearsed sequence rather than a decision tree. What follows is what each stage actually does, and exact self-talk for it, at four different time budgets.

What each stage is for

  • Activation — notice the body before the story. A racing heart, tight jaw, heat in the face. This is your earliest, most reliable warning — earlier than the thought "I'm fine."
  • Labeling — name the emotion in one or two words, silently or out loud. This isn't a formality: naming an emotion measurably reduces amygdala reactivity and re-engages the prefrontal regions you need for the rest of the sequence.†
  • Pause — the one non-negotiable step. Its only job is to create a gap between label and action. Nothing needs to be resolved here.
  • Regulation — bring the body back inside a workable range before asking it to think. You cannot reason your way out of a flooded nervous system; you have to physiologically downshift first.
  • Perspective — a single reframing question, not a full analysis.
  • Options — at least three candidate actions, not one. (Lesson 3 drills this specifically.)
  • Consequences — a fast check of each option against tomorrow, not just against the next ten seconds.
  • Deliberate action — chosen, not defaulted into.

The 10-second version

For mid-conversation, mid-reaction — you have no privacy and no time, but you have one breath.

Say to yourself "[Name the emotion], noted." — one physiological sigh (short inhale through the nose, second short top-up inhale, long slow exhale through the mouth — this specific pattern outperforms ordinary deep breathing at dropping arousal fast‡) — "I don't have to respond yet."

That's it. It buys you nothing but a beat — which is precisely the point. The 10-second version doesn't reach options or consequences; its only job is to stop the automatic handoff from label to action so a longer version becomes possible.

The 2-minute version

For a bad email, a tense hallway exchange, a moment you can step to the side for.

Say to yourself, in order Activation (10s): "Where is this in my body?" — actually locate it (chest, jaw, stomach).
Label (10s): "This is [emotion], at about [0–10]."
Pause (20s): Three physiological sighs. Nothing else — no problem-solving yet.
Perspective (30s): "What would I think if a friend described this exact situation to me?"
Options (40s): "What are three different things I could do right now?" — force three, even a bad one counts.
Choose (10s): "Given tomorrow-me, which of these?"
Practice the 2-minute version now
2:00

Pick any mildly annoying thing from today — a slow reply, a small slight — and actually run the six lines above out loud while this counts down. Rehearsed-but-never-used is not the same skill as rehearsed-and-run.

The 20-minute version

For something that actually matters — a fight worth having correctly, a decision with real stakes — where you have enough time to go all the way through consequences.

Say to yourself Run the 2-minute version first, in full. Then, with the body already downshifted: "What information am I actually missing here?" → "What am I tempted to do purely to make this discomfort stop right now?" → "If this intensity were half of what it is, what would I choose?" → "What will future-me, a week from now, wish I'd done?" → "Am I moving toward something I actually want, or just away from this feeling?" Write the answers down if you can — the physical act of writing keeps you in the slow system.

This is the version that produces the material for a regulation log entry once it's over, and it's the version later sessions will build the full decision-making framework on top of.

Leave-and-return

For when the body is past the point where any cognitive step will land — this is a TIPP-territory situation§, not a talk-yourself-down one. The skill here isn't regulation, it's exiting without escalating and actually coming back.

Say out loud, to the other person "I want to keep talking about this, and I'm too activated right now to do it well. Give me [20 minutes / an hour / until tonight] and I'll come back to it." — a concrete time, not "later." Then leave.
Say to yourself, once alone Cold water on your face or wrists (triggers the dive reflex, drops heart rate fast), or a hard, short burst of physical exertion if cold isn't available — then paced breathing until you'd rate your intensity at 4/10 or below. Only then: re-run the 2-minute version before you go back. Returning is part of the skill. A leave that never becomes a return is avoidance wearing regulation's clothes.
