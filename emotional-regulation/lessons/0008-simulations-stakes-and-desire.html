Simulations II: Stakes and Desire

Six moments where the pull is toward wanting something too much, or fearing a loss too much, to think straight about it.

Same format as Lesson 7: write your real answer first, then reveal. These six lean harder on urgency and desire than on interpersonal conflict — pay attention to whether your impulse is actually solving anything or just resolving the discomfort of wanting/fearing something in an unresolved state.

1. You want to quit immediately after a bad day

A genuinely rough day at work — a mistake, a hard conversation, feeling unappreciated. By evening you're drafting a resignation email in your head, maybe on your phone.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Wanting out immediately is completely understandable — quitting is a fast, total way to make a bad-day feeling stop. It's rarely wise precisely because it's a permanent action sized to a temporary state; Lesson 4's question "what must be decided right now" has an unusually clean answer here: nothing about your employment has to be decided tonight. The job might genuinely be wrong for you — that's a real possibility worth taking seriously — but that's a conclusion to test over a week of ordinary days, not one bad night, and it deserves the 20-minute or leave-and-return version, not a message sent at 11pm.
2. You discover something that makes you jealous

You see something — a message, a photo, an offhand comment — that triggers a sharp spike of jealousy about a partner or close friend.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Interrogating them immediately, or quietly digging for more "evidence" first, are both understandable — jealousy is an intensely information-hungry emotion. Neither is usually wise done at peak intensity: interrogation from a place of high activation tends to sound like accusation regardless of intent, and digging further usually escalates the story before you have any actual facts. Jealousy is a strong case of "contains information, shouldn't control behavior" from Lesson 4 — it may be flagging something real (a boundary, an insecurity, an actual issue) worth raising, but the raising goes much better after the 20-minute version than in the first five minutes.
3. Someone threatens to leave

In the middle of a hard conversation, someone says some version of "maybe we shouldn't do this anymore" — a relationship, a job, a partnership.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

The panic-driven urge is usually to resolve everything in that exact conversation — fix it, promise things, get an immediate reassurance that it's not really happening. Completely understandable: the threat of loss is one of the sharpest visceral triggers there is. It's rarely wise, because promises and concessions made from panic are exactly the ones that don't hold up later, which can make the actual relationship problem worse, not better. Naming the panic without acting on it works better here than almost anywhere else: "I don't want that, and I also don't think I can respond well to it right this second — can we keep talking, but slower?"
4. You're afraid you'll miss an opportunity

Something time-limited appears — a deal, an opening, a narrow window — and the framing (real or implied) is that hesitation means losing it.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Acting immediately to lock it in is understandable — scarcity (real or manufactured) produces urgency by design, that's not a flaw in you, it's how the feeling is supposed to work. It's often not wise, because genuinely good opportunities rarely evaporate over a 24-hour gap, and the ones that frame themselves as "act now or lose it forever" are disproportionately the ones engineered to exploit exactly this impulse. Lesson 4's checklist question is almost purpose-built for this: "what's actually lost by waiting 24 hours?" If the honest answer is "nothing," the urgency was never about the opportunity.
5. You desperately want an uncertain outcome

Something you want badly — a result, a decision, an answer from someone — is still unresolved, and the not-knowing itself has become hard to sit with.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Checking obsessively, reaching out to push for an answer, or trying to control variables that are actually outside your control are all understandable ways to convert unbearable uncertainty into the feeling of doing something. Almost none of it changes the outcome — it mostly just spends the waiting period badly. The tell is in Lesson 4's question 7: is this action moving you toward the outcome you want, or just away from the discomfort of not knowing yet? Checking a status page for the fifth time in an hour is almost always the second one.
6. You feel ashamed after making a mistake

You catch a real error you made — one with actual consequences for someone else — and the first wave is a hot, specific wish to disappear.

What's happening for you right now — body, emotion, the story your mind is telling you, and the impulse?

What would you actually do?

Hiding it, or the opposite — over-disclosing and self-flagellating before anyone's even asked — are both understandable ways to manage the exposure shame creates. Neither is usually wise: hiding often turns a small, fixable mistake into a bigger, trust-damaging one once it surfaces on its own; over-disclosing centers your discomfort instead of the fix, which puts the burden of reassuring you on the person your mistake actually affected. The wiser move, from Lesson 5's rationalization work and Lesson 6's repair structure: assess the actual scope first, then state the fact and the fix plainly, without narrating your own shame as part of the report.
