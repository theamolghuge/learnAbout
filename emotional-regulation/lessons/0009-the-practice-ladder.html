The Practice Ladder

Everything so far has been knowledge. This is how it turns into a trained reflex — in 5–15 minutes a day, in your actual life.

Why difficulty has to be progressive

Running the in-moment protocol for the first time during a genuine crisis is like trying out a new golf swing at match point — technically possible, unlikely to work. Skills built under load need to be built at low load first, then deliberately exposed to more, the same way physical training progressively overloads a muscle. Each rung below targets a slightly higher-stakes version of the same core move: notice → label → pause → choose. Don't skip rungs because a higher one feels more urgent to fix — the lower ones are what make the higher ones possible under real pressure.

The nine rungs

1. Low-stakes irritation

Next time something trivially annoying happens — slow traffic, a slow page load, someone walking slowly in front of you — silently label it: "this is irritation, noted." No action required, no protocol needed yet. Just practice the labeling reflex where nothing is at stake. Aim for 3 times this week.

2. Mild anxiety

Before a small task you're mildly dreading — a phone call, sending an unsure message, a minor confrontation — run the 10-second version before doing it anyway. The goal isn't to make the anxiety go away first; it's practicing acting with the feeling still present.

3. Interpersonal friction

In your next minor disagreement (what to eat, a scheduling conflict), run the 2-minute version before you respond, including the "three options" step from Lesson 3. Notice whether your first response would have been one of the three, or a fourth thing you didn't generate at all.

4. Criticism

Deliberately ask someone for a small piece of honest feedback you can predict will sting a little. When it lands, label the sting in real time and try to name, right then, whether your first-instinct response would have been "emotionally understandable" or "strategically wise."

5. Uncertainty

Pick one small decision this week you'd normally make instantly (what to order, a minor purchase) and deliberately leave it open for 24 hours. Notice the urge to resolve it early just to stop the discomfort of not-yet-decided — that urge is the actual target of this exercise, not the decision itself.

6. Rejection

Ask for something with a real chance of "no" — a discount, a favor, a schedule swap. If it's a no, apply the delay rule from Lesson 7: don't interpret what it means about you until at least a few hours have passed.

7. Anger

The next time you feel genuine anger (don't manufacture it — wait for a real one), run the full 2-minute version before responding to whatever triggered it. Log the entry afterward regardless of how it went — a log entry after a well-handled one is just as useful as after a badly-handled one.

8. Fear of loss

Name one thing you're currently avoiding specifically because of fear of loss — a conversation, an ask, a decision. Run the Lesson 4 checklist on it this week, in writing, and take the smallest real step the checklist suggests.

9. High-stakes decisions

When an actual high-stakes decision is live in your life, run the full 20-minute version on it, in writing, and open a regulation log entry regardless of how activated you were going in. This rung isn't manufactured — it's whatever's real for you right now.

How to actually run this Don't try to hit all nine in one week. Spend a few days on whichever rung is currently easiest for you to find real examples of, then move up only once the lower rung feels close to automatic — noticing and labeling without deciding to. If a rung repeatedly doesn't happen naturally in your week, that's worth mentioning directly rather than forcing it.
Ask your coach Report back on which rungs actually happened this week and how they went — that's exactly the kind of real material the personal dysregulation map and future learning records are built from.
