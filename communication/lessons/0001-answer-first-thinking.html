Answer-First Thinking

Say your point before you explain it. This one habit fixes more rambling than anything else you'll learn in this course.

You already know the content of what you want to say. What's missing, almost every time you ramble, is not knowledge — it's order. This lesson gives you one structural habit — borrowed from how McKinsey trains new consultants to write and speak — that you can apply to an email, a meeting answer, or a toast, starting today.

The idea

Most people explain things in the order they figured them out: context, then more context, then a complication, then finally — three sentences later — the actual point. Barbara Minto, who built McKinsey's writing training in the 1970s, called this backwards. Her fix, the Pyramid Principle, is simple to state and hard to do under pressure:

Lead with the answer. Then give the two or three reasons that support it. Only then, if asked, go into evidence and detail.
Bottom-up (the default): "So we started the project in March, and the vendor was a bit slow at first, and then in June we hit a scoping issue, and honestly the team was also a bit understaffed, but by August things picked up, so… I'd say overall we're in okay shape, maybe two weeks behind."
Answer-first: "We're about two weeks behind schedule. Three reasons: a slow vendor start, a scoping issue in June, and being understaffed through the summer. We've recovered pace since August."

Same information. The second version tells a busy listener the one thing they need in the first five seconds, and lets them stop you if they only needed that.

Why it works It matches how the listener's brain wants to process you: get the conclusion, then evaluate whether the supporting logic holds up — not the reverse. It also forces you to know your own point before you open your mouth, which is where most real clarity comes from.

The one rule that makes it hard: MECE

Your supporting reasons should be Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive — no overlap, no gaps. This is the part that takes actual thought, and it's exactly the compression Feynman used when forced to explain physics to a room of non-physicists: if you can't cleanly separate your reasons, you haven't actually finished thinking yet. Drill (do this now — ~12 minutes)

Record, extract, rebuild

  1. Pick a real question you get often — "How's the project going?", "Why should we do X?", "How was your trip?" — anything with a real answer, not small talk filler.
  2. Record yourself (voice memo is fine) answering it exactly as you normally would. Don't plan it. Time it.
  3. Listen back once. Find the sentence that is actually your answer — the "so what." Write it down as one sentence.
  4. Find your 2–3 supporting reasons from what you said. Check them against MECE: do any two overlap? Is anything important missing?
  5. Re-record using the template: "[Answer]. There are [N] reasons: first …, second …, third …." Time it again.

Check your work: the second recording should be shorter, and a stranger who only heard the first sentence should already know your bottom line. If it isn't shorter, your "answer" sentence is probably still a lead-in, not the actual point — go back to step 3.

Retrieval check

What does MECE stand for?

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.

In the Pyramid Principle, what goes first?

The answer, before any reasons or evidence.
