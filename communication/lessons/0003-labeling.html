Labeling

Name the emotion in the room before you respond to the content. One sentence, and a technique FBI hostage negotiators built a career on.

Lessons 1 and 2 were about clarity — getting your own point across cleanly. This lesson flips direction: it's about what you do with someone else's point, especially when there's emotion under it. The technique is called labeling, and it comes from Chris Voss, formerly the FBI's lead international hostage negotiator.

The idea

When someone is upset, frustrated, or anxious, the instinct is to fix the problem immediately. Voss's insight: people can't hear your solution until they feel heard. Labeling is how you signal "heard" in one sentence, before you say anything else.

Solution-first (the default): Coworker: "This deadline got moved up with basically no warning and now I have to redo half my plan." You: "Okay, well, we can probably just push the review meeting a few days."
Label-first: Coworker: "This deadline got moved up with basically no warning and now I have to redo half my plan." You: "It sounds like this landed on you with zero notice, and that's thrown off everything you'd already planned." [pause] "…Yeah. We can probably push the review a few days if that helps."

Same eventual solution. But the second version gets there after the person feels understood, not instead of it — and often they'll tell you something in the pause that changes what you'd have offered.

The two rules

  • Statement, not question. "It sounds like you're frustrated" — not "Are you frustrated?" A question makes them do the work of confirming; a statement just needs a nod.
  • Then stop talking. The label opens a silence. Don't fill it. What they say next is usually the real information.

Drill (do this now — ~10 minutes, then apply live)

One label, one real conversation

  1. Right now: write down one label starter you'll actually use today — "It sounds like …", "It seems like …", or "It looks like …". Say it out loud twice so it's not the first time it leaves your mouth in the real moment.
  2. Today, in a real conversation where someone seems even mildly frustrated, worried, or excited, use one label before you respond to the content of what they said.
  3. Pause for two full seconds after the label, even if it feels uncomfortable. Don't add anything else until they respond.
  4. Right after: jot one line: what did you label, and what did they say back that you wouldn't have heard if you'd jumped straight to responding?

Check your work: if the label was accurate, you'll usually get a small confirming reaction ("yeah, exactly") before they say more. If you get pushback ("no, it's not that, it's…"), that's also a win — you just got a more accurate read, for free.

Retrieval check

Why should a label be a statement rather than a question?

A question makes them confirm; a statement does that work for them.

What should you do immediately after delivering a label?

Stop talking and let the silence sit for a beat.
