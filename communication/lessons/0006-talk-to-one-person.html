Talk to One Person

The camera is not an audience. It's one person's eyes. Fix your eye line and your mental model, and most of what feels "stiff" on camera disappears.

Most on-camera stiffness comes from two small, fixable habits: looking at the wrong point in the room, and imagining the wrong-sized audience. Fix both and the same voice, structure, and pauses you've been practicing in Lessons 1–4 carry straight onto video.

Fix 1: Look at the lens, not the screen

Your instinct is to watch the screen — your own face, or the other person's. But the lens is the only point that transmits eye contact to whoever's watching. Every glance down at the preview reads, to the viewer, as you looking away from them.

Fix 2: Picture one person, not "an audience"

Talking to "everyone": energy gets vague, generic, performative — the tone people use for a room they can't quite see.
Talking to one specific person you know: tone gets warmer and more direct automatically, because you already know how to talk to that one person.

This is the single highest-leverage mental trick for on-camera work: pick one real person before you hit record, and address the whole thing to them.

Drill (do this now — ~12 minutes, camera required)

30–60 second direct-to-camera clip

  1. Pick one real person you know — a friend, a colleague — who you'll imagine is watching.
  2. Pick a question to answer: "What did you actually work on today?" or reuse your Lesson 1 answer-first line.
  3. Set up your phone or laptop camera, and if you can, cover or shrink your own self-preview so you're not tempted to watch it.
  4. Record 30–60 seconds, looking at the lens, talking to that one person, using the answer-first structure and a deliberate pause after your opening line.
  5. Watch it back twice: once muted (checking only: did your eyes stay on the lens, does your energy look like a conversation or a performance?), then once with sound (checking structure and pacing).

Check your work: if your eyes visibly drift down/sideways on playback, that's the self-preview pulling you — cover it entirely next take. If the energy still looks "presenting" rather than "talking," re-record picturing that one person more specifically — what they'd be wearing, where they'd be sitting.

Retrieval check

What should you look at on camera, and why?

The lens — it's the only point that transmits eye contact.

What should you picture instead of "an audience"?

One specific real person you already know.
