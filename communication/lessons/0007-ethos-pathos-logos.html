Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Why a "technically correct" argument sometimes doesn't move anyone — and the two sentences that usually fix it.

Aristotle, writing in Rhetoric around 350 BC, identified three separate levers of persuasion: logos (the logic), ethos (the speaker's credibility), and pathos (the audience's emotional stake). Everything you've built so far — the Pyramid Principle, BLUF, the throughline — is pure logos. It's the lever most naturally clear thinkers over-rely on, because it's the one that feels the most "correct." It's also, on its own, often the weakest at actually persuading anyone.

The diagnostic

Next time an argument that's "technically right" isn't landing Ask: is this missing ethos, pathos, or both? Not "is my logic wrong" — the logic is probably fine. The question is whether the listener has a reason to trust this claim, and a reason to care about it.

Adding the missing levers, without rebuilding the argument

You keep your logos skeleton from Lesson 1. You add one sentence for each missing lever:

Logos only: "We're two weeks behind. Three reasons: slow vendor start, a June scoping issue, summer understaffing."
+ ethos, + pathos: "We're two weeks behind. [ethos] I flagged this exact vendor-onboarding risk on the last two launches, so this isn't a surprise pattern. [logos] Three reasons: slow vendor start, a June scoping issue, summer understaffing. [pathos] If we don't adjust the launch date now, marketing ends up promoting a date we'll miss publicly — which is a worse outcome than a quiet two-week slip today."

Same skeleton, same MECE reasons — but now there's a reason to trust the speaker and a reason for the listener to actually care, not just nod along.

Drill (do this now — ~12 minutes)

Add the missing levers to something you already wrote

  1. Pull up your Lesson 1 recording or your Lesson 2 BLUF email rewrite.
  2. Diagnose it: does it have ethos (a reason to trust you/your source on this)? Does it have pathos (a concrete stake for this specific listener)? Almost always, at least one is missing.
  3. Write one ethos sentence — a credential, a track record, or a trusted source — that's actually true, not invented.
  4. Write one pathos sentence — the real, specific consequence for the actual listener if this goes right or wrong.
  5. Insert them around your existing logos skeleton and read the whole thing out loud.

Check your work: read the original and the revised version back to back. If the revised version makes you want to actually act, and the original just made you nod, the levers worked. If it now feels manipulative rather than persuasive, you likely overstated the ethos or pathos claim — both sentences should be true, not just effective.

Retrieval check

Which of the three levers does the Pyramid Principle / BLUF structure supply on its own?

Logos — the logic and structure of the argument.

What's the risk of overstating an ethos or pathos sentence?

It stops persuading and starts feeling manipulative instead.
