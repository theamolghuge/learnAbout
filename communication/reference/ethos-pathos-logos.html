Ethos, Pathos, Logos

The three levers of persuasion, from Aristotle's Rhetoric. From Lesson 7.

The three

  • Logos — the logic. The argument itself: reasons, evidence, structure. This is what the Pyramid Principle gives you.
  • Ethos — the credibility. Why should this listener trust you (or your source) on this specific claim?
  • Pathos — the emotional stake. Why does this matter to them, specifically, right now?

The diagnostic

When an argument that's "technically right" isn't landing Ask which lever is missing. A pure-logos argument (all reasons, no credibility, no stakes) is the most common failure — it's "correct" and still doesn't move anyone.

Adding the missing levers without losing your structure

You don't rebuild the argument — you add one sentence each:

  • Ethos sentence: "I've seen this pattern on the last two launches" / cite a source the listener already trusts.
  • Pathos sentence: name the concrete consequence for this listener — what changes for them if the answer is right or wrong.

Historical grounding

Aristotle ranked logos as primary in an ideal world of perfectly rational judges — but noted that in the real world, ethos and pathos usually decide it. Both MLK's "I Have a Dream" (pathos-heavy, built on anaphora and moral stakes) and a McKinsey-style pyramid memo (logos-heavy) are legitimate — they're calibrated to different audiences and moments.
