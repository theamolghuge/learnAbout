Lessons 22 and 23 gave real research treatment to four of the ten distinctions your original brief named: discipline vs. compulsion, work ethic vs. workaholism, confidence vs. ego, and high standards vs. perfectionism. Lesson 20 touched competition vs. comparison. That leaves ambition vs. insecurity, sacrifice vs. self-destruction, persistence vs. stubbornness, pain tolerance vs. ignoring legitimate limits (Lesson 17's case study), and obsession with mastery vs. obsession with validation (this whole curriculum's opening theme, Lesson 1) — completing the set without pretending every row has the same evidentiary weight.

The full table, with a specific test for each row, is now a standalone reference: The Dark Side Checklist. Three of its rows are marked "Reasoned" rather than "Researched" — this workspace's own logical distinctions, not findings from a cited study. That's a deliberate choice, not an oversight: better to be honest about which claims are backed by evidence and which are this curriculum's own best reasoning than to dress up reasoning as research.

The pattern across all ten Look at the "test" column across all ten rows. Almost every single one comes down to the same underlying question, asked in a different costume: what happens when things don't go your way — when you fall short, get challenged, face a setback, or have to change course? The healthy version holds steady. The dark-side version reacts — defensively, rigidly, compulsively, or by seeking an audience to repair the damage. If you only remember one thing from this stage, it's that the tell isn't in how someone behaves when things are going well. It's what happens the moment they aren't.
A genuinely hard question to close this stage Go through the checklist honestly, row by row, applied to yourself right now — not to Kobe, not to Goggins, not to anyone else this curriculum has used as a case study. Where do you currently land on the dark side of at least one of these ten? Not "where could you theoretically slip" — where are you actually, right now, closer to the corrosive twin than the healthy trait. Naming zero is itself worth being suspicious of.
