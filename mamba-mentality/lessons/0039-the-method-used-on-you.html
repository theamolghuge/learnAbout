The Method Used On You

Every "sit with this" box in this curriculum has been borrowing from one person, unnamed until now.

Extends Stage 2, closing a real gap: Socrates never got covered, despite being named explicitly in your original brief — and despite this curriculum quietly using his method in every single lesson.

Socrates wrote nothing himself. Everything known of him comes through his student Plato's dialogues, in which Socrates rarely states a positive doctrine — he mostly does something else: asks someone to define a concept they're confident they understand (justice, courage, piety), then, through further questions, shows that their own further beliefs contradict the definition they just gave. This is the elenchus — not a lecture, a structured unraveling. It doesn't end with Socrates supplying the correct answer. It ends in aporia — productive puzzlement, the honest admission that the confident answer didn't hold up.

In the Apology, his defense speech at his own trial — the one that ended in his execution — Socrates gave his reason for spending his life doing this to people, at genuine cost to himself: "the unexamined life is not worth living." Not a slogan. A statement he was, by that point, dying for. He traced the underlying principle to an inscription at the Temple of Delphi: know thyself.

What this curriculum has actually been doing to you

Every "sit with this" box across 38 lessons has followed the same structure: not a lecture delivering an answer, but a question designed to expose a gap between what you'd confidently say you believe and what your actual answer, honestly given, reveals. Lesson 22 asked what would happen if you deliberately skipped your practice. Lesson 30 asked which case study's method is least like your own. This lesson's own confront question, below, is built the same way on purpose — it's worth noticing that structure now that it has a name, rather than after 38 more lessons of it happening without comment.

Two different modes this curriculum has run in Worth distinguishing precisely: most of this curriculum's actual content has been Aristotelian in method — positive claims, defined terms, cited research, systematic argument (Plato's student was Aristotle; the two approaches genuinely differ). But its confrontation callouts have been Socratic — not building a positive claim, but destabilizing an assumed one. Both are real philosophical methods, doing different work. Confusing them — mistaking a Socratic question for something with a "correct" answer to look up — is a category error worth avoiding going forward.
The elenchus, applied to you directly Define, in one sentence, what "excellence" actually means to you — not Lesson 1's definition, your own, right now. Now: does your actual behavior over the past week match that stated definition, or does your behavior reveal a different, unstated definition you're actually operating by? Don't resolve the gap if you find one. Sit in it — that's aporia, and per Socrates, it's the honest starting point, not a failure to reach.
Write it down.

Your one-sentence definition of excellence. Then, honestly, the gap (if any) between that definition and your actual behavior this week. If there's no gap, say what specifically confirms that — not just the absence of a feeling of contradiction.
