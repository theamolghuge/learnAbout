Specific, Difficult, and Believed

One of psychology's most replicated findings, in three words — and the belief that determines whether it works at all.

Extends Stage 4, closing a real gap: goal-setting theory and self-efficacy were both explicitly named in your original brief and never covered.

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's goal-setting research spans roughly 400 studies over four decades, and its core finding is unusually clean for psychology: people pursuing specific, difficult goals outperform people pursuing vague or easy ones ("do your best" reliably underperforms a specific hard number). Not a subtle effect — one of the most consistently replicated findings in industrial-organizational psychology.

The variable that makes or breaks it

The theory's own account of why some people commit to hard goals and others don't runs through self-efficacy — Albert Bandura's term for the specific belief that you're capable of the task in front of you (not general self-esteem, a narrower, task-specific confidence). Locke and Latham found that people with higher self-efficacy set higher goals for themselves, commit to them more strongly, respond better to negative feedback instead of being derailed by it, and are more likely to develop effective strategies when a straightforward approach isn't working. Goal difficulty and self-efficacy aren't independent — a genuinely difficult goal without the belief you can reach it tends to produce disengagement rather than effort.

Where this connects to what's already built "Specific" is the same requirement Lesson 15's practice-structure builder already asked for — a named target, not "get better." "Difficult" is Lesson 13's deliberate-practice push past current ability. What this lesson adds is the missing variable: self-efficacy isn't the same as Lesson 23's confidence-vs-ego distinction (that was about stability under challenge) — it's narrower still, a belief about this specific task, and it's buildable through exactly what Lesson 15 asked you to build: a structure with real feedback, so small real wins accumulate into a track record you can actually believe in, rather than a belief resting on nothing.
Sit with this before moving on Look at your current goal for your main pursuit. Is it actually specific and difficult, or is it a vague version dressed up as ambitious ("get really good at this")? Separately: rate your honest self-efficacy for it right now — not your general confidence, your belief that you specifically can hit this specific target. If that belief is low, per the research, the fix isn't a pep talk — it's smaller, real wins that build actual evidence.
Write it down.

Rewrite your current goal so it's genuinely specific and difficult, not vague. Then name one small, real, achievable-soon task that would give you actual evidence toward believing you can reach it — not motivation, evidence.
