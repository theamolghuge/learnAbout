Why Pursue Excellence At All?

Before discipline, before mastery, before any of it — what is this actually for?

This is the first lesson in a ten-stage curriculum (see the full roadmap). Everything after this assumes you've sat with this one question honestly. Skipping it doesn't skip the question — it just means the question surfaces later, at a worse time, usually after a failure.

Ask most people why they want to be exceptional at something, and you'll get an answer that sounds like a reason but is actually a feeling: "I want to be great." "I want to prove people wrong." "I don't want to be mediocre." None of those are wrong to feel. But none of them tell you what to do on a Tuesday when no one's watching and nothing's on the line.

There are two different engines that can drive the exact same behavior — showing up, practicing, competing — and they produce very different people over twenty years.

Two engines, same behavior

Validation-orientation: you do the work because of what it will get you — praise, proof, rank, relief from the fear of being ordinary. The work is a means. Success feels good because it silences a question about your worth; failure feels catastrophic because it reopens that question.

Mastery-orientation: you do the work because closing the gap between what you can do today and what you could do tomorrow is itself the thing you want. The work is the point. Success is data. Failure is data. Neither one is a verdict on you.

These aren't a purity test — almost no one is 100% one or the other, and wanting to win is not, by itself, validation-seeking. The distinction that matters is what happens to your effort when the applause is guaranteed to never come. If the answer is "I'd stop," that's worth knowing about yourself now, not after a decade.

In his own words Kobe Bryant, on why he played: "I play to figure things out, I play to learn something... if you play with the fear of failure, or if you play with the will to win, if that will supersedes your fear of failure, it's a weakness either way." Notice what he's doing: he's rejecting both fear of failure and a "will to win" that's really just fear of failure wearing a competitive jersey. The tell of validation-orientation isn't wanting to lose less — it's that winning is functioning as relief rather than as information.

An answer that's 2,500 years old

This isn't a new question, and one of the oldest, most direct answers to it comes from the Bhagavad Gita. In Chapter 2, Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna: "You have a right to your actions, but never to the fruits of your actions." This is the doctrine of nishkama karma — action without attachment to outcome.

It's easy to misread this as "don't try hard" or "don't care what happens." That's backwards. Nishkama karma asks for full effort — the Gita has no patience for laziness — while refusing to let your peace of mind, or your sense of self-worth, depend on how the outcome lands. You act because the action is yours to do well; the result was never fully yours to control in the first place. Easwaran, translating this for a modern reader, is careful to distinguish it from passive "good works" done for reputation — that's still ego-driven, just wearing a nicer outfit. The test is the same one above: would you still do the work if no one ever found out?

Modern evidence, same claim Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory — one of the most replicated frameworks in motivational psychology — identifies competence (the felt sense of mastery and growth) as one of three basic psychological needs, alongside autonomy and relatedness, that predict sustained motivation, performance, and wellbeing when satisfied intrinsically rather than chased through external reward. This is the evidence-based version of the same claim the Gita makes philosophically: wanting to get better at something, for its own sake, is a real and durable engine. Wanting proof that you're good enough is a leakier one — it works, but it runs out, and it costs more the longer you run on it.

Where the ancient and modern accounts agree: mastery-orientation is more sustainable than validation-orientation, because it doesn't require an audience, a win, or anyone's approval to keep running. Where you should stay honest with yourself: neither the Gita nor SDT is saying validation-orientation doesn't work at all — it demonstrably produces effort and results too. The claim is narrower and more useful than that: it's a worse foundation for decades, because it's contingent on something you don't fully control.

Sit with this before moving on What would you actually pursue if you knew, with total certainty, that no one would ever find out, applaud, or be impressed? Not what you'd say you'd pursue — what you'd actually still get up and do. If nothing comes to mind, that's the honest starting point for this whole curriculum, not a failure to answer correctly.
Write it down. No one grades this, and nothing here leaves your machine.

Name one thing you currently do mostly to be seen doing it well, and one thing you do mostly because you'd do it even in private. What's different about how each one feels while you're doing it — not after, while?
