One Good, or Many?

Modern "Stoicism" markets itself as compatible with everything. The actual doctrine picks a fight.

Builds on Lesson 2 (dichotomy of control, sourced from Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius) and Lesson 3 (Aristotle's eudaimonia). Pulls the same two Greek thinkers back in and shows they don't actually agree on what a good life requires.

Popular "Stoicism" today gets sold as a flexible toolkit — focus on what you control, don't sweat externals, stay calm under pressure — phrased loosely enough to sit comfortably next to almost any other philosophy, including Aristotle's. It isn't. The actual technical doctrine, held by the school from Zeno through Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius, is sharper and stranger than the toolkit version, and it directly contradicts Aristotle on a specific, load-bearing point.

The actual claim: virtue alone is good

Classical Stoic ethics holds that virtue alone is good, and vice alone is bad. Full stop. Everything else you'd normally call good or bad — health, wealth, reputation, friendship, even your own life — is classed as adiaphora, "indifferent." Not indifferent in the sense of not mattering practically. Indifferent in the stricter sense that none of it determines whether you're living well, because that's settled entirely by whether your character and reasoning are virtuous.

The Stoics weren't nihilists about practical life, though — they split indifferents into preferred (health, resources, a good reputation — things "in accordance with nature") and dispreferred (illness, poverty — "contrary to nature"), and they built a precise distinction to keep this from collapsing into "nothing matters": you rationally select the preferred indifferent — you should still choose health over illness, given the option — but you choose virtue. Only the second word describes something good in the strict sense.

Aristotle's direct disagreement

Aristotle read this move and rejected it. In Nicomachean Ethics Book I, chapters 8–9, he argues that eudaimonia genuinely requires some external goods — friends, resources, decent birth, the absence of catastrophic misfortune. He calls this "equipment" (chorēgia): it's difficult or impossible to do noble acts without the material means to act at all, and someone who is friendless, solitary, or hit by severe enough misfortune cannot honestly be called flourishing, no matter how virtuous their character.

This isn't a rounding error between two similar old philosophies. It's a real fork: the Stoics say virtue is the only good, full stop, and everything else is strictly indifferent to whether your life is good. Aristotle says virtue is necessary but not sufficient — you also need enough of the external stuff to actually act on it. Same starting respect for virtue, genuinely different conclusion about whether losing everything else can touch your good life.

Why this isn't just academic Lesson 2 gave you the dichotomy of control — focus on what's up to you, let go of what isn't — without fully flagging where it comes from. It's the practical technique built on top of the stricter Stoic claim above. If you take the full claim seriously, losing the outcome you were chasing literally cannot diminish your good life, because the outcome was never part of "the good" to begin with — only your effort and character were. That's a genuinely more radical, more resilient position than "focus on what you control" sounds like on its own. It's also a real commitment, not a free technique: it means treating your health, your reputation, and the thing you're building toward as, strictly speaking, indifferent.
Sit with this before moving on If you permanently lost the outcome you're currently working toward — through no fault of your own, tomorrow — would you still call your life good, as long as your effort and character stayed intact? Answer the question you'd actually live out under real loss, not the answer that sounds wise to type.
Write it down.

Pick one thing you're currently chasing. Are you, honestly, closer to the Stoic view (only my effort and character are actually "good" — the outcome is indifferent) or Aristotle's (I genuinely need this to go a certain way for my life to be good)? What would have to change for you to actually live the answer you'd prefer to be true?
