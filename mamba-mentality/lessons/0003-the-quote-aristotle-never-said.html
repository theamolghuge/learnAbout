The Quote Aristotle Never Said

A live example of the exact thing you asked this curriculum to protect you from.

Opens Stage 2 — what a good life actually looks like, and where traditions agree and honestly disagree about it. Builds on Stage 1's mastery-vs-validation and dichotomy-of-control material.

You've almost certainly seen this line, attributed to Aristotle, usually next to a stock photo of a sunrise or a gym:

"We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit."

Aristotle never wrote that sentence. It's from Will Durant's 1926 book The Story of Philosophy — Durant's own paraphrase, in his own words, summarizing his reading of Aristotle for a popular audience. It got quoted so often, detached from Durant's name, that it now circulates as if Aristotle said it directly.

This isn't a trivia aside. It's exactly the pattern you asked this curriculum to guard against: a real thinker's name attached to a tidy, inspiring line that sounds like their idea but isn't actually what they argued. The fix isn't to distrust the sentiment — it's close to Aristotle's actual view. The fix is to go read what he actually argued, which turns out to be more precise and more useful than the bumper-sticker version.

What Aristotle actually argued

In Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, chapter 1, Aristotle's real claim is this: arete (excellence, virtue, the quality of being genuinely good at being human) is not a feeling you have, not an innate gift, and not a single decision you make once. It's a hexis — a stable, trained disposition of character. And the way you build a hexis is by repeatedly performing the corresponding actions: you become brave by doing brave things, disciplined by doing disciplined things, not by feeling brave or deciding to be brave in the abstract.

Notice what this adds to Durant's paraphrase, not contradicts: it's not just "do it a lot and it becomes a habit" — it's that the specific actions you repeat are what you're being trained into. Repetition isn't neutral. It's directional. (This is also where this stage will eventually connect forward to Stage 5's deliberate practice — Aristotle and Ericsson are making structurally similar claims about how repeated, specific action builds capability, 2,300 years apart.)

What a "good life" even means here

Aristotle's word for the target of all this is eudaimonia — usually translated "happiness," which is a bad translation. It doesn't mean a mood or a feeling of pleasure. It's closer to "flourishing" or "living well" — a description of an entire life, not a moment in it. In Book I.7 he makes the point directly: "one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy" — you have to add "in a complete life."

Put the two ideas together and you get Stage 2's actual claim: excellence is not a trophy you win and then rest on. The good life, for Aristotle, just is the ongoing activity of excellence, sustained across a whole life — not a state you achieve once and then bank.

Where this agrees with the Gita — and where it honestly doesn't

Lesson 1 introduced nishkama karma — action performed fully, without attachment to its results. Put next to Aristotle, there's a real, non-forced agreement: both traditions reject the idea that excellence is a single achievement you can point to and be done with. Both build it through sustained, repeated action, not a one-time decision.

But look at what each is actually for, and they diverge in a way worth sitting with rather than smoothing over. Aristotle's arete exists in service of your own eudaimonia — your flourishing, your good life (even though he thinks that flourishing is realized partly through friendship and community, it's still centered on you). The Gita's dharma is not centered on your flourishing at all — nishkama karma explicitly asks you to release attachment to what the action does for you, oriented instead toward duty and ultimately liberation (moksha). One tradition puts your own good life at the center of the whole project. The other asks you to stop centering yourself in it. That's a genuine disagreement, not a translation gap — and you don't have to resolve it today, but you should notice it rather than blend the two into a mush that agrees with everything.

Sit with this before moving on Picture yourself having actually become excellent at whatever you're putting years into. What is that excellence actually for — because it will make your life fuller and better (Aristotle's frame), or because doing the work well is simply the right thing to do, regardless of what it gets you (the Gita's frame)? Don't reach for the answer that sounds more evolved. Notice which one is actually true of you right now.
Write it down.

Which frame is closer to true for you today — pursuing excellence because it makes your own life better, or because the work itself is simply owed, regardless of the payoff to you? Is that the answer you'd want a friend to have, or just the honest one?
