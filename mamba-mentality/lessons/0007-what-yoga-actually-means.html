What Yoga Actually Means

Not postures. And the two things it says you need at the exact same time.

Opens Stage 3 — the mechanics of controlling mind, emotion, and impulse. Turns Stage 2's convergence ("don't cling, but keep practicing") into an actual technical instruction, from the same tradition that gave Lesson 1 nishkama karma.

In most modern usage, "yoga" means a class at a studio — postures, stretching, maybe some breathing. That's not what the word meant to Patanjali, who compiled the Yoga Sutras around the early centuries CE. His own definition, stated in the second line of the text (Sutra 1.2): "yogas citta-vritti-nirodhah" — yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind. Posture (asana) shows up later as just one of eight limbs of the practice, and its stated purpose is mainly to let you sit still long enough to actually do the mental work — not an end in itself. The gym-studio meaning isn't wrong, exactly; it's a small, physical-fitness-shaped slice of a system that was originally, centrally, about training attention and impulse.

The actual instruction: two things, at once

Sutra 1.12 gives the method directly: "abhyasa vairagyabhyam tannirodhah" — the fluctuations of the mind are stilled through abhyasa and vairagya, together. Not either one. Both, simultaneously.

Abhyasa (Sutras 1.13–14) is sustained practice — effort toward steadiness, applied for a long time, without interruption, and with genuine care rather than going through the motions. It's Patanjali's version of exactly what Stage 5 will later call deliberate practice: showing up, consistently, for real.

Vairagya (Sutras 1.15–16) is non-attachment — releasing the pull of craving toward things seen or heard about, and, at its higher level, releasing attachment even to the subtlest states you might reach through practice. This is the same move Lessons 1, 2, 4, and 5 already gave you in four other vocabularies: nishkama karma, the dichotomy of control, Stoic indifferents, anatta. Patanjali just names it as one specific half of a two-part mechanism.

Why it has to be both

The grammar of Sutra 1.12 matters here: both words are in a single compound, achieved together, not offered as alternatives. That's the actual teaching, and it rules out two failure modes you've probably lived through already:

Abhyasa without vairagya is grinding: showing up relentlessly while white-knuckling the outcome, anxious the whole way through. It looks like discipline. It runs on tension instead of steadiness, and it's a preview of a distinction Stage 8 will name directly — discipline vs. compulsion.

Vairagya without abhyasa is avoidance wearing the costume of wisdom: "I've let go of attachment to the outcome" used as a reason to stop actually doing the work. Real non-attachment, in this text, is paired with — not a substitute for — sustained effort.

The practical translation Self-mastery isn't one skill. It's two, held at once: show up consistently and fully (abhyasa), while loosening your grip on how it has to turn out (vairagya). Most people are lopsided toward one side without noticing — either gripping too hard to ever relax, or too "chill" to ever actually train.
