You Could Leave Life Right Now

A Roman emperor's private reminder to himself. It's not about dying — it's the sharpest filter this curriculum has for what actually matters.

A short, direct lesson. Meditations has been Tier 1 since session 1 — this is the specific line Marcus Aurelius returned to as an ongoing discipline, not a one-time thought.

Book 2, Section 11 of Meditations: "You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think." Marcus Aurelius wasn't writing this once and moving on — memento mori, "remember you will die," recurs throughout his private notebook as a practiced discipline, not a passing mood. He didn't treat death as something to fear or dwell on morbidly. He treated the fact of it as a filter: if this could genuinely be your last day, does the thing occupying your attention right now actually deserve it?

What this practice is not This isn't an invitation to constant anxious rumination about dying, and used that way it would work against everything this curriculum has built. The Stoic practice is deliberate and brief — a specific check applied to a specific decision, not a permanent background hum of dread. If mortality awareness starts producing ongoing anxiety rather than occasional clarity, that's a sign to back off the practice, not a sign you're doing Stoicism correctly.

What this actually clarifies

This is the thing that gives real stakes to nearly everything already covered. Lesson 1 asked whether you'd still pursue your craft if no one would ever find out — mortality sharpens that question further: would you still spend today on it if today might genuinely be one of the last ones you get? Lesson 15's "a thousand days to forge" only means something if the days are actually finite and actually running. Lesson 25's second-act question only has urgency if you're honest that the runway for any given chapter, including this one, isn't infinite.

Sit with this before moving on If you knew, with real certainty, that you had one year left — not as a dramatic exercise, genuinely — would the thing you're currently pouring effort into still be what you'd spend that year on? If the honest answer is no, that's worth taking seriously as data about why you're actually pursuing it, not just an interesting hypothetical to set aside.
Write it down.

Answer the question above honestly. If the answer is yes — good, that's real evidence for Lesson 1's mastery-orientation, not validation. If the answer is no, name specifically what you'd do instead, and what that reveals about what you're actually pursuing right now and why.
