What They Actually Agree On

Four traditions, laid side by side, without pretending they're the same thing wearing different hats.

Closes Stage 2. No new source material — this lesson only rearranges what Lessons 1–5 already gave you, and asks you to state your own position before Stage 3 moves from philosophy into the mechanics of self-mastery.

Four traditions have come up: the Gita's dharma and nishkama karma, Aristotle's arete and eudaimonia, Stoic virtue and the dichotomy of control, and Buddhism's Four Noble Truths and anatta. It would be tempting, and dishonest, to conclude they're all basically saying the same thing in different words. They aren't. Here's the actual shape of it.

TraditionWhat's the ultimate point?Is there a stable self?What must you not cling to?
Gita (dharma) Fulfilling your dharma; ultimately, liberation (moksha) — not your own happiness Yes — atman, an eternal self identical with Brahman The fruits of your action, not the action or the self
Aristotle (arete) Your own eudaimonia — flourishing across a complete life Yes — a rational soul with settled character (hexis) Nothing, fully — external goods genuinely matter too
Stoicism Virtue — the only good, exercised through reason Yes — a rational "ruling faculty," but externals don't touch it Everything except your own judgment and choice
Buddhism Cessation of dukkha, by uprooting craving itself No — anatta; no fixed self anywhere in the picture Everything, including the idea of a fixed self doing the clinging

Where they actually converge

Two things hold across all four, and they're the two ideas this curriculum will keep building on:

  1. Excellence is sustained practice, not a trophy. None of them treat a single achievement, win, or peak moment as the thing that matters — arete is a hexis built over time, dharma is fulfilled through ongoing right action, Stoic virtue is continuously exercised judgment, the Eightfold Path is a practice, not a certificate.
  2. Don't cling to what you can't hold. Every tradition tells you to release your grip on something — outcomes, externals, or the self itself — while still acting fully. None of them tell you to stop trying.

Where they actually diverge — and it doesn't split East vs. West

Notice, from the table, that the split isn't "Indian philosophy says X, Greek philosophy says Y." Aristotle and the Gita both assume a stable self and both think externals or duty genuinely matter beyond your own attitude toward them — closer to each other on those two questions than either is to Buddhism or Stoicism. Stoicism and Buddhism both arrive at radical non-attachment to almost everything external — but for different reasons, and Stoicism still keeps a stable rational self at the center while Buddhism denies one exists at all. The real fault lines run through both hemispheres, not between them.

Before Stage 3, state your actual position Not which one sounds wisest to cite. Which one, functionally, is closest to how you actually want to relate to your own pursuit of excellence — your own flourishing (Aristotle), duty regardless of your own happiness (Gita), virtue as the only thing that's really yours (Stoicism), or releasing even the self that's doing the striving (Buddhism)? You're allowed to say "mostly one, but I don't buy the metaphysics" or "genuinely torn between two." You are not allowed to dodge into "they're all the same really."
Write your actual position — in your own words, not a restatement of any one tradition.

What is excellence ultimately for, as far as you're concerned right now? It's fine if this is a blend, a preference with reservations, or genuinely unresolved — but make it specific enough that you could recognize, in six months, whether you still believe it.

Not saved yet.
Why this doesn't need to be resolved yet Stage 3 turns from philosophy to mechanics: how to actually control attention, emotion, and impulse. Every one of these four traditions offers real techniques for that — Patanjali's abhyasa and vairagya, Stoic cognitive discipline, Buddhist attention training — and the techniques converge far more than the metaphysics does. You don't have to pick a winner above before you can start training your mind; you just have to know, honestly, where you currently stand.
Stuck, or disagree? If the table above flattens something important — a tradition made to sound more settled or more different than it really is — say so directly. Four rows in a table is a compression of entire schools of thought; push back on any row that feels wrong.
