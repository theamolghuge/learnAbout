Case Studies

Six figures across science, sports, art, and business — each answering the same six questions your original brief asked for.

Not comprehensive biographies — extracted for one specific, applicable pattern each, per this workspace's own rule against hero-worship. Built for Lesson 30, which uses two of these in depth; the rest are here for breadth, with real, deliberate attention given to Indian figures per your original brief.
Srinivasa Ramanujan — mathematician (India, 1887–1920)
Believed: that mathematical truth could be reached by intuition first, proof later — he claimed many results came to him whole, in dreams, and worked backward to justify them.
Trained: almost entirely self-taught from a single tutorial textbook, in colonial-era South India with no access to formal mathematical education or community.
Responded to failure: after repeated rejection and obscurity in India, kept working alone for years before G.H. Hardy at Cambridge recognized his 1913 letter as genuine genius.
Sacrificed: left India for Cambridge despite the cultural and dietary isolation it caused; his health — already fragile — worsened in the English climate, and he died at 32.
Did repeatedly, that ordinary people don't: kept three notebooks of results built with no peer feedback for over a decade, entirely for their own sake, before anyone outside his immediate circle knew they existed.
Apply: real, unwitnessed work can compound for years before anyone recognizes it — the recognition was not the reason the notebooks existed.
A.P.J. Abdul Kalam — aerospace scientist and 11th President of India (1931–2015)
Believed: that scientific capability and personal humility weren't in tension — stayed known for a simple lifestyle through the highest office in India.
Trained: as an aerospace engineer, working up through India's space and missile programs over decades before leading the SLV-III satellite launch (1980) and the 1998 Pokhran-II nuclear tests.
Responded to failure: India's early satellite launch vehicle program had real, public failures before the SLV-III's success — he stayed in the program through them rather than exiting after a setback.
Sacrificed: a conventional post-government retirement — spent his later years engaging more than a million students directly, in person, rather than withdrawing from public life.
Did repeatedly, that ordinary people don't: treated direct engagement with students as core work, not a side project — an unusual use of a former head of state's time.
Apply: technical mastery and public humility are a genuine combination to aim for, not a contradiction to resolve.
Sachin Tendulkar — cricketer (India, 24-year international career)
Believed: "discipline, focus, desire, passion, and fitness" were the actual requirements for longevity — his own stated formula, not a media narrative.
Trained: kept an essentially unchanged personal routine before every series for 24 years — consistency of process across two and a half decades, not novelty.
Responded to failure: by his own account, deliberately never let opponents see a weakness or a rattled reaction — treated composure itself as a skill to maintain regardless of how a specific innings went.
Sacrificed: adapted his own equipment and technique repeatedly (designing his own ankle-toe support, for instance) rather than accepting standard gear that didn't fit his mechanics.
Did repeatedly, that ordinary people don't: sustained elite output across 24 years in a sport where careers routinely collapse after a handful — this stage's "thousand days" timeframe, made concrete.
Apply: longevity is a design problem (routine, equipment, composure), not just a talent or willpower problem.
Richard Feynman — physicist (1918–1988, Nobel Prize 1965)
Believed: that the actual engine of good science and a good life was curiosity for its own sake — "not knowing is unbearable, and knowing is wonderful" — not prizes or recognition.
Trained: by relentlessly translating abstract ideas into concrete, personal stories and analogies until he genuinely understood them himself, not just could recite them.
Responded to failure: treated "doubt everything" as a permanent discipline, including doubting his own prior conclusions, rather than defending a position once taken.
Sacrificed: reputation, at times — he was famously willing to say "I don't know" publicly rather than perform certainty.
Did repeatedly, that ordinary people don't: kept pursuing tangents purely because they were interesting (his famous plate-spinning digression is one of many), without needing them to be productive.
Apply: this curriculum's whole mastery-vs-validation axis (Lesson 1), from a working scientist's own mouth — the version of him worth learning from was chasing understanding, not proof of brilliance.
Ludwig van Beethoven — composer (1770–1827)
Believed: per his own 1802 Heiligenstadt Testament, that his art was the specific thing holding him back from suicide amid his encroaching deafness — an explicit, written statement of what he found meaning in, under real crisis.
Trained: continued composing and performing as his hearing symptoms progressed from the late 1790s onward, adapting his methods as the loss deepened.
Responded to failure/adversity: the years immediately following the Heiligenstadt crisis (from roughly 1802) are widely regarded as his "heroic period" — his output didn't just continue, its ambition and emotional range expanded.
Sacrificed: the assumption that a composer needs functioning hearing at all — by the end of his life he was almost completely deaf and still composing.
Did repeatedly, that ordinary people don't: wrote the Heiligenstadt Testament privately, for himself and his brothers, and never published or performed it — it was found only after his death, meaning the reckoning it documents wasn't for an audience.
Apply: a documented crisis of despair and a documented "heroic" creative period came from the same person, a few years apart — adversity's aftermath isn't linear or guaranteed (see Lesson 18), but it also isn't foreclosed by a genuine crisis.
Charlie Munger — investor (1924–2023)
Believed: "an even temperament is more important than a big brain" — his own explicit ranking of what actually predicts good decisions over time.
Trained: deliberately built a "latticework of mental models" by studying psychology, economics, physics, biology, and history together, at a time when most investors specialized narrowly.
Responded to failure: used "inversion" as a standing method — instead of asking how to succeed, routinely asked how a plan could fail, and worked backward from there.
Sacrificed: the comfort of specialization — building genuine competence across multiple unrelated fields is slower and less immediately rewarded than going deep in one.
Did repeatedly, that ordinary people don't: by his own account, tried to be a little wiser each day than the day before — a standard measured against yesterday's self, not against other people.
Apply: temperament (steadiness under pressure, resistance to overconfidence) is a trainable variable worth deliberately investing in, not just a fixed trait some people have.
