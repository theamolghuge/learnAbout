Glossary

The canonical terms for this workspace — Sanskrit, Greek, Japanese, and psychological vocabulary, defined once and used consistently everywhere else.

Populated as each term is introduced in a lesson, not only after it's mastered — this topic spans too many unfamiliar traditions to withhold a reference until later. Grows every session; see Lesson 1 for the terms below in context.

Stage 1 — Awakening

Mastery-orientation
Doing work because closing the gap between your current and possible ability is intrinsically worth doing — the work is the point, and both success and failure are read as information rather than verdicts.
Avoid: "growth mindset" (a related but distinct, narrower term — see Stage 6), "passion"
Validation-orientation
Doing work primarily for what it will get you — praise, proof, rank, relief from a fear of being ordinary — such that the work is a means and the outcome carries your sense of worth.
Avoid: "ego," "insecurity" (related, covered more precisely in Stage 8)
Dharma
In the Gita's usage: one's proper duty or role in a given situation — the action that is genuinely yours to do, as distinct from an action taken to chase an outcome or imitate someone else's role.
Avoid: "destiny," "purpose" (looser modern words that lose the specificity of "the action that is actually yours, here, now")
Nishkama karma
"Action without desire [for its fruits]" — full effort applied to an action while releasing attachment to how the outcome turns out. Not indifference to results, and not an excuse for low effort; the attachment being released is to the outcome, not to doing the work well.
Avoid: "don't care about winning," "detachment" used loosely
Competence (Self-Determination Theory)
One of three basic psychological needs identified by Deci and Ryan (with autonomy and relatedness) — the felt sense of mastery and effectiveness. Satisfying it intrinsically (for its own sake) predicts more durable motivation than chasing it through external reward.
Avoid: "confidence" (a related but different concept — competence is about effectiveness, confidence is about belief)
Dichotomy of control
Epictetus's sort, opening the Enchiridion: some things are "up to us" (judgment, effort, choice, response) and some are not (outcomes, other people, luck, the past). Nearly all of Stoic practice is re-applying this sort to a specific situation.
Avoid: "control what you can," "don't worry about it" (both flatten the sort into vague advice)
Responsibility (this workspace's usage)
Full ownership of the "up to us" half of a situation — effort, preparation, response — with no attempt to control or take blame for the "not up to us" half. Distinct from both fatalism (doing nothing because outcomes aren't guaranteed) and self-blame (owning things that were never controllable).
Avoid: "accountability" (a narrower, more institutional word), "it's on me" used to mean blanket guilt

Stage 2 — Philosophy

Arete
Aristotle's term for excellence or virtue — the quality of being genuinely good at the activity of being human. Not a feeling or an innate gift; built through repeated, specific action (see hexis).
Avoid: "talent," "virtue" used loosely to mean generic moral goodness
Hexis
A stable, trained disposition of character, built by repeatedly performing a specific kind of action until it becomes who you are — the mechanism by which arete is built (Nicomachean Ethics II.1).
Avoid: "habit" alone (loses the emphasis that the specific actions repeated are directional, not neutral)
Eudaimonia
Aristotle's term for the good life — commonly mistranslated "happiness." Better read as "flourishing": a description of an entire life lived through excellent activity, not a mood or a peak moment (Nicomachean Ethics I.7, "in a complete life"). Aristotle holds this also requires some external goods — see "equipment" below, which the Stoics explicitly deny.
Avoid: "happiness" without qualification, "wellbeing" used to mean a feeling rather than a whole-life description
Adiaphora (indifferents)
In Stoic ethics: everything that isn't virtue or vice — health, wealth, reputation, even life itself. Classed "indifferent" because none of it determines whether your life is good; that's settled by virtue alone. Split into "preferred" (health, resources) and "dispreferred" (illness, poverty) — you rationally select a preferred indifferent, but only choose virtue.
Avoid: "doesn't matter" (indifferents still matter practically — they just don't count as "good" in the strict Stoic sense)
Equipment (chorēgia)
Aristotle's term for the external goods — friends, resources, the absence of catastrophic misfortune — that he argues are necessary alongside virtue for eudaimonia (Nicomachean Ethics I.8–9). The direct point of disagreement with the Stoic claim that virtue alone is good.
Avoid: "luck," "privilege" (both import modern connotations Aristotle isn't making)
Dukkha
The First Noble Truth: ordinary life, as usually lived, is pervaded by suffering or unsatisfactoriness — a stronger claim than "life has pain," extending to the instability of even pleasant experience.
Avoid: "suffering" alone (loses the "unsatisfactoriness" nuance — dukkha isn't only acute pain)
Tanha
Craving — the Second Noble Truth's cause of dukkha: grasping at impermanent things (including a sense of fixed self) as if they could be held onto permanently.
Avoid: "desire" alone (ordinary wanting isn't the target — grasping at impermanence as if it were permanent is)
Elenchus
Socrates' method: asking someone to define a concept, then using their own further beliefs to show the definition doesn't hold — ending in aporia (productive puzzlement), not a supplied correct answer. The structural template behind this workspace's own confront callouts.
Avoid: "Socratic questioning" used loosely to mean any leading question (elenchus specifically targets a stated definition's internal contradictions)
Aporia
The state of productive puzzlement an elenchus ends in — genuine confusion about something you thought you understood, treated as a legitimate stopping point, not a failure to reach a resolved answer.
Avoid: "confusion" alone (loses the "productive," deliberately-arrived-at quality)
Satori
Zen's term for direct, unmediated enlightenment — reached through meditation and practice, not scriptural study or argument. Marks Zen's distinct epistemological stance from the more discursive traditions covered elsewhere in Stage 2.
Avoid: "enlightenment" alone (loses the specific emphasis on direct experience over textual/rational understanding)
Mushin
"No-mind" — a Zen state of acting without hesitation, calculation, or conscious attention to one's own mechanics. Converges with Beilock's explicit-monitoring research on choking (Lesson 19) from a completely different method.
Avoid: "flow" alone (related but distinct — flow theory, in your attention workspace, is a modern psychological construct; mushin is a specific contemplative one)
Wu wei
"Effortless action" (Tao Te Ching) — acting without forcing against the grain of a situation. A genuine tension with tapas (Stage 4), not a synonym for it — best read as describing mastery's felt experience after the effortful years, not a substitute for them.
Avoid: "laziness," "going with the flow" casually (both flatten a specific claim about skilled, non-resistant action into passivity)
Anatta
"Not-self" — the doctrine that there is no unchanging, permanent self underneath experience, only a continually changing process. Directly disagrees with the Gita/Vedanta's atman (an eternal self identical with Brahman) — a real fault line inside Indian philosophy, not a rounding error.
Avoid: "ego death," "no personality" (both import meanings anatta doesn't claim)

Stage 3 — Self-Mastery

Yoga (Patanjali's definition)
"The stilling of the fluctuations of the mind" (Yoga Sutras 1.2) — mental discipline, not physical postures. Asana (posture) is one of eight limbs, included mainly to enable sitting still for the mental work.
Avoid: "yoga" used to mean only the physical practice, without qualifying which sense is meant
Abhyasa
Sustained practice — effort toward mental steadiness, applied for a long time, without interruption, with genuine care (Yoga Sutras 1.13–14). One of two jointly required components of mental discipline.
Avoid: "discipline" alone (loses the specific emphasis on duration and continuity)
Vairagya
Non-attachment — releasing the pull of craving toward sensory or worldly objects (Yoga Sutras 1.15–16). The other jointly required half of stilling the mind; this workspace's technical version of the "don't cling" move seen across Stage 2.
Avoid: "detachment" used to mean apathy or checking out — vairagya is paired with, not a substitute for, abhyasa
Cyclic sighing
A breathing technique — double inhale through the nose, long exhale through the mouth — shown in a Stanford RCT (Balban et al., 2023) to reduce physiological arousal faster than box breathing or meditation. A body-first regulation tool, distinct from the reasoning-based techniques covered in the anvikshiki workspace.
Avoid: "deep breathing" generically (the double-inhale/long-exhale structure is what the evidence supports, not breathing deeply in general)

Stage 4 — Discipline

Tapas
Disciplined effort, often translated "austerity" — the Sanskrit root carries a sense of heat. One of three components of kriya yoga (Yoga Sutras 2.1). Effort that includes deliberate friction, not effort that only happens when it feels good.
Avoid: "willpower" (tapas is the effort applied once you're already engaged, not the mechanism that gets you there — see context cue)
Context cue
A stable environmental trigger — time of day, location, a preceding action, the people present, or emotional state — that, paired with repetition and reward, makes a behavior automatic rather than motivation-dependent (Wendy Wood's habit research). The evidence-based answer to "what makes me do it when I don't feel like it."
Avoid: "trigger" alone in a clinical/emotional sense (this is the neutral behavioral-science usage)
Self-efficacy
Bandura's term for the belief that you're specifically capable of a specific task — narrower than general confidence or self-esteem. Predicts goal commitment, response to negative feedback, and strategy development (Locke & Latham). Built through real, small wins, not encouragement.
Avoid: "confidence" alone (loses the task-specific scope)
Goal-setting theory (specific and difficult)
Locke & Latham's core, heavily replicated finding: specific, difficult goals outperform vague or easy ones ("do your best" underperforms a specific hard target). Requires self-efficacy to actually produce commitment rather than disengagement.
Avoid: "ambitious goals" alone (loses the specificity requirement, which is doing real work in the research)
Abstinence violation effect
Guilt and self-judgment after a lapse ("I have no discipline," "I've ruined it") that predicts full abandonment far better than the lapse itself does (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985). Originally addiction-relapse research; used in this workspace as a borrowed mechanism for ordinary missed-practice days, not a claim of equivalence.
Avoid: "falling off the wagon" (imports the addiction framing this workspace deliberately keeps at arm's length)
Implementation intention
A decided-in-advance "if [situation], then [response]" plan (Gollwitzer, 1999) — outperforms a general intention because the decision is already made before the moment of resistance arrives. Sourced in full in the attention workspace; used here for resistance and lapse recovery specifically.
Avoid: "goal," "intention" alone (the if-then structure is what does the work, not just having a plan in mind)

Stage 5 — Mastery

Deliberate practice
Practice with a specific goal for the session, full attention, immediate feedback on correctness, and a deliberate push past current ability — as distinct from mere repetition, which doesn't reliably improve skill regardless of hours logged (Ericsson & Pool, Peak).
Avoid: "10,000 hours," "practice" alone (both lose the specific structural requirements that make practice "deliberate")
Testing effect (retrieval practice)
Trying to recall information from memory produces significantly better long-term retention than rereading it (Roediger & Karpicke) — one of only two study techniques (with spacing) rated "high utility" in the comparative research. This workspace's own reason for ending every lesson with a journal prompt rather than a summary.
Avoid: "studying" generically (rereading and highlighting, the most common study methods, are specifically the lower-utility ones this contrasts with)
Grit (ultimate concern)
Perseverance and passion for long-term goals (Duckworth) — a single concern organizing effort across years, not short-term intensity. Explains a real but modest ~4% of variance in the original research; don't inflate beyond what the data shows.
Avoid: "passion" alone (loses the multi-year duration requirement)
Growth mindset
Interpreting setbacks as information requiring a new strategy and feedback-seeking, not just more effort (Dweck; built on Mueller & Dweck's 1998 praise study). The common misreading — "believe you can improve and try harder" — drops the strategy-change requirement that the original research actually found.
Avoid: equating growth mindset with effort alone

Stage 6 — Adversity

Logotherapy
Viktor Frankl's theory that the primary human drive is meaning, not pleasure or power — found through purposeful work, love, or unavoidable suffering. Distinct from a claim that suffering itself is good; the claim is about the residual freedom to choose one's attitude toward it.
Avoid: "everything happens for a reason" (Frankl's claim is narrower and doesn't assert suffering has inherent purpose)
Hedonic adaptation / arrival fallacy
The drift back to a stable happiness baseline after major positive or negative changes (Brickman & Campbell) — lottery winners and accident survivors converge toward baseline faster than intuition predicts. The "arrival fallacy" (Ben-Shahar) is the false belief that reaching a goal produces lasting happiness; it produces real but fast-fading delight instead.
Avoid: "nothing makes you happy" (overstates the claim — baseline returns, it isn't zero)
Post-traumatic growth
Positive psychological change some people report after struggling with major adversity (Tedeschi & Calhoun). Real for some people under some conditions, but not automatic or guaranteed — its main measurement instrument captures self-perceived rather than verified change, a genuine evidentiary limitation.
Avoid: "what doesn't kill you makes you stronger" (overclaims certainty the research doesn't support)

Stage 7 — Performance

Explicit monitoring
Consciously attending to the step-by-step mechanics of a well-learned skill under pressure — the mechanism Beilock's research identifies as disrupting automated performance. Distinct from distraction (task-irrelevant worry), which affects cognitively demanding tasks instead.
Avoid: "overthinking" alone (loses the specific target — it's monitoring your own mechanics specifically, not thinking in general)
Social comparison (information vs. verdict)
Festinger's basic human drive to evaluate ability against others — not eliminable, but usable in two modes: extracting specific, actionable information (useful) versus collapsing into a global judgment of self-worth (corrosive). The lever that matters is which mode is active, not whether comparison happens.
Avoid: "don't compare yourself to others" (asks to override a basic drive rather than redirect it)

Stage 8 — Character

Work engagement vs. workaholism
Both involve high effort and long hours (Schaufeli). What discriminates them is compulsion: engagement is driven by enjoyment and can be chosen not to happen without lasting distress; workaholism is driven by an inner compulsion that produces real anxiety or guilt even when stopping would be wise. This workspace's operational test for discipline vs. compulsion generally, not just at work.
Avoid: "hard worker" as a compliment without asking which mode is driving it
Secure vs. fragile self-esteem
Kernis's distinction — both can look like confidence from outside, but differ on stability (does it swing with wins/losses) and contingency (does it depend on continued approval). Verbal defensiveness under direct challenge is the measurable marker: less for secure, more for fragile. This workspace's operational test for confidence vs. ego.
Avoid: "high self-esteem" alone (both types present this way — the marker is what happens under challenge, not the baseline level)
High standards vs. perfectionism
Same standard, different response to falling short: self-compassion (adaptive) vs. harsh self-criticism (Hewitt & Flett's perfectionism research). Hewitt himself argues no form of perfectionism, strictly defined, is fully healthy — hold "adaptive perfectionism" loosely rather than as a settled good/bad split.
Avoid: "healthy perfectionism" stated flatly (the research this workspace cites pushes back on this framing)

Stage 9 — Purpose

Memento mori
"Remember you will die" — a Stoic practice (Meditations 2.11) of briefly using mortality as a filter for what deserves attention, not a permanent state of morbid dwelling. Used deliberately and specifically, then set aside.
Avoid: constant background dread (a misuse of the practice, not the practice itself)
Lokasangraha
"Welfare of the world" (Bhagavad Gita 3.20, 3.25) — Krishna's account of why even someone with nothing left to personally gain continues to act: skill redirected entirely toward others' benefit, not personal advancement. The Gita's version of purpose after mastery.
Avoid: "giving back" (a vaguer modern phrase that loses the specific "skill redirected, not retired" claim)
