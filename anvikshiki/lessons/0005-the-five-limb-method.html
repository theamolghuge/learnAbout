The Five-Limb Method

Four lessons gave you separate tools: name your hetu, name your pramana, test vyapti, screen for hetvabhasa. This lesson assembles them into one procedure you can actually run.

You now have everything the method needs — you just haven't run it as one motion yet. Classical Nyaya already had a name for the five-step shape you learned in Lesson 3: Pañcāvayava (पञ्चावयव, "the five limbs"). This lesson keeps that exact five-step skeleton, but shows you where each earlier lesson's check actually plugs into it — turning it from "a way to lay out a smoke-and-fire example" into a general-purpose procedure for any claim, belief, or decision.

The five limbs, with everything plugged in

LimbWhat you doWhere it plugs in
1. Pratijna
claim		 State precisely what you're concluding. Vague claims can't be tested. Lesson 1: do you even have a hetu, or is this an unexamined belief?
2. Hetu
reason		 State your reason, and name which pramana it actually is. Lesson 2: perception, inference, comparison, or testimony? If testimony, run the three-part apta test right here.
3. Udaharana
rule + example		 If it's an inference, state the general rule out loud and actively hunt for a counter-example. Lesson 3: the vyapti test. Lesson 4: screen for savyabhicara (inconstant sign), viruddha (contradictory sign), and satpratipaksha (an equally strong opposing reason).
4. Upanaya
application		 Confirm the specific case actually fits the rule. Lesson 4: is something directly observable already contradicting this (badhita)? Direct perception overrides inference.
5. Nigamana
conclusion		 Accept, reject, or explicitly suspend. If any earlier limb broke, this is not "accept" by default — it's "suspend, pending better evidence."

The 10-second version

For anything, fast Claim. Ground. Break it. Fit. Verdict. — say what you're claiming, say what it's grounded in, try hard to break the rule behind it, check the specific case actually fits, then conclude or admit you can't yet.

Worked example, start to finish

The recurring example from Lessons 3 and 4, run as one motion instead of separate exercises:

  1. Pratijna: "He's upset with me."
  2. Hetu: "He didn't reply quickly." This is anumana (inference) — I didn't perceive his emotional state directly, I'm inferring it from a sign.
  3. Udaharana: The rule underneath is "whenever someone doesn't reply quickly, they're upset." Counter-example, found immediately: my sister didn't reply for two days last week because her phone was in another room — she wasn't upset at all. Vyapti fails. This is also savyabhicara — the sign shows up constantly among people who aren't upset.
  4. Upanaya: He did, in fact, not reply — the specific case fits the sign. But that's beside the point now: the rule itself already broke at step 3, so confirming the sign is present doesn't rescue the conclusion.
  5. Nigamana: Suspended. "I don't actually know why he hasn't replied yet. I don't have valid grounds to conclude he's upset — I have a sign with no reliable rule behind it."

Notice what the method produced: not certainty, and not "he's definitely fine either" — a specific, honest admission of what you don't yet know, plus a clear description of what evidence would settle it (asking him, or waiting to see the pattern). That's the method working correctly. A method that always hands you a confident answer isn't examining anything — it's just decorating your first impression.

Retrieval check

What does Pañcāvayava mean?

The five limbs — the five-step syllogism structure.

At which limb do you name the pramana and run the apta test?

Limb 2, hetu — stating and classifying your reason.

At which limb do you test vyapti and screen for most hetvabhasa?

Limb 3, udaharana — stating and stress-testing the rule.

If an earlier limb breaks, what should limb 5 (nigamana) be?

A suspended verdict, not a forced conclusion either way.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Run a live claim through all five limbs

  1. Pick a claim or belief that's currently live for you — not a reused example — something you're actually unsure about or reacting to right now.
  2. Write all five limbs out, in full sentences, using the table above as your checklist. Don't skip the pramana-naming or the counter-example search — those are where real inferences usually get caught.
  3. If a limb breaks, stop trying to rescue the conclusion. Write the honest nigamana: accept, reject, or suspend — and if suspended, name exactly what evidence would resolve it.

Check your work: a suspended verdict with a named next step ("I'd need to ask her directly" / "I'd need to see this pattern repeat three times") is a better result from this drill than a confident accept or reject — it means the method actually did something instead of rubber-stamping what you already believed.
