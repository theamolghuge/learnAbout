Chains and Ranges

Most decisions fail for one of two boring reasons: nobody asked what the outcome itself would cause next, and nobody asked how bad the bad version could actually get.

Lessons 12 and 13 got you to a clean comparison between well-framed options. This lesson adds two more checks before committing — both of them small extensions of tools you already have, not new machinery.

Second-order effects: a sadhya can become a hetu

Recall from Lesson 3 that sadhya is the thing being proved — the conclusion of an inference. Nothing stops that conclusion from becoming the hetu of a further inference. Most people run the five-limb method once, reach a first-order conclusion, and stop. The second-order check is just: take that conclusion, and ask what it now makes more or less likely, one link further out.

It branches, not just chains A single first-order effect often feeds multiple second-order effects, not one tidy line. "More capital" (first-order effect of a loan) is simultaneously a hetu for "more capacity to expand" and a hetu for "more debt-service obligation." Draw the branch, don't just extend the line.

Detection question: if this first-order outcome actually happens, what does it now make more likely, or less likely, that I haven't priced in?

Range instead of a single point

Running the five-limb method once and getting one verdict feels complete, but it usually only tests the modal case — the udaharana you found most plausible on the first pass. The stronger move: construct three separate udaharana claims on purpose — the most optimistic plausible rule, the most pessimistic plausible rule, and the most likely one — and vyapti-test each independently, rather than defending your first draft.

This directly connects to Lesson 12's reversibility check. Reversibility isn't a fixed property of a decision in the abstract — it's determined by how bad the worst case actually is. A decision with a tolerable worst case behaves like a two-way door even if it feels big; a decision with a catastrophic or unrecoverable worst case behaves like a one-way door regardless of how unlikely that outcome seems. You can't know which one you're actually facing until you've deliberately built the worst-case scenario, not just the likely one.

Worked example

Decision: leaving a stable job to start a business.

First-order effect: loss of salary.

Second-order branch: loss of salary → financial stress → strain on a close relationship (one branch) and, separately, freed time → faster skill development in an area the current job never used (a different branch, from the same first-order root).

Worst case, explicitly built: business fails within a year, savings are largely depleted, re-entering the job market is harder than expected because of the gap. Checked for severity: if this is genuinely recoverable within, say, two years — uncomfortable but not catastrophic — treat the decision as closer to a two-way door than it first felt. If savings are thin enough that this scenario means real hardship for dependents, it's a one-way door, and Lesson 12's apta-qualified conversations become the right next step, not just "trying it."

Best case: business gains real traction within a year, income eventually exceeds the old salary, and the second-order skill development compounds. Likely case: moderate financial strain, real learning, a return to employment within 8-12 months at a comparable or slightly better position than before.

None of these three replace judgment — but a decision made after actually writing all three, instead of just the hopeful one, rarely gets blindsided by an outcome nobody considered.

Retrieval check

What makes an effect "second-order"?

A first-order conclusion (sadhya) used as a new hetu.

Can one first-order effect lead to more than one second-order effect?

Yes — it typically branches, not just chains linearly.

Why build three separate udaharana claims instead of one?

A single pass usually only tests the modal case.

What actually determines whether a decision is a one-way or two-way door?

How severe and recoverable the worst case actually is.

Drill (do this now — ~20 minutes)

Extend the same decision one step further

  1. Use the decision from Lesson 12 or 13's drill. Name its clearest first-order effect.
  2. Chain at least one second-order effect from it — and check whether it actually branches into more than one, rather than forcing a single line.
  3. Write worst case, best case, and likely case as three short, separate paragraphs — each with its own one-sentence rule (udaharana), not just three adjectives.
  4. Reassess reversibility specifically from the worst case: is it a one-way door or a two-way door, now that you've actually written it out?

Check your work: if your worst case reads almost identically to your likely case, you softened it — go back and write the version you'd actually be upset about, not the version that's comfortable to imagine.
