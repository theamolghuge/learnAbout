Four More Biases, Closing the List

Two more precise parallels, one that extends a lesson you've already had, and one final honest non-parallel — closing out the decision-relevant biases deferred from earlier lessons.

Sunk cost

Continuing to invest in something because of what you've already put in, rather than what it's actually likely to produce going forward.

Daily version: finishing a bad movie because you already sat through an hour of it — as if the hour already spent could still be recovered by sitting through more.

The classical parallel: "I already invested X" gets treated as a hetu for "continuing is worthwhile" — but is it? Check savyabhicara (Lesson 4): does having sunk costs show up both when continuing genuinely is worthwhile and when it isn't? Constantly — some continuations after heavy investment pay off, some don't, and the amount already spent doesn't discriminate between the two at all. It's an inconstant sign wearing the costume of a good reason.

Detection question: if I'd invested nothing so far, would I start this today, from here? If not, the past investment isn't actually informing the decision — it's just making it feel harder to stop.

Self-serving bias

Crediting your own ability for successes, and blaming circumstances or other people for failures.

Daily version: "I got the promotion because I worked hard" (success, internal) versus "I didn't get the raise because my manager doesn't get it" (failure, external) — for the same person, in the same job, in the same year.

The classical parallel — already established: this is ahamkara (Lesson 6) doing exactly what it does — claiming ownership of what flatters, and declining ownership of what doesn't. You don't need a new concept for this; it's the same "I-making" function from Lesson 6, applied specifically to explaining outcomes rather than to naming emotions as "mine."

Detection question: would I explain someone else's identical outcome the same way I'm explaining my own?

Base-rate neglect

Ignoring the actual, established general frequency of something in favor of a single vivid or recent case.

Daily version: deciding a new hire will probably be great because the last interview was impressive, while ignoring the actual historical hit rate of "impressive interview" predicting "great hire" at your company.

The classical parallel: this is Lesson 11's hasty generalization, run in reverse. Hasty generalization builds a rule from too small a sample; base-rate neglect ignores a well-established larger-sample rule in favor of one small, vivid, unrepresentative case. Same underlying error — sample size versus salience — approached from opposite directions.

Detection question: what does the actual historical rate say, and am I overriding it with a single memorable case?

Overconfidence — the second honest non-parallel

Stated confidence exceeding actual accuracy — feeling 90% sure while being right closer to 60% of the time.

Why this doesn't get a hetvabhasa either Like anchoring in Lesson 10, overconfidence isn't a structural flaw in a specific inference — it's a miscalibration between how certain you feel and how often that feeling turns out to be right, measured across many judgments. There's no single sign, rule, or application to critique. It's a measurement problem, not a reasoning-structure problem, and pretending otherwise for the sake of a tidy classical label would be dishonest.

Detection question: across my last ten predictions made at "I'm 90% sure," how many actually came true? (Most people, checked honestly, find the number well below 90%.)

Corrective: state confidence as a range or a rough percentage rather than a binary certainty, and actually track your calibration over time on decisions that matter.

Retrieval check

Why is "I already invested this much" an unreliable hetu?

It's inconstant — present in both good and bad continuations.

Which existing concept explains self-serving bias?

Ahamkara — claiming credit, declining blame, as usual.

How does base-rate neglect relate to hasty generalization?

The same error, approached from the opposite direction.

Why doesn't overconfidence get a hetvabhasa parallel?

It's a calibration problem, not a structural inference flaw.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Check all four against one real situation

  1. Pick something you're currently continuing mostly because you've already put time or money into it. Ask the sunk-cost question honestly: would you start it today, from zero?
  2. Pick a recent success and a recent failure of similar size. Write your explanation for each. Would you give someone else's identical outcome the same explanation?
  3. Pick a recent prediction you were confident about. What's the actual base rate for that kind of prediction being right, if you know it or can estimate it? Does the vivid specific case you're leaning on actually beat that rate?
  4. Look back at your last several "I'm sure" moments. Roughly how often were you actually right?

Check your work: the self-serving bias check is the one people most often skip past too quickly — sit with the comparison (same outcome, someone else, same explanation?) for at least a full minute before answering.
