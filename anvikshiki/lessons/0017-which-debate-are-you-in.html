Which Debate Are You Actually In?

Nyaya named three kinds of debate — only one of them is worth arguing in good faith. It also named the specific tricks used in the other two, over two thousand years before "straw man" had a name.

Every lesson so far has assumed you're examining a claim honestly. Sometimes you're in an exchange where the other side isn't — and continuing to reason carefully, as if you were still in a good-faith conversation, is exactly the wrong move. Gautama's Nyaya Sutra named this distinction directly.

Three kinds of debate

TypeSanskritGoal
DiscussionVadaGenuinely ascertaining the truth — both sides could update.
WranglingJalpaWinning, by fair means or foul — tricks are in bounds.
CavilVitandaDefeating the other side without even establishing your own position.

The first practical skill is just noticing which one you're actually in. Arguing in good faith, vada-style, against someone running jalpa or vitanda is exhausting and usually losing — you're playing a different game than they are. Sometimes the right move isn't to argue harder. It's to name the mismatch, or step out of the exchange entirely.

Chala: the three named quibbles

Chala (छल) means deliberately misreading what someone said to derail the argument, in one of three specific ways:

Chala typeWhat it doesClassical example
Vak-chala
verbal quibble		 Willfully taking a word to mean something other than what was intended. Someone says a child has a nava kambala ("new blanket"). The other pretends to hear nava as "nine" instead of "new" and objects: why would a child need nine blankets?
Upacara-chala
figurative quibble		 Taking a metaphorical statement literally, or vice versa, to invalidate it. Someone says "my company pays me peanuts" (meaning: a low salary). The other responds: "no, they pay you money" — taking the figure of speech literally to score a pointless point.
Samanya-chala
generalization quibble		 Attacking an inflated, more extreme general version of a specific, bounded claim. Someone says "this medicine cures this disease." The other responds: so any existing thing cures any other existing thing? — generalizing a specific claim until it's absurd, then attacking the absurd version.

The modern names for these are already in your vocabulary from Part 5's original list. Vak-chala and upacara-chala are both what modern logic calls equivocation — shifting a word's meaning mid-argument. Samanya-chala is exactly straw man — responding to a bigger, distorted version of the claim instead of the actual one.

Modern straw man, same pattern as samanya-chala: "I think we should slow down and check the requirements more carefully." — "Oh, so you think we should never move fast and just analyze forever?"

Jati: objection by surface resemblance

Jati (जाति) is an objection based on a superficial similarity or difference, with no real connecting relation to the actual claim. Gautama's own example: someone infers "sound is non-eternal, because it's an effect — like a pot." The objector answers: "sound is eternal, because it's intangible — like the sky." The rebuttal sounds structurally parallel, but "intangible" has no real bearing on "eternal" the way "being an effect" does — it's a surface-level echo of the original argument's shape, not a real counter-argument.

Modern name: close to false equivalence — treating two things as comparable because of one shared surface trait, ignoring every relevant difference.

Two honest non-parallels

Ad hominem and circular reasoning Ad hominem (attacking the person instead of the claim) doesn't get one of Nyaya's specifically named chala or jati tricks in the sources for this lesson. It fits the spirit of vitanda in general — abandoning any attempt to establish your own position and just attacking — but that's a category-level connection, not a named technique, and it's presented here as such rather than as a precise classical match. Circular reasoning likewise doesn't have a clean parallel in the material sourced for this lesson. Both are worth knowing on their own modern terms rather than forced into a classical box that doesn't quite fit.

The practical move: name it out loud

A chala or jati trick depends on the other person pretending it's a real argument. Naming it plainly and calmly — "that's responding to a bigger claim than the one I made" or "I think we're talking past each other on what that word means" — usually does more than continuing to argue the substance, because it removes the cover the trick needs to keep working.

Retrieval check

Which debate type genuinely aims at the truth?

Vada — the only one worth arguing in good faith.

Which chala matches "straw man"?

Samanya-chala — attacking an inflated general version.

Which two chala both match "equivocation"?

Vak-chala and upacara-chala — shifting a word's meaning.

Why isn't ad hominem paired with a specific named chala?

No precise classical match was found — only a loose fit.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Classify a real argument

  1. Recall a recent disagreement — in person, in writing, anywhere.
  2. Classify it: vada, jalpa, or vitanda? Be honest about which side you were on too.
  3. If a trick was used against you (or, honestly, by you), identify which one: vak-chala, upacara-chala, samanya-chala, or jati.
  4. Write the specific sentence you could have said in the moment to name it plainly, without escalating.

Check your work: if you couldn't find yourself using any trick at all, look again at your strongest moment in the exchange — the line you were proudest of tends to be worth double-checking.
