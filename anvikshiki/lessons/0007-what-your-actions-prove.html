What Your Actions Actually Prove

"I value my health." Is that a fact you checked, or testimony from a source you never cross-examined — yourself?

Lesson 2 taught you to run testimony through the apta test before trusting it: did the source actually know, were they honest, did they communicate clearly? You've been running that test on other people. This lesson turns it on the person you cross-examine least: you.

Self-testimony is still testimony

"I value my family." "I'm not materialistic." "I care about my health." Each of these is a claim about you, spoken by you — which makes it shabda, testimony, exactly as much as if someone else said it. And per Lesson 2, testimony only counts as a real pramana if the source clears three tests. Applied to your own self-talk:

  1. Does the source actually know this? Or is it aspirational — what you'd like to be true, inherited from what you were taught to say, never actually checked against evidence?
  2. Is the source being honest? Or is there a motive to present a flattering story — and per Lesson 6, whose motive would that be? Ahamkara's — the function whose entire job is curating "mine" into something presentable.
  3. Is the claim precise enough to test? "I value health" is too vague to check against anything. "I prioritize sleep over late-night screen time" is precise enough to fail or pass.

Most self-testimony fails test 1 quietly. Nobody sets out to lie to themselves about their values — they just never ran the check, the same way "everyone says X" gets repeated without anyone actually verifying it.

The more reliable pramana: watch, don't ask

If self-testimony is shaky, what's better? Pratyaksha — direct observation — of what you actually did, over a real stretch of time, is far harder to spin than a sentence about yourself. Economists have a modern name for this exact move — revealed preference: what you choose, under real constraints, is better evidence of what you value than what you say you value. Classical Anvikshiki got there first, just with different vocabulary: don't trust unverified shabda when pratyaksha is available.

The instrument: calendar and wallet For the last two real weeks — not a hypothetical average week — where did your free hours actually go? Where did discretionary money actually go? Where did unprompted conversation actually go? This is observational data, not self-report. It's the raw material the rest of this lesson reasons from.

But don't stop at the data — run it through the method

This is the part most "just look at your calendar" advice skips, and it's where things go wrong if you skip it too. Time spent on something doesn't automatically mean it's valued — that's exactly the savyabhicara trap from Lesson 4: the sign (time spent) shows up both when something is genuinely valued and when it's habit, obligation, or avoidance. Run the actual inference through Lesson 5's method instead of stopping at the raw observation:

  1. Pratijna: "My behavior shows I actually prioritize [X] over [stated value Y]."
  2. Hetu: "Because I spent [specific observed time/money/attention] on X." Name this as anumana — you're inferring a priority from a sign, not observing the priority directly.
  3. Udaharana: state the rule — "time spent on something reflects what's valued" — and hunt hard for a counter-example. Is there a real case where you spent time on X without valuing it? (Doom-scrolling isn't a value, it's often ahamkara and habit running on autopilot, closer to what Lesson 6 called prakriti than to anything purusha actually endorses. A demanding job isn't a value freely chosen each day, it's a constraint.)
  4. Upanaya: does this specific case actually fit the rule, once habit and constraint are ruled out?
  5. Nigamana: accept the revealed priority, or suspend — "I don't yet know if this is value or autopilot; here's how I'd tell the difference."
Skipping the method: "I spent 12 hours on my phone this week and only 2 with my kids, so I must not really value family." Fast, guilt-inducing, and unexamined — it never checked whether the phone time was habit/avoidance (savyabhicara) rather than a freely endorsed priority.
Running the method: "12 hours of phone time this week. Was that chosen, or automatic? Checking — most of it was scrolling after the kids were already asleep, driven by exhaustion, not preference. The 2 hours with them, on the other hand, were the only unscheduled time I actively protected. That's better evidence of what I'd choose if I weren't exhausted — which points at a constraint to fix, not a value to doubt."

What to do with a real gap

When stated and revealed values genuinely diverge — after the method, not before it — that gap is data, not a verdict on your character. It tells you exactly one of three things: the stated value was aspirational testimony that was never true, the revealed behavior is autopilot rather than a real priority, or there's a structural constraint standing between what you'd choose and what you're currently doing. Which one it is determines what's worth changing — a decision-making question this course returns to later.

Drill (do this now — ~20 minutes)

Cross-examine one stated value

  1. Pick one value you'd currently claim about yourself. Run the three-part apta test on the claim itself first: do you actually know this, are you being honest, is it precise enough to test?
  2. Pull real data from the last two weeks — calendar, spending, or actual conversations — relevant to that value.
  3. Run the full five-limb inference from the section above: state the candidate priority, name it as anumana, state the rule, hunt for a counter-example (habit? obligation? constraint?), apply, conclude or suspend.
  4. If a real gap survives the method, write down which of the three causes (false testimony, autopilot, or constraint) it most likely is, and why.

Check your work: if your conclusion arrived without you seriously trying to find a counter-example at the udaharana step, you've done the "skipping the method" version from the example above, not the real drill — go back and argue against your own conclusion for a minute before accepting it.
