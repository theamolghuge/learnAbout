Correlation, Crowds, and Credentials

Three more everyday reasoning errors — all three, this time, are precise modern names for something Lessons 1-5 already gave you the tools to catch.

Lesson 10 covered two biases with genuine classical parallels and one honest non-parallel. This installment covers three that all map cleanly onto tools you already have — a hasty rule, a coincidence mistaken for a cause, and testimony that was never actually checked.

Hasty generalization

Drawing a general rule from a sample too small or unrepresentative to support it. One bad meal, and a restaurant is written off forever. One difficult person from a group, and the whole group gets a label.

The classical parallel: this is a corrupted udaharana (Lesson 3) — the general rule step, populated from a sample too small to have had any real chance of surfacing a counter-example. The rule wasn't tested against enough cases to have earned the name "vyapti" (invariable concomitance); it was asserted after one or two data points, which is closer to a guess wearing the vyapti costume.

Detection question: how many cases did I actually check before deciding this is a rule, not an incident?

Corrective: before generalizing, name the sample size out loud. One or two cases is a hypothesis worth testing further, not a conclusion worth acting on.

False cause

Concluding that A causes B because A happened before B, or because the two correlate — without any mechanism connecting them and without ruling out coincidence or a third factor causing both.

Daily version: "I wore this shirt and we won, so it's lucky." Or, less obviously silly: two unrelated trends rising over the same years, assumed to be cause and effect just because the timing lines up.

The classical parallel: this is exactly what vyapti exists to rule out. Smoke reliably indicates fire not because they happen to occur together sometimes, but because there's a real, exception-free connection. Repeated timing or correlation, with no stated mechanism and no attempt to rule out a third factor, hasn't earned that connection — it's borrowing vyapti's authority without doing vyapti's work.

Detection question: is there an actual mechanism connecting these two things, or just repeated timing? Could something else explain both?

Corrective: name the mechanism explicitly as part of your udaharana. If you can't state one, treat the inference as provisional, not settled.

Appeal to authority and appeal to popularity

Accepting a claim because a credentialed or admired person said it, without checking whether their expertise actually covers this specific claim — or accepting a claim because many people believe it, without checking whether any of them independently verified it.

The classical parallel — a direct callback: both of these are exactly the shabda and apta framework from Lesson 2. A fallacious appeal to authority is testimony that fails apta test 1 for this specific domain — a brilliant physicist speaking confidently outside physics, a celebrity endorsing a medical claim. Appeal to popularity is the exact "everyone says X" example already flagged in Lesson 2: popularity isn't independent verification. If none of the "many people" ever checked the claim themselves, their agreement isn't many pieces of testimony — it's one unverified claim, copied and repeated, with a broken link at the very start of the chain.

Detection questions: is this person's expertise actually in the domain of this specific claim? Did these people independently verify it, or are they all repeating the same unverified original?

Corrective: trace testimony back to its actual originating source and run the apta test there — not on whoever repeated it to you most recently.

Retrieval check

What's wrong with the udaharana in a hasty generalization?

It's built from too small a sample to test it.

What does false cause borrow without earning?

Vyapti's exception-free connection, from mere correlation.

What framework do appeal to authority and popularity both fail?

The apta test — for shabda, testimony.

Where should you check the apta criteria in a chain of repeated claims?

At the originating source, not the most recent repeater.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Diagnose one real belief

  1. Pick one belief from recent memory — something you concluded, or accepted from someone else, without deeply examining it at the time.
  2. Check it against all three: was it built from too small a sample (hasty generalization)? Does it assume a cause from mere correlation or timing (false cause)? Does it rest on someone's say-so that you never traced back to an actual apta source (appeal to authority/popularity)?
  3. Name which one (or more than one) applies, and write specifically what would fix it — a larger sample, a named mechanism, or tracing the claim to its actual origin.

Check your work: if none of the three seem to apply, try one more belief before concluding your reasoning was clean here — these three are common enough that a completely clean first pick is somewhat unlikely.
