When the Reasons Tie

A hard decision usually isn't one option being secretly wrong. It's two real, comparably strong reasons pointing opposite directions — and this course already told you what to do about that.

Most of this course has been about finding the belief that fails the test. Real decisions are often harder than that: both candidate options survive a first-pass check, both have real hetu behind them, and you're still stuck. That's not a failure of reasoning. It's a specific, already-named state.

Recognizing genuine satpratipaksha in a decision

Recall satpratipaksha from Lesson 4 — an equally strong reason exists on the other side, and the honest state is a stalemate, not a proof. A hard decision is usually exactly this, applied to two competing claims about the future: "if I take the stable job, [outcome]" versus "if I take the risky offer, [outcome]." State each option as its own pratijna with its own hetu. If both survive an honest vyapti check — you genuinely can't find the counter-example that breaks one of them — you're not failing to decide. You're in a real satpratipaksha, and forcing a pick by sheer confidence at that point is exactly the mistake Lesson 4 warned about.

The corrective, already given — now operationalized

Lesson 4's corrective for satpratipaksha was: don't force a pick, go looking for a tiebreaker — ideally something closer to direct perception (pratyaksha) than another layer of inference. In a decision, that tiebreaker has a name: a small, real action that generates actual observation, instead of more debating with yourself. More thinking doesn't resolve a genuine tie between two comparably-reasoned claims about the future — you've already thought as hard as the available evidence allows. What resolves it is new evidence, and the fastest way to get new evidence is to go look, not to keep reasoning in place.

How big a probe: the reversibility check

Not every tiebreaker can be "just try it and see." Amazon's Jeff Bezos, in his 2015 letter to shareholders, drew a useful distinction — not an ancient one, credited plainly as modern — between decisions that are like one-way doors (consequential, hard or costly to reverse, deserving slow and careful handling) and two-way doors (changeable, safe to walk through and back out of if it's wrong). The size and safety of your tiebreaker probe should match which kind of door you're facing:

  • Two-way door: just take the smallest real version of the action and observe what actually happens. Direct pratyaksha, cheaply obtained.
  • One-way door: you can't cheaply get direct perception of the outcome, so get the next best thing — testimony from someone who has actually been through it. This is shabda, and it's worth running the full apta test (Lesson 2) on whoever you talk to: did they actually experience this, are they being honest about how it went, can they describe it precisely enough to be useful? A vague "it worked out for me" from someone who barely resembles your situation isn't a real tiebreaker.

Worked example

Decision: stay in a stable job, or take a risky early-stage offer.

Pratijna A: "Staying gives me security and steady growth." Hetu: track record of the current role, predictable trajectory.

Pratijna B: "Taking the offer gives me faster growth and more upside." Hetu: the role's scope, the team, the market opportunity.

Both survive a first vyapti check — neither has an obvious counter-example that breaks it outright. Genuine satpratipaksha.

Reversibility check: taking the new job is closer to a one-way door — a resume gap or a failed venture is harder to walk back than staying put. Direct trial ("just take it and see") is too costly a probe here.

Tiebreaker, matched to the door type: instead of more internal debate, talk directly to three people who made a comparable jump in the last year or two. Run the apta test on each: did they actually experience a genuinely similar situation, are they leveling with you about the hard parts and not just the highlight reel, can they answer specific questions rather than general reassurance? That conversation is closer to direct evidence than anything more reasoning alone can produce.

Retrieval check

What state is a genuinely hard decision usually in?

Satpratipaksha — two comparably strong reasons, tied.

What resolves a genuine satpratipaksha — more reasoning, or something else?

New evidence — closer to direct perception, not more inference.

What determines how big and safe your tiebreaker probe should be?

Reversibility — a one-way versus a two-way door.

For a one-way door, what should you run the apta test on?

Testimony from someone who actually lived it.

Drill (do this now — ~20 minutes)

Diagnose and design a tiebreaker for a live decision

  1. Pick a real decision you're currently sitting with. State each option as its own pratijna with its own hetu.
  2. Honestly check: is this genuine satpratipaksha (both survive a real vyapti check), or is one option actually weaker and you've just been avoiding admitting it (in which case, this isn't a tie — go back to Lesson 5's method instead)?
  3. If it's genuinely tied, run the reversibility check: one-way door or two-way door?
  4. Design one concrete tiebreaker action you can actually take in the next week — a small real trial for a two-way door, or a specific, apta-qualified conversation for a one-way door.

Check your work: if your "tiebreaker" is another round of thinking about it rather than an action that produces new information from outside your own head, it isn't a tiebreaker yet — go back and make it concrete.
