What You Actually Know About Someone

You can perceive what someone did. You can never perceive why. Almost every relationship conflict starts with those two things getting fused into one.

Everything in this course has been building toward a distinction that matters more in relationships than almost anywhere else: what you directly observed, and what you inferred from it. In this domain specifically, the two arrive as a single, seamless experience — nobody experiences "they didn't reply" and "they're upset with me" as two separate claims. It happens as one perception. It isn't one.

The category error, named precisely

You can perceive someone's words and actions — that's genuine pratyaksha, direct observation, exactly as reliable as it is anywhere else in this course. You can never perceive their intent. Intent is invisible. Every claim you make about why someone did something is anumana — inference — no matter how obvious it feels in the moment.

A framework built from what you already have Split every judgment about a person into five parts: what I know (pratyaksha — what was actually said or done), what I infer (anumana — my read on why), what I don't know (the honest gap between the two), what I should ask (converting the inference into real testimony, from the one person with direct access to it), and what action is appropriate (nigamana, sized to what's actually been verified).

Why this domain is especially error-prone

You've already met this exact failure twice — "he didn't reply, so he's upset" showed up in Lessons 3, 4, and 5 as the running example for a broken inference. That wasn't a coincidence. Intent-inferences about people are unusually rich in savyabhicara (Lesson 4): almost any single visible behavior — silence, a short reply, a missed invitation, a flat tone — is genuinely, constantly compatible with a dozen different intentions, most of them having nothing to do with you. The sign doesn't discriminate, which is exactly what makes it such an unreliable hetu, no matter how certain it feels.

The move unique to this domain: you can just ask

A rock doesn't tell you why it fell. A person can tell you why they did something — which makes them a directly available apta source (Lesson 2) about the one thing you can never observe directly. This is the single biggest upgrade this lesson offers over the rest of the course: instead of running more internal five-limb checks on your own inference, convert it into shabda by actually asking — turning a guess about an unobservable into testimony from the only witness who has direct access to it.

Asking well still has to clear the apta test:

  • Do they actually know? Mostly true of someone's own intent — though people aren't always fully self-transparent either, so treat the answer as strong evidence, not infallible certainty.
  • Will they be honest? Depends heavily on how you ask. An accusatory framing ("why are you mad at me?") invites a reflexive, defensive "I'm not" whether or not it's true.
  • Will it communicate clearly? A specific, answerable question gets a specific answer. A vague one gets a vague, socially safe one.

Worked example

What I know: I wasn't invited to a friend's gathering. I saw photos of it afterward.

What I infer: "They don't want me around anymore" / "the friendship is fading."

Checked against the pattern: is "not invited" invariably connected to "doesn't want me around"? Counter-examples arrive immediately — a headcount cap, an assumption I was busy, an invite sent through a channel I don't check, a plan that wasn't fully theirs to control. Savyabhicara, same as the reply-delay example from earlier lessons.

What I don't know: the actual reason — not yet, and guessing further doesn't produce it.

What I should ask: specific, non-accusatory, genuinely curious — "Hey, saw the photos from your thing, looked fun! Was that a small group, or did I miss an invite somewhere?" A real question, not a loaded one.

What action is appropriate: nothing drastic before the answer arrives. If it turns out to be a genuine, patterned exclusion, that's real information worth acting on. If it's a headcount cap or a missed message, the entire original inference — and whatever cooling-off already started happening internally — was resolving a problem that never existed.

Retrieval check

What can you actually perceive about another person's intent?

Nothing directly — intent is always inferred, never observed.

Why are intent-inferences especially prone to savyabhicara?

One visible behavior fits many different real intentions.

What makes "asking" a uniquely available move in this domain?

The person has direct apta access to their own intent.

What should determine the size of your response to someone's behavior?

What's actually been verified — not how strong the feeling is.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Split a real, unresolved read on someone

  1. Pick a real situation with someone in your life where you've formed a read on their intent that's still unresolved.
  2. Write all five categories out: what you know, what you infer, what you don't know, what you should ask (write the actual question, word for word, non-accusatory and specific), and what action is currently appropriate given only what's verified so far.
  3. Decide honestly whether you're actually going to ask it. If not, write down why — and notice whether the reason is good judgment (not worth the conversation) or avoidance (Lesson 8's territory).

Check your work: if your "appropriate action" already matches the full weight of your inference rather than the smaller, provisional response the unverified state actually calls for, the framework hasn't done its job yet — scale the action back to match what's confirmed.
