How Inferences Go Wrong

Lesson 3 gave you one test — vyapti — for whether an inference holds. This lesson gives you names for the four most common ways it fails, so you can recognize the pattern instantly instead of re-deriving it every time.

Nyaya logicians didn't just say "check for vyapti" and stop there. They catalogued the recurring, recognizable ways reasoning breaks — called hetvabhasa (हेत्वाभास, literally "the appearance of a reason" — something that looks like a valid hetu but isn't). The full classical list has five categories with several subtypes each; this lesson covers the four that map onto genuinely distinct, everyday reasoning failures. The fifth is more technical and is skipped here on purpose — you won't need it to catch most of your own bad inferences.

1. Savyabhicara — the inconstant sign

व्यभिचार. Plain meaning: the sign shows up both when the conclusion is true and when it's false. It isn't really evidence at all — it's noise that happens to coexist with the conclusion sometimes.

This is exactly what broke the classical example from Lesson 3: "all knowable things are fiery, the hill is knowable, therefore fiery." Being knowable shows up constantly alongside things that aren't fiery (water, air, anything). The sign doesn't discriminate — so it proves nothing.

Modern version: "It worked out well, so it was a good decision." A good outcome shows up after both good decisions and bad decisions that got lucky. The sign (good outcome) doesn't reliably distinguish good decisions from lucky bad ones — it's inconstant.

Detection question: does this sign ever show up when the conclusion is false?

2. Viruddha — the contradictory sign

विरुद्ध. Plain meaning: the reason you're citing doesn't just fail to prove your conclusion — it actually argues for the opposite one.

Classical example: "Sound is eternal, because it is produced." Produced things are, if anything, generally impermanent — so "being produced" is evidence against eternity, not for it. The reasoner grabbed a fact and pointed it the wrong direction.

Modern version: "He's being so calm about this, he must not care." Calm under pressure is at least as often a sign of someone working hard to stay in control because they care — the same fact could argue the opposite conclusion just as easily.

Detection question: if I flipped my conclusion, would this exact same reason actually support the flip better?

3. Satpratipaksha — the counterbalanced reason

सत्प्रतिपक्ष. Plain meaning: an equally strong reason exists on the other side. Not "someone disagrees with me" — a reason of comparable weight, pointing the opposite way. The honest state here is a stalemate, not a proof.

Classical example: one side argues "sound is eternal, because it's audible"; the other argues "sound is non-eternal, because it's produced." Both reasons are genuinely structured arguments, not assertions — and they cancel each other out until better evidence arrives.

Modern version: two studies, both methodologically reasonable, reach opposite conclusions about whether a habit is good for you. Picking whichever one you read first — or whichever one you wanted to be true — isn't reasoning. It's just landing wherever momentum dropped you.

Detection question: is there a reason of comparable strength on the other side that I'm not weighing, because I already believe my side?

What to do about it: don't force a pick. Suspend judgment and go looking for a tiebreaker — ideally something closer to direct perception (Lesson 2's pratyaksha) than another layer of inference.

4. Badhita — contradicted by something stronger

बाधित. Plain meaning: the conclusion is directly contradicted by something you can perceive, or by a more reliable pramana — and you're holding onto the inference anyway.

Classical example: "Fire is cold, because it is a substance." You can just touch fire. Direct perception refutes the conclusion outright; no amount of clever reasoning about "substances" rescues it.

Modern version: "The relationship must be fine — we never fight." Meanwhile you can directly observe growing distance, shorter conversations, less eye contact. The inference ("no fights" implies "fine") is being defended by reasoning around what's directly observable, instead of updating in light of it.

Detection question: is there something I can observe directly, right now, that already contradicts this conclusion — and am I reasoning around it instead of looking at it?

This is where Lesson 2's four pramanas connect back up: when a direct perception (pratyaksha) conflicts with an inference (anumana), the inference is the one that breaks. Direct observation outranks a chain of reasoning built to get around it.

Retrieval check

What does hetvabhasa mean?

Something that looks like a valid reason but isn't.

Which hetvabhasa names a sign that shows up whether the conclusion is true or false?

Savyabhicara — the inconstant, non-discriminating sign.

Which hetvabhasa names a reason that actually supports the opposite conclusion?

Viruddha — the contradictory sign, pointing the wrong way.

When a direct perception contradicts an inference, which one wins?

Perception — the inference is treated as badhita, broken.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Diagnose three inferences

  1. Gather three inferences — reuse ones from earlier drills, or pick fresh ones from something you currently believe.
  2. For each, run all four questions in order: Does the sign ever show up when the conclusion is false (savyabhicara)? Could the same reason argue the opposite conclusion (viruddha)? Is there a comparably strong reason on the other side I'm not weighing (satpratipaksha)? Is something directly observable already contradicting this, that I'm arguing around (badhita)?
  3. Name which hetvabhasa, if any, applies to each. It's fine — expected, even — if one applies to more than one of your three.
  4. If none apply after a genuine attempt to break it, the inference has survived a real test, not just your confidence in it.

Check your work: "he didn't reply, so he's upset" from Lesson 3 — which hetvabhasa does it match? (It's savyabhicara: not replying shows up constantly among people who aren't upset at all.)
