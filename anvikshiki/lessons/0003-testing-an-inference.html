Testing an Inference

Lesson 2 taught you to spot when a belief rests on anumana — inference from a sign. This lesson gives you the one test that separates a real inference from a guess wearing its clothes.

"He didn't reply right away, so he must be upset with me." "I felt great after taking it, so it must work." "It's knowable, so it must matter." All three have the shape of an inference — sign, therefore conclusion. Only one of the three (spoiler: none of these three, actually) will survive the test in this lesson. The test isn't about how confident the reasoning feels. It's about one specific, checkable relationship called vyapti.

Vyapti: the one thing that makes an inference valid

Vyapti (व्याप्ति, "pervasion" or "invariable concomitance") is the relationship between a sign (hetu) and the thing it's meant to prove (sadhya, साध्य) when the sign is never present without the thing it proves — no exceptions, not "usually," not "in my experience." Smoke and fire have this relationship: there is no real case of smoke occurring with no fire or heat source behind it anywhere. That's what makes "there's smoke, therefore there's fire" a valid inference rather than a hopeful guess.

Compare a classical inference that looks the same shape but fails:

Fails vyapti: "All knowable things are fiery. The hill is knowable. Therefore the hill is fiery." Structurally identical to the smoke-fire case — sign, therefore conclusion. But is being knowable ever present without being fiery? Constantly — water is knowable and not fiery. Vyapti fails, so the conclusion doesn't follow, no matter how tidy the sentence sounds.

The test, in one question you can actually use: is there a real case where the sign is present but the conclusion is false? If you can find even one, vyapti fails, and the inference collapses — regardless of how many times the sign-and-conclusion have shown up together before.

Applied to daily life "He didn't reply right away, so he must be upset with me" — is there ever a real case of someone not replying right away who isn't upset? Obviously: busy, phone dead, didn't see it, thinking about the reply. Vyapti fails immediately. This isn't a small technicality — it's the difference between an actual inference and a story you told yourself that happens to use the word "because."

The five-step structure for laying an inference out

Nyaya didn't just define vyapti — it gave a five-step structure for stating an inference so that the vyapti claim is forced into the open, where it can be checked instead of assumed. Using the classical example:

StepSanskritWhat it doesExample
1. ClaimPratijnaState what you're claiming, about this specific case."There is fire on the hill."
2. ReasonHetuState the sign you're reasoning from."...because there is smoke there."
3. Rule + exampleUdaharanaState the general rule you're relying on, with a supporting case and, ideally, a contrasting one."Wherever there's smoke, there's fire — like a kitchen hearth. Not like a lake, where mist rises with no fire."
4. ApplicationUpanayaConfirm this specific case actually has the sign."This hill does have smoke."
5. ConclusionNigamanaRestate the conclusion, now checked rather than assumed."Therefore, there is fire on the hill."

Step 3 is where almost every everyday "inference" quietly falls apart, because people skip straight from the sign (step 2) to the conclusion (step 5) without ever stating the general rule out loud. "He didn't reply, so he's upset" jumps from hetu to nigamana with no udaharana in between — no stated rule, no example, no counter-example considered. Forced to fill in step 3 — "whenever someone doesn't reply right away, they're upset, like [supporting case] — not like [counter-case]" — the rule usually won't survive being said out loud. That's the entire mechanism this lesson is teaching you to use.

Retrieval check

What does vyapti mean?

The sign is never present without the thing it proves.

If you find one real case where the sign is present but the conclusion is false, what happens to the inference?

Vyapti fails, and the inference no longer holds.

Which of the five steps do people skip most often in everyday reasoning?

Udaharana — stating the general rule and example.

What is sadhya?

The thing being proved — the conclusion of the inference.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Lay out one of your own inferences, full structure

  1. Go back to the belief you classified as anumana in Lesson 2's drill (or pick a fresh one — any conclusion you've drawn from a sign rather than seeing it directly).
  2. Write all five steps out, in full sentences: pratijna, hetu, udaharana (rule + supporting example + a genuine attempt at a counter-example), upanaya, nigamana.
  3. Stare specifically at your udaharana. Try hard to find a real counter-example — a case where your hetu was true but your sadhya wasn't. Don't soften the search; the whole point is to try to break it.
  4. If you find a counter-example, the inference fails vyapti. That doesn't mean the conclusion is false — it means you don't actually have grounds for it yet, and you now know exactly what kind of evidence would fix that.

Check your work: if you couldn't find any counter-example after genuinely trying, your inference likely survives — but notice whether you searched hard or just declared victory quickly. The value of this drill is in the searching, not the verdict.
