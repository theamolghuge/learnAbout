Confirmation Bias Is a Skipped Step

Modern cognitive science named some of the same failures Nyaya named 2,000 years earlier — and named at least one that has no classical counterpart at all. Both facts are worth knowing.

This lesson pairs three well-documented modern biases against what you already have. Two of them turn out to be precise modern names for a classical hetvabhasa. The third doesn't map onto anything from Lessons 1-8 — and that's said plainly here rather than forced, because inventing a parallel that isn't real would undercut the whole point of this course.

Confirmation bias

The tendency to search for, notice, and remember information that confirms what you already believe, while underweighting or not bothering to look for what would disconfirm it. Commonly traced to psychologist Peter Wason's 1960 rule-discovery experiments, where subjects given a number sequence and asked to guess the rule behind it overwhelmingly tested only guesses that would confirm their first hypothesis, rarely one designed to break it.

Daily version: re-reading a friend's text for the fifth time, noticing only the phrases that support "they're upset with me," while a perfectly ordinary explanation for the tone sits in the same message, unnoticed.

The classical parallel — this one is exact: Lesson 3's vyapti test requires actively hunting for a counter-example — a real case where the sign is present but the conclusion false. Confirmation bias is the psychological default that does the opposite: hunting only for cases where sign and conclusion show up together, which proves nothing about vyapti at all. Finding ten confirming cases and zero disconfirming ones, when you never actually looked for a disconfirming one, isn't evidence of vyapti — it's evidence you didn't run the test.

Detection question: have I actually looked for a case that would prove me wrong, or only for cases that prove me right?

Corrective: this is why Lesson 5's drill instructions ask you to argue the prosecution case against your own conclusion before accepting it — that instruction exists specifically to counter this bias, not as an arbitrary extra step.

Availability heuristic

Judging how common or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind, rather than by its actual frequency. Documented by Tversky and Kahneman in their landmark 1974 paper on judgment under uncertainty.

Daily version: overestimating the risk of a shark attack right after a news story about one, while underestimating the risk of heart disease — far more common, but far less vivid and far less recently reported.

The classical parallel: this is savyabhicara from Lesson 4 — the inconstant sign. "Comes to mind easily" is the sign; "actually common" is the conclusion. Does ease of recall always track true frequency? No — vivid, recent, or heavily reported events are recalled easily whether or not they're actually common. The sign shows up whether the conclusion is true or false, which is the textbook definition of an inconstant sign.

Detection question: am I estimating this from actual data, or from whatever example is easiest to remember right now?

Corrective: before estimating a frequency or probability, ask what the real base rate is — or admit you don't know it — instead of substituting the most memorable example as a stand-in for data.

Anchoring — the honest non-parallel

In the same 1974 paper, Tversky and Kahneman spun a rigged roulette wheel in front of subjects, then asked them to estimate what percentage of United Nations members are African countries. Subjects who saw the wheel land on 10 guessed around 25%. Subjects who saw it land on 65 guessed around 45% — dragged toward a number they knew was random and irrelevant, just because they'd seen it moments before.

Why this one doesn't get a hetvabhasa Every hetvabhasa in Lesson 4 is a failure in the structure of an inference — a bad sign, a bad rule, a bad application. Anchoring isn't an inference at all. There's no sign, no claimed rule, nothing to run vyapti on — it's a numerical estimate getting physically dragged toward an arbitrary starting number, before any reasoning happens. Forcing this into the hetvabhasa framework just to keep a tidy parallel running would be exactly the kind of overreach this course is trying to avoid. It's a real and well-documented bias. It's just not this tradition's bias to have already named.

Detection question: did I just hear a number — a price, a first offer, someone else's estimate — right before forming my own?

Corrective: generate your own independent estimate before seeing anyone else's number, whenever the decision actually matters.

Retrieval check

Which classical test does confirmation bias undermine by skipping it?

Vyapti — the active hunt for a counter-example.

Which hetvabhasa parallels the availability heuristic, and why?

Savyabhicara — ease of recall doesn't track true frequency.

Why doesn't anchoring get paired with a hetvabhasa?

It isn't an inference at all — nothing structural to compare.

What's the corrective for confirmation bias, already built into this course?

Argue the prosecution case before accepting a conclusion.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Two separate checks

  1. Confirmation bias: pick a prediction or belief you're currently fairly confident about. List the evidence you've noticed supporting it. Then spend five real minutes actively hunting for evidence against it — not imagining what a skeptic might say, actually looking.
  2. Anchoring: think of a number you estimated recently — a price you'd pay, a timeline, a probability. Was any number mentioned to you shortly before you formed your own estimate? If so, would your estimate have looked different arriving in the opposite order?

Check your work: if step 1 turned up zero counter-evidence after a genuine five-minute search, that's a legitimate result — but check whether you actually searched somewhere new (a different source, someone who disagrees) or just re-read the same confirming material more slowly.
