Before You Compare Options

Most bad decisions aren't bad comparisons. They're comparisons run on an objective nobody checked, against a constraint nobody verified, ignoring an alternative nobody named.

Lesson 12 assumed you already had two well-formed options and a genuine tie between them. Most decisions go wrong earlier than that — in how the objective, the constraints, and the alternatives were framed in the first place. Three checks, all built from tools you already have, catch most of it before any comparison starts.

1. The objective is self-testimony too

"I want to advance my career." "I just want us to be happy." Every stated objective is a claim about what you want — which, per Lesson 7, is self-testimony, subject to the same apta test as any other shabda: do you actually know this is your objective, or is it aspirational, inherited, or the answer that sounds right rather than the one that's true? A decision optimized perfectly against the wrong objective is still a bad decision — it just fails somewhere you never checked.

Unchecked objective: deciding between two job offers by comparing salary and title, when the honest objective — checked against what Lesson 7's calendar-and-wallet test would actually reveal — is more about time with family than either salary or title.

2. Most constraints were never verified

"I don't have time." "That's not realistic for someone like me." "We can't afford that." Some constraints are real — physically, legally, or financially checkable, direct pratyaksha. Many more are exactly what Lesson 1's very first drill was built to catch: a belief held with no actual hetu, never examined, that's been quietly acting as a hard boundary on the decision anyway.

Detection question: if I actually checked this constraint against direct evidence right now — the calendar, the account balance, the actual policy — would it hold, or has it just never been checked?

Checked constraint: "I don't have time" turns into "here is my actual calendar for the next four weeks" — sometimes confirming the constraint is real, sometimes revealing six hours a week that were never actually accounted for.

3. Opportunity cost is the counter-example you didn't hunt for

Evaluating one option by asking only "is this good?" without ever concretely stating what the next-best alternative would have given you is the decision-making version of skipping the counter-example search in udaharana (Lesson 3). "Is this a good use of my time?" sounds answerable in isolation — it isn't, until you've named the specific alternative use of that same time and stated, concretely, what it would have produced.

Detection question: what, specifically, would I be doing instead — not "something else," but the actual next-best concrete alternative — and what would that have gotten me?

Worked example

Decision: whether to take on a demanding new project at work.

Objective, checked: "I want to advance my career" was the first-draft answer. Running the apta test on it: does the source (me) actually know this, or is it what sounds ambitious to say? Checking against Lesson 7's revealed-preference evidence — recent choices have consistently favored stability and time outside work over visibility projects — reveals the honest objective is closer to "steady growth without sacrificing evenings," a more precise, checkable claim than the first draft.

Constraint, checked: "I don't have the bandwidth" — checked against the actual calendar. Partially real (evenings are genuinely full); partially assumed (two mid-day blocks marked "flexible" were never actually accounted for).

Opportunity cost, named: the concrete alternative use of that time isn't "free time" in the abstract — it's finishing a specific certification that directly serves the checked objective (steady growth) better than the visibility project does.

Run through these three checks, the decision changes shape entirely — not because the project is bad, but because the original framing (advance career, no clear constraint check, no named alternative) was answering a question nobody had actually verified.

Retrieval check

What kind of claim is a stated decision objective?

Self-testimony — shabda, subject to the apta test.

What's the detection question for an unverified constraint?

Would it hold if checked against direct evidence now?

What does evaluating an option without naming the alternative skip?

The counter-example search — udaharana, done properly.

Drill (do this now — ~20 minutes)

Run all three checks on one real decision

  1. Pick a decision you're facing — the same one from Lesson 12's drill works well here.
  2. Write your stated objective, then apta-test it against Lesson 7's revealed-preference evidence: does recent actual behavior support this being the real objective, or does it point somewhere else?
  3. List every constraint you've been treating as fixed. For each, check it against direct evidence — calendar, balance, an actual rule — and mark it real or unverified.
  4. Name the specific, concrete next-best alternative for whatever resource this decision consumes (time, money, attention), and what it would concretely produce.

Check your work: if every constraint came back "real" and the objective needed no adjustment, that's a legitimate result — but make sure you actually pulled up the calendar or the number, rather than reasoning about it from memory.
