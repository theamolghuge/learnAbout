Before You Act

Every tool in this course was building toward one thing: not winning an argument, but becoming someone whose default action is examined.

Kautilya's own line about Anvikshiki, from Lesson 1, was never really about belief in isolation — it said the discipline "bestows excellence of foresight, speech, and action." This lesson closes the loop from believing to acting to becoming, using nothing but tools you already have.

The chain

Belief → Intention → Decision → Action → Habit → Character → Consequences

A belief examined once, well, is a single good judgment. A belief examined the same way, every time, for years, is what Kautilya meant by character. Nothing about the mechanism changes between the two — only the repetition does.

The pre-action checklist

Eight questions, each one a direct application of a tool you already have:

QuestionHow you actually check it
Is it based on reality? Has the belief driving this survived the full five-limb method (Lesson 5) — or does it just feel true?
Is my reasoning sound? Checked for hetvabhasa (Lesson 4) and the modern biases from Lessons 10, 11, and 15?
What are my motives? Run viveka (Lesson 6) — is this ahamkara's want, or something buddhi actually endorses on reflection? Apta-test the stated motive (Lesson 7).
Who is affected? Run Lesson 16's know/infer/don't-know framework for everyone touched, not just yourself.
What are the likely consequences? First- and second-order effects, worst/best/likely case (Lesson 14).
What am I ignoring? The badhita check (Lesson 8) — is there direct evidence I'm reasoning around instead of confronting?
Would I endorse this after reflection? From the witness stance viveka creates — not the fused, immediate-impulse answer.
What happens if I repeat this for years? The one genuinely new move — see below.

The new move: run the method on the pattern, not the instance

Every other question in this checklist evaluates a single action. This one asks you to treat repetition itself as the pratijna: "if I handle situations like this the same way, every time, for years — " and run Lesson 14's worst/best/likely case thinking on that claim, not on today's isolated decision. A single white lie is a small thing to evaluate. "I am someone who defaults to comfortable dishonesty when things get hard" is a different claim entirely, evaluated on the same evidence. This is the literal mechanism behind Kautilya's claim that the discipline builds character, not just correct individual judgments — character is what repetition of the same examined (or unexamined) pattern produces.

Worked example

Situation: deciding whether to tell a close friend a difficult truth, or stay quiet to avoid conflict.

Based on reality: checked against what actually happened, not an assumption about how they'll react.

Reasoning sound: "it's not my place to say anything" — checked for hetvabhasa; is this a real principle or does it collapse under a counter-example (would I want a friend to tell me)?

Motives: viveka reveals staying silent is less about respecting their autonomy and more about avoiding an uncomfortable ten minutes — ahamkara's comfort, not a genuinely endorsed value.

Who's affected: the friend directly; possibly others depending on the situation.

Consequences: first-order — an uncomfortable conversation now. Second-order, if silent instead — eroded trust later if they learn I knew and said nothing.

What I'm ignoring: direct evidence (badhita) that they already sense something is off and are hurting from the uncertainty, not from the eventual truth itself.

Would I endorse it reflectively: from the witness stance, yes — the honest version serves the friendship; the comfortable version serves only the next ten minutes.

Repeated for years: a pattern of choosing comfortable silence compounds into a friendship built on things unsaid, and a character that defaults to avoidance whenever honesty costs something. Evaluated as a pattern, not an instance, the choice is clearer than it was in isolation.

Retrieval check

What does repeated, examined judgment become, according to Kautilya's own claim?

Character — the accumulated pattern of the choices.

Which tool checks "what are my motives" specifically?

Viveka — separating ahamkara's want from buddhi's endorsement.

What's the genuinely new move this lesson adds?

Running the method on the repeated pattern, not one instance.

Which lesson's framework checks "who is affected"?

Lesson 16 — the know/infer/don't-know framework.

Drill (do this now — ~20 minutes)

Run the full checklist on a real, current action

  1. Pick something you're actually deciding whether to do or say in the near future — not a hypothetical.
  2. Answer all eight questions in the table, in full sentences, using the specific tool each one points to rather than a generic gut answer.
  3. Write the "repeated for years" version explicitly — the pattern-level claim, not just the one-off.
  4. Decide, and write the decision down alongside the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

Check your work: if the "repeated for years" answer changed your decision from what the single-instance answer suggested, that's the checklist doing exactly what it's for — the pattern-level view is usually the more honest one.
