Name Your Pramana

Lesson 1 asked "what's your hetu?" This lesson asks the sharper question: what kind of reason is it — and does it actually qualify?

Not all reasons are equal. "I saw it happen," "everyone knows that's true," "it's obviously like the other thing," and "my friend told me" are four completely different kinds of reason, with different failure modes. Classical Indian epistemology built a precise vocabulary for exactly this distinction, called pramana (प्रमाण) — literally "means of correct knowing," from pra- ("forth, correctly") + mā ("to measure, to know"). A pramana isn't just "a reason" — it's a reliable process that, used properly, cannot produce a false belief. That's a high bar, and most of what people call "reasons" don't actually clear it.

The four pramanas (Nyaya's list)

The Nyaya school — the branch of Anvikshiki that specialized in reasoning, introduced in Lesson 1 — recognized four:

PramanaWhat it isClassical example
Pratyaksha
प्रत्यक्ष
perception		 Direct sensory contact with something. The most basic pramana — everyone agrees this one counts. You see a pot sitting on a table.
Anumana
अनुमान
inference		 Knowing something you can't directly perceive, by way of a sign reliably connected to it. You see smoke rising from a hill and conclude there's fire — you didn't see the fire, but smoke is a reliable sign of it.
Upamana
उपमान
comparison / analogy		 Learning what an unfamiliar thing is by its stated similarity to something familiar. Someone tells you "a gavaya is like a cow." Later, you see an animal in the wild and recognize it as a gavaya because it resembles a cow the way you were told.
Shabda
शब्द
testimony		 Knowing something because a trustworthy person told you, in words you understood. Someone who was there tells you, "it rained upstream." You weren't there and didn't infer it — you were simply told, by someone qualified to know.
Why this matters more than it sounds like it should Every claim you accept — from a friend's story to a news headline to your own gut feeling — is actually resting on one of these four, or on something dressed up to look like one of these four without qualifying. Naming which one forces you to check whether it actually qualifies, instead of just feeling convinced.

Not every school agreed — and that's useful

Recall from Lesson 1 that Lokayata was the skeptical, materialist school grouped under Anvikshiki. Its skepticism wasn't limited to the soul — it extended to method: Lokayata accepted only perception as a genuine pramana, distrusting inference and testimony as too easy to fake or get wrong. Other schools went the other way — Mimamsa added more pramanas beyond Nyaya's four. There's no single "correct" number that all of classical India agreed on; the table below situates a few positions.

SchoolPramanas accepted
Lokayata (Carvaka)Perception only — rejects inference as unreliable
VaisheshikaPerception, inference (testimony's status debated)
NyayaPerception, inference, comparison, testimony
MimamsaNyaya's four, plus presumption and non-perception

The useful takeaway isn't "which school was right" — it's that even within a tradition built entirely around examined reasoning, serious thinkers disagreed about which sources of knowledge to trust at all. Being rigorous doesn't mean reaching the same conclusion as everyone else; it means being honest about which pramana you're actually relying on.

The one that fails silently: shabda (testimony)

Most of what you believe about the world — most of the time — isn't perception or inference. It's testimony: someone told you. That makes it the pramana most worth scrutinizing, because it's also the easiest to fake. Nyaya set a real bar for when testimony counts as a genuine pramana: the speaker must be an apta — a trustworthy source — meaning they:

  1. Actually know the thing themselves (through their own perception, inference, or reliable prior testimony — not a guess)
  2. Intend to communicate honestly, without deceiving you
  3. Communicate it clearly enough that you actually understand what's being claimed

A claim that fails any one of these three isn't really testimony in the pramana sense — it's noise wearing testimony's clothes. "Everyone says X" usually fails test 1 (does "everyone" actually know, or are they repeating each other?). A confident scammer fails test 2. A vague rumor fails test 3.

Looks like shabda, isn't: "My cousin's friend read that this supplement cures headaches." Unknown whether the original source ever actually knew this (test 1), and the claim has passed through enough retellings that you can't verify test 3.
Actually is shabda: "My doctor, who examined my chart and my bloodwork, told me to stop taking this." A specific, qualified source, examined the relevant evidence herself, communicated directly.

Drill (do this now — ~12 minutes)

Classify four real claims

  1. Pick four things you currently believe — can be the three beliefs from Lesson 1's drill plus one new one, or four fresh ones. Include at least one you picked up from news, social media, or something someone told you.
  2. For each, name which pramana it actually rests on: pratyaksha, anumana, upamana, or shabda. If none fit cleanly, write "unclear" — that's a real and useful answer.
  3. For any claim resting on shabda, run the three-part apta check: did the source actually know it themselves? Were they being honest? Did you actually understand what was claimed, precisely? Mark pass/fail on each.
  4. For any claim resting on anumana, name the sign you're reasoning from (like smoke) and the thing you're concluding (like fire). Ask: is the sign actually reliably connected to the conclusion, or could the sign be present without the conclusion being true?

Check your work: if a belief marked "shabda" fails even one apta test, you're holding a belief with a source you haven't actually verified. That's not necessarily wrong — but you now know it's provisional, which is the whole point.

Retrieval check
