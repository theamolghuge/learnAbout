An Evaluation Protocol for Anything You Read

You already have every tool this needs. Modern media adds exactly one new wrinkle: trust cues that are manufactured, not earned.

News, social feeds, and AI-generated text all present claims dressed up to look more verified than they are — a photo looks like pratyaksha, a share count looks like consensus, fluent AI-generated prose looks like it came from somewhere that checked. This lesson assembles your existing toolkit into one compact protocol, tuned specifically for that manufactured layer.

What's actually new here

The apta test (Lesson 2) already checks whether a source knows the claim and means to be honest. Modern information environments add a layer the classical test didn't need to name explicitly: incentive structure. A platform, publisher, or algorithm can amplify a claim not because it's true, but because outrage, novelty, or engagement gets rewarded regardless of accuracy — which can corrupt what reaches you even when no single person involved is lying outright. "What does this source, or this platform, gain if I believe and share this?" is a direct extension of the apta test's honesty check, sized for an environment with automated amplification in it.

The protocol

QuestionWhat it's really asking
Who is making this claim? Trace to the actual originating source — not the account that shared it to you (Lesson 11's appeal-to-popularity check).
What exactly is being claimed? State it precisely, in your own words. Vagueness is often doing work — a claim too fuzzy to test can't be checked, which is sometimes the point.
What pramana is this, really? A photo or video is closer to shabda (someone chose to capture, frame, and share it) than pure pratyaksha. Don't grant it perception's full authority.
What evidence supports it? If it's anumana, run the vyapti check (Lesson 3). If it's shabda, run the full apta test.
What evidence would weaken it? Actually go look — this is Lesson 10's corrective, applied here specifically.
What incentives exist? What does the source, platform, or algorithm gain if you believe and share this — regardless of whether it's true?
What alternative explanations exist? The counter-rule search from udaharana (Lesson 3) — is there a mundane explanation that fits the same evidence?
How confident should I be? State a range, not a binary — and check it against Lesson 15's overconfidence calibration.

A special case: AI-generated content

Fluent, confident-sounding text from an AI system has no inherent connection to any of the four pramanas unless it's grounded in cited, checkable sources. Run the apta test on the system the same way you would a person: does it actually have verified access to this specific fact, or is it producing plausible-sounding text with no real claim to having checked? Treat unsourced AI output as untested testimony at best — worth exactly as much independent verification as an anonymous online claim, no more, regardless of how authoritative the prose sounds.

Worked example

Claim encountered: a viral post states "90% of people believe X," with no cited source, high engagement, no named study.

Who's making it: an account with no visible connection to any research body. What exactly is claimed: a specific statistic, stated with false precision. What pramana: presented as if it's established fact (shabda from a qualified source), but there's no traceable origin — it may not be shabda from anyone qualified at all, just a number that sounds plausible. Evidence for it: none cited. Evidence against it: a quick search finds no matching study; the number doesn't appear anywhere except reposts of this exact post. Incentives: a striking, shareable statistic drives engagement regardless of accuracy — that's the platform's incentive, not necessarily the poster's, but it explains why an unsourced number like this spreads either way. Alternative explanation: the number may be fabricated, badly misremembered from something else, or invented for engagement. Confidence: very low — treat as unverified until a real source appears, not as "probably roughly true."

Retrieval check

What new detection question does this lesson add beyond the apta test?

What incentives exist for this source or platform?

What pramana is a shared photo or video actually closer to?

Shabda — someone chose to capture and share it.

How should unsourced AI-generated claims be treated?

As untested testimony — no more trustworthy than that.

What should "how confident should I be" produce?

A calibrated range, not a binary belief or disbelief.

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Run the protocol on something from your actual feed

  1. Find a claim you encountered in the last day or two — news, social media, a statistic, anything you half-believed without checking.
  2. Run all eight questions from the table, writing a real answer to each, not a placeholder.
  3. Land on an explicit confidence range, and write what specific evidence would move that range up or down.

Check your work: if "who is making this claim" led straight to a verifiable, named, qualified source, that's a legitimate good result — but make sure you actually traced it there, rather than assuming the account that shared it to you was the origin.
