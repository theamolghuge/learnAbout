The Belief You Won't Check

"What am I avoiding?" is a question you can dodge as easily as anything else. This lesson gives you a way to find the answer without asking it directly.

Every tool so far in this course assumes you're willing to run the check: state the claim, name the pramana, hunt the counter-example, confirm the specific case. Avoidance is what happens when you're not willing — when a belief sits there, structurally protected from ever reaching upanaya, the step in Lesson 5's method where you'd actually confirm it against the specific case in front of you.

Naming the mechanism precisely

Recall badhita from Lesson 4: a conclusion directly contradicted by something you can observe, held onto anyway. Most avoided beliefs are exactly this, mid-collapse — you already have enough directly observable evidence (pratyaksha) to know the belief wouldn't survive being checked. Avoidance is the strategy of never running the check, so the belief never has to officially fail.

A working definition What you're avoiding is best found by asking: which beliefs would very likely fail badhita if I actually ran upanaya on them — and which have I arranged my attention to avoid checking? Not "what am I afraid of," which invites a story. This version invites a specific, checkable search.

Why asking directly doesn't work

"What am I avoiding?" asked straight out returns an answer generated by the same source that's been avoiding it. Per Lesson 7, self-testimony is shabda from a source — ahamkara — with a standing motive to keep the story flattering. Asking ahamkara to report on ahamkara's own blind spot is asking a source to fail its own apta test on command. It usually just produces a plausible-sounding non-answer, or a decoy that's uncomfortable but not the actual thing.

The better signal: watch the reaction, not the self-report

Instead of interrogating yourself directly, use pratyaksha the way Lesson 6 taught: observe the layer beneath ahamkara's narration. Specific, checkable signs that a belief is being avoided rather than examined:

  • A flash of bodily dread, tightness, or irritation when a specific topic comes up — before any thought has formed a sentence about it
  • Reflexively changing the subject, joking it off, or steering conversations away from one particular area
  • A task or conversation you keep intending to get to and never quite do, with no ordinary logistical explanation
  • Getting disproportionately defensive at a mild, even friendly, question in one specific area

Treat any of these as a hetu — a sign — in an inference: "this reaction is present, therefore there's likely a belief here that hasn't been checked against evidence." That's an anumana you can actually test, unlike a direct self-report that's asking the interested party to testify against itself.

Running the check once you've found the candidate

The technique isn't new — it's Lesson 5's method, aimed specifically at the belief the reaction pointed to, with special attention to the step avoidance always skips:

  1. Pratijna: state the belief plainly, in one sentence, no hedging.
  2. Hetu: what's it actually resting on — and be honest if the answer is "nothing, I've just never checked."
  3. Udaharana: if there's a rule underneath it, state it and look for a counter-example.
  4. Upanaya — the step that was being avoided: does the belief actually hold up against what you can directly observe right now, without flinching away partway through? This is the step that never got run before; running it fully, once, is the entire point of the lesson.
  5. Nigamana: accept, reject, or suspend with a named next step — same as always.
Stopping early: noticing the dread, naming the belief, and then drifting back to something easier before reaching upanaya. This is avoidance completing its usual move one step later than usual — noticing isn't the same as checking.
Completing it: noticing the dread about "the business is fine," naming the belief precisely, and then actually opening the numbers — running upanaya against the specific, current figures — instead of reasoning around them.

Retrieval check

Which hetvabhasa is most relevant to an avoided belief?

Badhita — already contradicted by observable evidence.

Why is asking "what am I avoiding?" directly unreliable?

Ahamkara is asked to testify against its own motive.

What kind of signal should you watch for instead?

Bodily reactions and behavior — dread, deflection, delay.

Which step of the five-limb method does avoidance specifically skip?

Upanaya — confirming the belief against the specific case.

Drill (do this now — ~20 minutes, plus noticing over the next day or two)

Find the signal, then run the check

  1. Over the next day or two, notice moments matching the four signs above — dread, deflection, chronic delay, disproportionate defensiveness. Write down exactly what topic or belief was nearby when it happened. Don't try to force one; wait for a real one.
  2. Once you have a candidate, name the belief in one plain sentence.
  3. Run the full five-limb check from this lesson, paying deliberate attention to actually completing upanaya — check it against the real, current, specific evidence, not a comfortable approximation of it.
  4. Write the nigamana honestly, even if it's "suspended, and here's exactly what I'd need to look at to resolve it."

Check your work: if the whole drill felt comfortable throughout, you likely picked a decoy belief instead of the one the original signal pointed to — the real one usually has at least a little resistance in the upanaya step, even when it's worth doing.
