Viveka: The Technique Behind Self-Inquiry

"What do I actually want?" is unanswerable while you're fused with the wanting. This lesson gives you the classical technique for getting enough distance to actually ask.

Lesson 1 mentioned that Kautilya grouped three schools under Anvikshiki: Samkhya, Yoga, and Lokayata. Samkhya's whole practical contribution to this course is a single, precise technique — not a belief you have to adopt, a move you can actually make — for examining your own mind instead of just being carried by it.

The problem it solves

Self-inquiry questions like "what am I actually avoiding?" or "where am I rationalizing?" only work if there's some distance between the part of you asking the question and the part of you generating the anger, the excuse, the craving. Without that distance, the "answer" you get is just the same impulse restating itself in more articulate words. Samkhya's technique, called viveka (विवेक, "discrimination" — the ability to correctly tell two things apart), exists specifically to create that distance.

Two things Samkhya says you're constantly conflating

Samkhya proposes a distinction between two categories:

  • Purusha (पुरुष) — the witness. Pure awareness. The thing that is aware of a thought, without itself being the thought.
  • Prakriti (प्रकृति) — everything that changes: your body, your senses, your emotions, your thoughts, your sense of "I," even your intellect and judgment. Everything you can notice happening is, by this definition, prakriti.

Samkhya's diagnosis of most human suffering is straightforward: you keep mistaking prakriti for purusha. You experience anger and think "I am angry" instead of "anger is happening, and I am aware of it." That collapse — content mistaken for self — is what makes an impulse feel like it has to be obeyed instead of examined.

Established vs. adopted That purusha and prakriti are two truly separate, metaphysically distinct realities — and that full separation from prakriti is the actual goal of a spiritual life (kaivalya, "aloneness" or liberation) — is Samkhya's substantive philosophical position, laid out classically in Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Samkhya-Karika (c. 350 CE). You don't have to adopt that metaphysical claim to use the technique below practically, the same way you don't need to believe in Freud's specific model of the unconscious to find "notice the gap between stimulus and response" useful. This lesson teaches the technique; the metaphysics is optional.

The layers, from grossest to subtlest

Samkhya maps several layers between raw sensation and the witness, worth knowing by name because later lessons will point back to them:

LayerWhat it does
Body, sensesRaw sensation — what you see, hear, feel physically.
Manas
मनस्		The mind that coordinates sense data and carries out the intellect's decisions — the "traffic controller."
Ahamkara
अहंकार		Literally "I-making" — the function that claims ownership: turns "there is anger" into "my anger," "there is an opinion" into "my opinion."
Buddhi
बुद्धि		Intellect — judgment, decision, the part that evaluates and concludes.
PurushaThe witness. Aware of all of the above; not identical to any of it.

Notice where ahamkara sits: it's the specific function responsible for the word "mine." Most self-inquiry gets stuck right there — at "my anger," "my fear," "my failure" — treated as identity instead of as something ahamkara just labeled.

The technique: three-part negation

The classical instruction, applied progressively from body up through intellect: for each layer, say — and actually mean —

"This is not me. It is not mine. I am not this."

Applied to a live example: anger arises after criticism.

  1. Body: heat in the chest, tight jaw. "This sensation is not me — it's happening, and I notice it."
  2. Manas/senses: replaying the exact words that were said. "This replaying is not me — it's a process running, and I notice it."
  3. Ahamkara: "How dare they — that's my reputation." "This claiming of 'mine' is not me — it's a function labeling something, and I notice it doing that."
  4. Buddhi: the judgment "I should say something sharp back." "This judgment is not me — it's a conclusion forming, and I notice it forming."

What's left after all four negations is not nothing — it's the noticing itself. That's the distance the rest of self-inquiry actually needs. You haven't suppressed the anger or pretended it isn't there; you've stopped being fused with it long enough to ask a real question about it instead of just executing it.

Why this comes before the other self-inquiry questions

"What do I actually want?" "What am I avoiding?" "Where am I rationalizing?" — every one of these questions, asked from inside full fusion with an impulse, just returns the impulse's own justification. Asked from the small distance viveka creates, they can actually be examined the way Lesson 5's method examines any other claim: what's the actual hetu behind "I want this," and does it survive a counter-example?

Retrieval check

What does viveka mean?

Discrimination — correctly telling two things apart.

What is the difference between purusha and prakriti?

Purusha is the witness; prakriti is everything that changes.

What does ahamkara literally mean, and what does it do?

"I-making" — it turns experience into "mine."

What is the three-part negation formula?

"This is not me. It is not mine. I am not this."

Drill (do this now — ~15 minutes)

Run viveka on something live

  1. Pick a real thought, emotion, or reaction you're having right now or had recently — the more charged, the more useful this drill is.
  2. Write down what's present at each layer: the raw sensation, what your mind keeps replaying, what ahamkara is claiming as "mine," what buddhi has already concluded.
  3. For each layer, write the sentence out fully: "This is not me. It is not mine. I am not this." Actually write it, don't just think it — the writing is what forces the distance.
  4. Once you've done all four, ask one self-inquiry question about the original situation — "what do I actually want here?" — and notice whether the answer that comes up now feels different from the first, fused answer you'd have given before doing this.

Check your work: if the post-viveka answer is identical to your first instinct, word for word, you likely rushed the negations. Go back and actually sit with each layer for a breath before writing the sentence.
