Glossary

Every technical and Sanskrit term used so far, in one place. This grows as new lessons introduce new terms — later lessons will link back here instead of re-explaining a term.

TermMeaning
Anvikshiki
आन्वीक्षिकी		 Literally "the science of looking-at-again" or "examination" — from the root anvīkṣ, to investigate, look into, or reflect upon. In Kautilya's Arthashastra, the fourth field of knowledge: the discipline of examining beliefs and claims by their reasons, rather than accepting them as asserted. See Lesson 1.
Atman
आत्मन्		 "Self" or "soul." One of the two things classical Anvikshiki examined — not just asserting what the self is, but examining the reasons offered for competing claims about it.
Hetu
हेतु		 "Reason" or "ground" — the justification offered for a claim. The second of the two things Anvikshiki examined. A belief without a hetu is dogma, not knowledge, in this tradition.
Trayi
त्रयी		 "The triad" — the three Vedas, treated by Kautilya as one of the four fields of knowledge (sacred/ritual knowledge: what is righteous and unrighteous).
Varta
वार्ता		 The science of economics — agriculture, cattle-keeping, and trade. One of Kautilya's four fields of knowledge (what creates and destroys wealth).
Dandaniti
दण्डनीति		 Literally "the policy of the rod" — the science of government and the legitimate use of coercive power. One of Kautilya's four fields of knowledge (what is expedient and inexpedient in rule).
Darshana
दर्शन		 Literally "a view" or "a seeing" — a philosophical school or system. The word later used for the "examine the self" branch that grew out of Anvikshiki.
Samkhya
सांख्य		 One of the three philosophical schools Kautilya grouped under Anvikshiki. Affirms the soul's existence and offers reasoned arguments for a dualist view of matter and consciousness.
Yoga
योग		 Here, a philosophical school (darshana) closely related to Samkhya — not the physical postures the word usually means in English today. One of the three schools Kautilya grouped under Anvikshiki.
Lokayata
लोकायत		 A materialist, skeptical school that denied the soul's existence and rejected claims not backed by direct perception. Grouped under Anvikshiki alongside Samkhya and Yoga — despite disagreeing with them on the conclusion — because it, too, argued from reasons.
Nyaya
न्याय		 Literally "rule" or "method" — the formal school of logic that later grew out of the "examine the reasons" branch of Anvikshiki. Systematized in Gautama's Nyaya Sutra and Vatsyayana's commentary, the Nyaya Bhashya.
Pramana
प्रमाण		 "Means of correct knowing" — a reliable process that, used properly, cannot produce a false belief. Nyaya recognized four: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. See Lesson 2.
Pratyaksha
प्रत्यक्ष		 Perception — knowledge from direct sensory contact. The most basic pramana; accepted by every school, including Lokayata, which accepted nothing else.
Anumana
अनुमान		 Inference — knowing something you can't directly perceive via a sign reliably connected to it (e.g. smoke as a sign of fire). See also hetu, the reason/sign itself.
Upamana
उपमान		 Comparison or analogy — learning what an unfamiliar thing is through its stated similarity to something familiar (the classical example: learning what a gavaya is by being told "it's like a cow").
Shabda
शब्द		 Testimony — knowledge from a trustworthy source's words. Only counts as a genuine pramana if the source actually knew the thing, meant to be honest, and communicated it clearly (the three-part apta test).
Apta
आप्त		 A "trustworthy source" — someone whose testimony can count as shabda. Must (1) actually know the claim through their own perception, inference, or reliable prior testimony, (2) intend to communicate honestly, and (3) communicate clearly enough to be understood.
Vyapti
व्याप्ति		 "Pervasion" or invariable concomitance — the relationship where a sign (hetu) is never present without the thing it proves (sadhya), with no exceptions. The single test for whether an inference is valid: can you find a real case where the sign holds but the conclusion doesn't? See Lesson 3.
Sadhya
साध्य		 "The thing to be proved" — the conclusion of an inference (in the smoke/fire example, the fire).
Pratijna
प्रतिज्ञा		 Step 1 of the five-membered Nyaya syllogism: stating the claim about this specific case ("There is fire on the hill").
Udaharana
उदाहरण		 Step 3 of the syllogism: stating the general rule (the vyapti claim) plus a supporting example and, ideally, a contrasting counter-example. The step most everyday reasoning skips.
Upanaya
उपनय		 Step 4 of the syllogism: confirming the specific case actually has the sign ("this hill does have smoke").
Nigamana
निगमन		 Step 5 of the syllogism: restating the conclusion, now that it has actually been checked rather than assumed.
Hetvabhasa
हेत्वाभास		 Literally "the appearance of a reason" — something that looks like a valid hetu but isn't. Nyaya's classical taxonomy of ways an inference fails. See Lesson 4.
Savyabhicara
व्यभिचार		 The "inconstant sign" hetvabhasa — the sign shows up whether the conclusion is true or false, so it doesn't actually discriminate anything.
Viruddha
विरुद्ध		 The "contradictory sign" hetvabhasa — the cited reason actually supports the opposite conclusion.
Satpratipaksha
सत्प्रतिपक्ष		 The "counterbalanced reason" hetvabhasa — an equally strong reason exists on the other side, so the honest state is a stalemate, not a proof.
Badhita
बाधित		 The "contradicted by something stronger" hetvabhasa — the conclusion is directly refuted by perception or a more reliable pramana, yet still held onto.
Pañcāvayava
पञ्चावयव		 "The five limbs" — the classical name for the five-step syllogism structure (pratijna, hetu, udaharana, upanaya, nigamana), used in this course as the name for the general-purpose inquiry method. See Lesson 5 and the method's quick-reference card.
Viveka
विवेक		 "Discrimination" — the Samkhya technique for correctly telling purusha (the witness) apart from prakriti (everything that changes: body, thoughts, emotions, ego, intellect). The distance-creating move behind all self-inquiry. See Lesson 6.
Purusha
पुरुष		 In Samkhya: pure awareness, the witness — aware of experience without being identical to it.
Prakriti
प्रकृति		 In Samkhya: everything that changes — matter, body, senses, mind, ego, intellect, emotions, thoughts.
Manas
मनस्		 The layer of mind that coordinates sense data and carries out the intellect's decisions.
Ahamkara
अहंकार		 Literally "I-making" — the function that converts experience into "mine" ("my anger," "my opinion"). Where most unexamined identity-fusion actually happens.
Buddhi
बुद्धि		 Intellect — the layer responsible for judgment, evaluation, and conclusion.
Kaivalya
कैवल्य		 "Aloneness" — Samkhya's term for liberation, the completed separation of purusha from prakriti. A substantive philosophical goal, not required to use viveka as a practical technique.
ABC model
modern, not Sanskrit		 Albert Ellis's 1956 framework (explicitly Stoic-derived): Activating event, Belief, Consequence — the belief, not the event, produces the emotional consequence. Used in Lesson 9 as a bridge into applying this course's tools to emotions.
Confirmation bias
modern, not Sanskrit		 Searching for, noticing, and remembering only what confirms an existing belief. Undermines vyapti-testing by skipping the counter-example search rather than by any classical hetvabhasa. See Lesson 10.
Availability heuristic
modern, not Sanskrit		 Judging frequency or likelihood by how easily examples come to mind. Parallels savyabhicara (the inconstant sign) — ease of recall doesn't invariably track true frequency.
Anchoring
modern, not Sanskrit		 An estimate gets pulled toward a recently seen number, even one known to be arbitrary or irrelevant. Deliberately not paired with a hetvabhasa in this course — it isn't a failure in the structure of an inference, so forcing a classical parallel would be dishonest.
Hasty generalization
modern, not Sanskrit		 A general rule drawn from too small or unrepresentative a sample. A corrupted udaharana — the rule was never actually tested against enough cases. See Lesson 11.
False cause
modern, not Sanskrit		 Concluding A causes B from mere sequence or correlation, with no stated mechanism and no ruling-out of coincidence or a third factor. Borrows vyapti's authority without doing vyapti's work.
Appeal to authority / popularity
modern, not Sanskrit		 Accepting a claim on a credentialed person's say-so outside their actual expertise, or because many people believe it without any of them independently verifying it. Both fail the apta test from Lesson 2.
One-way / two-way door
modern, not Sanskrit		 Jeff Bezos's terms (2015 Amazon shareholder letter) for irreversible/costly-to-reverse decisions versus reversible, cheap-to-try ones. Used in Lesson 12 to size a satpratipaksha tiebreaker.
Sunk cost
modern, not Sanskrit		 Continuing to invest based on past, unrecoverable investment rather than future value. A savyabhicara case — past investment doesn't discriminate between continuations worth making and ones that aren't. See Lesson 15.
Self-serving bias
modern, not Sanskrit		 Crediting yourself for successes, blaming circumstances for failures. Ahamkara (Lesson 6) doing its usual job — claiming what flatters, declining what doesn't.
Base-rate neglect
modern, not Sanskrit		 Ignoring an established general frequency in favor of one vivid, unrepresentative case. Hasty generalization (Lesson 11), approached from the opposite direction.
Overconfidence
modern, not Sanskrit		 Stated confidence exceeding actual accuracy. Like anchoring, deliberately not paired with a hetvabhasa — it's a calibration problem across many judgments, not a structural flaw in one inference.
Vada
वाद		 Genuine discussion aimed at ascertaining the truth — the only one of Nyaya's three debate types worth arguing in good faith. See Lesson 17.
Jalpa
जल्प		 Wrangling — debate aimed at winning by fair means or foul; tricks are considered in bounds.
Vitanda
वितण्डा		 Cavil — debate aimed at defeating the other side without even establishing one's own position.
Chala
छल		 Quibbling — deliberately misreading what someone said to derail the argument. Three kinds: vak-chala (verbal), upacara-chala (figurative), samanya-chala (generalization).
Jati
जाति		 An objection based on surface similarity or difference with no real connecting relation to the actual claim — close to modern "false equivalence."
Incentive check
modern, not Sanskrit		 What a source or platform gains if you believe and share a claim, regardless of whether it's true — a modern extension of the apta test's honesty check, sized for algorithmic amplification. See Lesson 18.
