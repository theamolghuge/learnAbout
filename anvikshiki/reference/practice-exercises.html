Leveled Practice Exercises

Ten levels, easiest to hardest. Attempt each exercise in writing before revealing the answer — the value is in the attempt, not the reading.

Level 1 — Observation

Someone walks into a room, sits down, doesn't make eye contact, and checks their phone twice in two minutes. Write only what you observed — no interpretation.

Observation: sat down, no eye contact, phone checked twice in two minutes. Everything else — bored, anxious, distracted, rude — is inference, not observation. If your list included any of those words, you wrote interpretation.

Level 2 — Questioning

A friend says "this never works out for me." Write three questions that would actually test the claim.

"What specifically didn't work out this time?" "How many times has this actually happened?" "What would working out have looked like?" Good questions convert a vague pattern-claim into something checkable.

Level 3 — Assumptions

"I can't ask for a raise, the company can't afford it right now." Name the hidden assumptions.

Assumes direct knowledge of company finances; assumes "can't afford it" and "won't grant it" are the same thing; assumes the present financial state predicts the near future.

Level 4 — Evidence

A headline says "study shows coffee prevents disease X." What do you need to know before treating this as strong evidence?

Who conducted it, sample size, correlation vs. causation, whether it's been replicated, the actual effect size, and who funded it.

Level 5 — Reasoning

"Every time I've wished for rain before a picnic, it rained. I must have some influence on the weather." Name what's wrong.

Confirmation bias plus hasty generalization — no search for the times a wish for rain didn't produce rain, no mechanism stated, no real vyapti.

Level 6 — Emotional situations

A one-line text from your boss — "can we talk tomorrow?" — triggers a spike of anxiety. Run the four-step protocol.

Feel it (let the spike be present). Name it ("this anxiety is not me, it is not mine"). Check it (the hetu for "I'm in trouble" is just ambiguity — savyabhicara, the sign fits both good and bad meetings). Then act (respond normally; ask for context only if it would genuinely help).

Level 7 — Decisions

Choosing between a cheaper apartment with a longer commute and a pricier one closer to work. Sketch objective, reversibility, and opportunity cost briefly.

Objective: check against revealed preference — has recent behavior favored money saved or time saved? Opportunity cost: name concretely what the extra commute time would otherwise buy. Reversibility: a lease is costly but not permanent to reverse — moderate stakes, not a one-way door.

Level 8 — Interpersonal conflict

A roommate keeps leaving dishes; resentment is building. Apply the know/infer/don't-know/ask/act framework.

Know: dishes left repeatedly. Infer: "they don't respect my space." Don't know: whether it's carelessness, different standards, or being overwhelmed. Ask: a specific, non-accusatory conversation about it. Act: sized to whatever the conversation actually reveals.

Level 9 — Self-deception

You've said you'll start exercising "next week" for two months running. Run the avoidance check.

Signal: the repeated delay itself, with no logistical explanation. Candidate avoided belief: something like "I'm worried I'll fail again and that will confirm something I don't want to face." Run upanaya honestly on that belief before deciding what to do about the exercise habit itself.

Level 10 — Complex real-world problem

You lead a small team. One member is underperforming; you've heard secondhand they're dealing with a family crisis. A major deadline is at risk. Two teammates have started quietly covering for them and are getting frustrated. What tools apply, and in what order?

No single correct answer — this tests integration. Roughly: verify the secondhand crisis claim's source before acting on it as fact (Lesson 2's apta test on secondhand shabda). Use Lesson 16's know/infer/ask split before confronting anyone. Use Lesson 19's "who is affected" — the covering teammates matter as much as the underperforming one. Use Lesson 14's second-order effects on both "say something" and "stay silent." If frustration flares in a conversation, Lesson 9's protocol applies. The deadline decision itself belongs in a decision journal entry.

Levels 1-5 draw on Lessons 1-5 and 10-11; levels 6-10 draw on Lessons 6-9, 12-16, and 19.
