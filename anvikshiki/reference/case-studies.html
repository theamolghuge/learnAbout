20 Case Studies

Kept compact on purpose — each one is meant to be recognized in thirty seconds, not studied like a textbook chapter. Ten fields each: situation, reaction, automatic error, examination, questions, alternatives, decision, action, consequence, lesson.

1. Anger — credit taken in a meeting

Situation: a coworker presents your idea as their own in a meeting. Reaction: hot anger, urge to call it out publicly, immediately. Error: viruddha-adjacent — assuming intent (deliberate theft) from an ambiguous sign (they may have genuinely forgotten the source). Examination: feel it, name it (L9); check what's actually known vs. inferred (L16). Questions: did they know it was mine? Is this a pattern or a one-off? Alternatives: honest memory lapse, parallel independent idea, deliberate credit-taking. Decision: ask privately and specifically rather than confront publicly. Action: a direct, non-accusatory conversation after the meeting. Consequence: either a genuine misunderstanding resolved without damage, or real information about a pattern worth escalating. Lesson: intent is never observed, only inferred — L16's whole point.

2. Money — impulse purchase

Situation: a large unplanned purchase after a stressful day. Reaction: immediate relief, urge to buy now. Error: emotional reasoning — treating the relief itself as evidence the purchase is a good idea. Examination: name it (L9); check the actual objective (L13) — is this serving a real goal or numbing a feeling? Questions: would I make this purchase on a calm day? Alternatives: the stress needs addressing directly; the purchase is a detour, not a solution. Decision: a 48-hour rule before large discretionary purchases. Action: wait, revisit with L9's protocol run fully. Consequence: most such urges fade; the ones that don't were probably real wants worth acting on anyway. Lesson: the four-step protocol works as well on desire as on anger.

3. Career — passed over for promotion

Situation: a colleague gets a promotion you expected. Reaction: hurt, a story forms fast: "they don't value me." Error: hasty generalization from one data point. Examination: apta-test the self-testimony "I deserved it" (L7); check the actual stated criteria for the decision. Questions: what specific gap, if any, was named? Did I ask, or assume? Alternatives: a real skill gap, a timing/budget issue unrelated to merit, a genuinely close call. Decision: ask directly for the specific reasoning behind the decision. Action: a direct, unemotional request for feedback. Consequence: either real, actionable feedback, or confirmation the process was arbitrary — both are more useful than the unexamined story. Lesson: "should ask" (L16) applies to institutions, not just individuals.

4. Relationships — a distant partner

Full worked example already in Lesson 9 (jealousy at a party) and Lesson 16 (missed invitation) — apply the same five-category split (know/infer/don't know/ask/act) to whatever the current specific behavior is, rather than the general feeling of distance, which is too vague to test.

5. Friendship — a friend stops initiating

Situation: a friend hasn't reached out in weeks. Reaction: quiet hurt, withdrawal in response. Error: savyabhicara — "stopped initiating" fits both "upset with you" and a dozen unrelated explanations (busy, personal crisis, doesn't realize the imbalance). Examination: L16's full split. Questions: has anything else changed in their life recently? Alternatives: life circumstances, an unspoken issue, simple asymmetry in who usually initiates. Decision: reach out yourself rather than waiting for proof either way. Action: a low-stakes, warm message. Consequence: resolves the ambiguity directly instead of letting a withdrawal spiral compound a possibly nonexistent problem. Lesson: withdrawal-in-response is often the actual driver of the outcome people fear.

6. Family — unsolicited advice

Situation: a parent criticizes a major life choice, unprompted. Reaction: defensiveness, urge to shut the conversation down. Error: treating disagreement as attack. Examination: viveka (L6) on the defensiveness itself; separate the content of the advice from the delivery. Questions: is there a real hetu buried in the advice, however badly delivered? Alternatives: genuine concern expressed poorly, an outdated model of your life, a real point worth hearing. Decision: respond to the content, not the tone, without conceding ground that isn't earned. Action: "here's what I'm actually weighing" rather than defending or capitulating. Consequence: the relationship survives disagreement better than either silent compliance or a shutdown would. Lesson: a badly delivered claim can still have a real hetu worth examining.

7. Social media — an outrage thread

Situation: a comment thread triggers a strong urge to respond. Reaction: the pull to type immediately. Error: confirmation bias plus appeal to popularity — the loudest thread feels like consensus. Examination: the full evaluation protocol (L18) before engaging at all. Questions: who's actually making the original claim? What incentive does the platform have in amplifying this specific framing? Alternatives: a manufactured controversy, a real issue poorly framed, a minority view amplified past its actual weight. Decision: don't respond until the protocol is run. Action: close the app; run L18 later if it still seems worth engaging with. Consequence: most urges to respond don't survive the wait. Lesson: the platform's incentive and your reasoning are two different, separable questions.

8. Workplace conflict — disagreement with a manager

Situation: your manager wants to ship a project faster than you think is safe. Reaction: frustration, urge to escalate or comply silently. Error: false dilemma — comply or fight, no third option considered. Examination: classify the conversation (vada/jalpa/vitanda, L17); check whether your own "we need more time" claim survives a vyapti test. Questions: what specific risk am I actually worried about, stated precisely? Alternatives: a genuine timeline mismatch, a risk that's real but smaller than it feels, a risk you can mitigate without a full delay. Decision: propose the smallest change that addresses the actual risk. Action: a specific, scoped counter-proposal, not a blanket objection. Consequence: most such conflicts resolve once the vague objection becomes a specific one. Lesson: udaharana's precision requirement applies to workplace pushback too.

9. Failure — a project fails publicly

Situation: a project you led doesn't work out, visibly. Reaction: shame, urge to either over-explain or disappear. Error: self-serving bias run backward — over-attributing the failure to yourself rather than examining causes fairly. Examination: the mistake review template (reference) — where specifically did the reasoning break, separate from the outcome. Questions: was this bad reasoning or bad luck on sound reasoning? Alternatives: a real reasoning error, an unforeseeable factor, a correct bet that didn't pay off. Decision: write the honest mistake review before deciding how to feel about it. Action: a specific, named change for next time — not a vague resolution to "do better." Consequence: the next attempt actually improves, instead of just feeling more cautious. Lesson: outcome and reasoning quality are separate axes, per the decision journal's review section.

10. Success — public praise for a team effort

Situation: you're publicly credited for something that was genuinely a team effort. Reaction: quiet satisfaction, temptation to just accept the credit. Error: self-serving bias, undisguised. Examination: ahamkara check (L6) — is accepting sole credit accurate, or just comfortable? Questions: would I explain a teammate's identical situation the same way? Alternatives: genuinely solo work, genuinely shared work, somewhere in between. Decision: credit accurately, in public, without false modesty either. Action: name the specific contributions of others when the praise happens. Consequence: trust compounds; the team notices who gives credit accurately under real incentive to keep it. Lesson: the self-serving bias check applies to success, not just failure.

11. Temptation — a shortcut nobody would catch

Situation: an opportunity to cut a corner with near-zero chance of consequence. Reaction: "just this once" reasoning. Error: evaluating the single instance instead of the pattern. Examination: L19's "repeated for years" question, run on this exact situation. Questions: if this became my default under any convenient pressure, what happens to the work over years? Alternatives: a genuinely trivial one-off, or the first instance of a pattern. Decision: treat it as the pattern-level claim, not the instance. Action: do it the right way this time, specifically because it's cheap to do so now and expensive to reverse as a habit later. Consequence: character is built exactly here, in the low-stakes instances nobody's watching. Lesson: L19's pattern-check is most useful precisely when the single instance seems too small to matter.

12. Procrastination — avoiding an unpleasant task

Situation: an important task keeps getting pushed to tomorrow. Reaction: vague guilt, no action. Error: treating "I'll do it tomorrow" as a plan rather than checking it against L8's avoidance signals. Examination: is there dread specifically, or just low energy? Different causes, different fixes. Questions: is there a belief underneath the avoidance ("I'll do it badly," "it'll go poorly") that's never been checked? Alternatives: genuine avoidance of a feared outcome, simple task-aversion, unclear next step. Decision: if it's avoidance, run L8's full check on the belief underneath; if it's an unclear next step, just define the next five minutes of it. Action: whichever the diagnosis calls for, specifically. Consequence: the two causes need different fixes, and treating avoidance like simple laziness doesn't resolve it. Lesson: procrastination is a symptom; L8 finds the actual cause.

13. Uncertainty — a job offer in another city

Full method already covered in Lesson 12 and Lesson 14 — satpratipaksha recognition, reversibility check, a matched tiebreaker, and explicit worst/best/likely cases. Apply directly.

14. Criticism — harsh feedback on your work

Situation: feedback lands harder than the work probably deserved. Reaction: defensiveness or collapse. Error: emotional reasoning — treating the sting as proof the feedback is either entirely right or entirely unfair. Examination: feel it, name it, check it (L9) before responding at all. Questions: which specific parts of the feedback have a real hetu, separate from the tone it was delivered in? Alternatives: the feedback is substantively right, substantively wrong, or a mix delivered badly. Decision: separate content from delivery before reacting to either. Action: address the specific, checkable claims first. Consequence: real improvement where the feedback was right, without absorbing the parts that weren't. Lesson: harsh delivery and correct content are independent variables.

15. Peer pressure — the group wants a decision you're unsure about

Situation: a friend group is unified on something you have real doubts about. Reaction: the pull to go along rather than be the outlier. Error: appeal to popularity — treating group agreement as if it were independent verification. Examination: L11's apta check — did anyone in the group actually verify this, or is everyone repeating the same unverified original? Questions: would I hold this view alone, without the group present? Alternatives: genuine consensus from independent reasoning, groupthink, or one confident voice everyone else deferred to. Decision: state the specific doubt plainly, once, rather than either capitulating silently or making it a fight. Action: "here's what's giving me pause" — said once, clearly. Consequence: either the doubt gets addressed with real information, or the group's actual reasoning (or lack of it) becomes visible. Lesson: popularity within a group is still just one unverified chain of shabda.

16. Misinformation — a shocking headline

Full protocol already in Lesson 18 — trace to the originating source, name the pramana, hunt disconfirming evidence, check the incentive. Apply before sharing, not after.

17. Major decisions — going back to school

Full framework across Lessons 12-15 — objective checked against revealed values (L7, L13), reversibility, second-order effects, worst/best/likely case, sunk-cost check if there's a prior investment pulling the decision one way already.

18. Personal values — ambition versus family time, forced into the open

Situation: a real opportunity forces a choice between two things both previously described as "important to me." Reaction: paralysis, or a fast decision that avoids sitting with the conflict. Error: treating both stated values as equally real without checking either. Examination: L7's revealed-preference check on both values, honestly, using real recent evidence. Questions: which one has actually been reflected in behavior over the past few months, not the past few conversations? Alternatives: one was aspirational testimony, both are real and genuinely in tension, or a third framing dissolves the apparent conflict. Decision: whichever the honest revealed-preference check actually supports, not whichever sounds better said aloud. Action: a real choice, made once the values are honestly ranked rather than left both notionally "important." Consequence: fewer decisions relitigated later, because the ranking was checked against evidence rather than assumed. Lesson: stated values that have never been ranked against each other aren't really values yet — they're aspirations.

19. Regret — a past decision, seen clearly too late

Situation: months later, a past choice clearly looks like a mistake. Reaction: rumination, self-criticism with no specific target. Error: hindsight bias — judging the past decision by information only available now. Examination: the mistake review template, applied honestly to what was actually knowable at the time. Questions: was the reasoning sound given what was known then, or was a step actually skipped? Alternatives: genuinely bad reasoning at the time, sound reasoning that hit a bad outcome, or new information that simply wasn't available then. Decision: only the first case calls for a behavior change; the other two call for acceptance, not more rumination. Action: write the honest verdict and, if warranted, the one specific change; otherwise, close the review deliberately. Consequence: regret that produces one real change is useful; regret that loops without a verdict is not. Lesson: the mistake review's job is to end the loop, not extend it.

20. Fear — avoiding a necessary hard conversation

Full worked example in Lesson 19 (the difficult-truth-to-a-friend case) — run the full pre-action checklist, with particular weight on "what am I ignoring" (the badhita check, L8) and "repeated for years," since avoidance of hard conversations is exactly the kind of pattern that compounds silently.
