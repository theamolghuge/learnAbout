# Anvikshiki Resources

## Knowledge

- [Wikipedia: Ānvīkṣikī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anviksiki)
  Summarizes the term's etymology and historical development, drawing heavily on Satischandra Vidyabhusana's *A History of Indian Logic* (1920). Use for: the origin story and the later split into *darshana* (philosophy) and *nyaya* (logic). Treat precise dates (e.g. "650 BCE") as one scholar's historical reconstruction, not a settled fact — flag as such in lessons.
- [Kautilya's Arthashastra, Book I, Chapter 2 — R. Shamasastry translation (1915, public domain)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Arthashastra/Book_I)
  The primary source: Kautilya's own four-*vidya* classification (*trayi*, *varta*, *dandaniti*, *anvikshiki*) and his direct praise of *anvikshiki* as "the lamp of all sciences." Use for: any direct quote or claim attributed to Kautilya himself. Note the translation is 110 years old — terminology (e.g. "Anvikshaki") and phrasing are dated but the meaning is reliable and widely cited by later scholarship.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Epistemology in Classical Indian Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-india/)
  Rigorous academic treatment of the *pramana* (means of valid knowledge) framework: perception, inference, comparison, testimony. Use for: the reasoning-toolkit cluster (observation, evidence, inference, causation).
- [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Nyaya](https://iep.utm.edu/nyaya/)
  The Nyaya school's five-membered syllogism (*pratijna*, *hetu*, *udaharana*, *upanaya*, *nigamana*) and *hetvabhasa* (fallacies of reasoning). Use for: the repeatable-method cluster and the fallacies cluster.
- [Wisdomlib: Fallacies of Inference in Nyaya (hetvabhasa)](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/essay/anumana-inference-in-nyaya/d/doc627333.html)
  Classical taxonomy of reasoning errors from the Nyaya tradition. Use for: mapping modern cognitive biases back to genuine classical counterparts — don't force a parallel where none really exists.
- [Encyclopaedia Britannica: The Nyaya Sutras](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-philosophy/The-Nyaya-sutras)
  The three types of debate (vada, jalpa, vitanda) and jati (objection by surface resemblance). Use for: Part 10 (speech/debate).
- [Wisdomlib: Dialectical Terms — Quibble (chala)](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/essay/philosophy-of-charaka-samhita/d/doc369721.html)
  The three kinds of chala (vak-chala, upacara-chala, samanya-chala) with classical examples (the nava-kambala pun, the "peanuts" salary example). Use for: Part 10 — maps cleanly to modern equivocation and straw man.
- [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Samkhya](https://iep.utm.edu/sankhya/)
  The purusha/prakriti distinction, the tattva layers (manas, ahamkara, buddhi), the classical three-part negation technique from Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Samkhya-Karika* (c. 350 CE), and kaivalya (liberation). Use for: self-inquiry lessons (Part 7) — this is the practical technique behind "turning inward," not just background philosophy.
- [Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." *Science*, 185(4157), 1124-1131.](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~bskyrms/bio/readings/tversky_k_heuristics_biases.pdf)
  The primary source for modern cognitive bias research: representativeness, availability, and anchoring heuristics, with the original experiments (including the UN-membership anchoring study). Use for: Part 5 (modern biases). Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) is the accessible book-length synthesis of this and later work, if a secondary source is needed.
- [PMC: "A Historical and Theoretical Review of Cognitive Behavioral Therapies" (peer-reviewed)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6208646/)
  Covers Albert Ellis's ABC model (Activating event → Belief → Consequence, 1956 — explicitly built on Stoic philosophy) and Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy: both formalize the claim that interpretation, not the event itself, produces the emotion. Use for: Part 6 (emotions) — this is the credible modern bridge for the event→interpretation→emotion chain. Note honestly: the ABC model's own stated lineage is Stoicism, not Indian philosophy — don't overstate the connection to Anvikshiki, but Lesson 6's viveka technique (naming a thought/reaction "not me, not mine") is structurally close to ACT's "cognitive defusion" concept described in the same source, which is a genuine, citable parallel worth drawing.
