Lesson 10 · Persuasion

Unity

Seventh and final row of the Cialdini reference table — the whole series started in Lesson 2 is complete after this one.

Thirty-two years after the original six principles, Cialdini added a seventh in his 2016 book Pre-Suasion: Unity — the pull of a shared identity. It's easy to confuse with Liking, so Cialdini is precise about the difference. Liking is affinity between two individuals who've gotten to know each other. Unity needs no such thing: a Chicago Cubs fan feels an instant bond with a total stranger wearing a Cubs cap, not because they know anything about that person, but because "Cubs fan" is a shared identity — family, team, nationality, religion, alma mater, veteran status.

The lever

The more someone registers as "one of us," the more automatically we extend trust — often before any actual claim has even been made. Cialdini calls this "pre-suasion" for a reason: establishing the shared identity first primes receptiveness to whatever comes next, and the identity-signal itself does none of the actual argumentative work.

At its most damaging, this is exactly the mechanism behind what the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission calls affinity fraud: investment scams that specifically target religious communities, ethnic groups, professional networks, or veterans — often run by someone who genuinely is (or convincingly poses as) a member of the group, sometimes recruiting respected community leaders to vouch for the scheme. The SEC's own guidance is blunt: shared background is not due diligence, and scammers rely on exactly that confusion.

Unity doesn't require the shared identity to be fake — an actual fellow veteran, alum, or churchgoer can still be running a bad deal. The identity match tells you nothing about the deal itself; it only tells you the two of you have something in common.

Your defense

Ask: would this claim or deal be equally credible coming from someone outside my group? If the honest answer is no — if "they're one of us" is doing real work in your trust calculation — that's Unity operating, not evidence.

Try it: identity or substance?

Four scenes. Decide whether the persuasion rests on real Substance, or is mostly riding on shared Identity.

An investment pitch circulates through your church's members-only group chat, presented by a fellow congregant, with vague details about the underlying fund.

Identity. Shared congregation membership is doing the trust-building; the actual investment details are vague — the classic shape of affinity fraud.

A fellow alum reaches out about a business opportunity and sends audited financials, a client list you can independently call, and a clear risk disclosure.

Substance. Shared alma mater opened the door, but the actual case stands on verifiable material — both can be true at once.

An ad uses your specific hometown's slang and a local landmark in the background, with no actual detail about why the product itself is good.

Identity. Manufactured local "one of us" signaling, doing the persuasive work a real product argument would otherwise need to do.

A veteran-owned company's ad states its veteran ownership plainly, alongside independently verifiable third-party reviews of the actual product.

Substance. Shared identity is disclosed honestly as one fact among others, not substituted for evidence about the product.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one moment today where a "we're the same kind of people" signal (shared alma mater, hometown, profession, fandom, background) showed up right before an ask. Journal it, and honestly answer: would this ask be equally credible from someone outside that group?
