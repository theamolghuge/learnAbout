Social Engineering: Pretexting & Elicitation

This is the synthesis lesson for the whole persuasion thread — every technique below is a Cialdini principle from Lessons 2–10, aimed at extracting information instead of a sale.

The FBI publishes a public brochure teaching its own employees to recognize elicitation: "a technique used to collect information that is not readily available, without raising suspicion that specific facts are being sought." It's not a fringe intelligence-agency concept — it's the same conversational manipulation used against ordinary people every day, and the FBI's own defensive material names the exact moves to watch for.

The moves

  • Feigned ignorance — pretending not to know something, so your natural urge to educate someone takes over and you say more than you meant to.
  • Flattery — praise that lowers your guard and makes you want to keep proving you deserve it.
  • Deliberate false statement ("bracketing") — saying something slightly wrong on purpose, so you correct it and hand over the accurate version without ever being asked directly. "I heard your migration got scrapped." "No, it's still happening — just pushed to March." You just gave the real date.
  • Manufactured rapport — instant "we're the same kind of people" small talk before the real ask, deployed exactly the way Liking and Unity predict.

Pretexting is the companion move: fabricating a believable scenario or identity — IT support, an auditor, a new vendor, a colleague from another office — to get trust and compliance without anyone verifying it. Add Authority's costume and a manufactured sense of urgency (Scarcity's lever), and you have the shape of the classic "urgent IT call" scam almost everyone has now encountered in some form.

Notice this whole lesson is really a recap: every technique here is a Cialdini principle you already have a defense for. Social engineering doesn't need new levers — it just points the same seven at information instead of money.

Your defense

One habit covers most of this: if someone contacts you (call, email, message) claiming urgency or authority and asks for information or action, don't continue on the channel they initiated. Independently look up the real contact point and verify through it. And notice the specific feeling of "let me correct that" mid-conversation — that pull is often the false-statement technique working exactly as designed.

Try it: casual or angled?

A new acquaintance at a conference says, "I heard your company's system migration got scrapped" — when it's actually still happening, just delayed — and you find yourself giving them the real timeline.

Angled — a textbook deliberate false statement, correctly identified by the urge to "correct the record" it triggered.

A friend asks how your weekend was, and you tell them about a hike.

Casual — ordinary reciprocal conversation, no extraction pattern present.

Someone calls claiming to be from "IT Support," says there's an urgent security issue, and asks you to read back the verification code just texted to you.

Angled — Pretexting (fake identity) plus manufactured urgency plus borrowed Authority. The request to read back a verification code is the clearest tell of all.

A colleague genuinely asks for your opinion on a work problem and listens to your actual answer, no unusual probing.

Casual — a real question, answered plainly, nothing extracted that wasn't the actual point of the conversation.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one interaction today where someone might be fishing for information — an oddly specific question from someone you don't know well, an unexpected call claiming urgency. Journal whether it read as Casual or Angled, and if Angled, whether you caught yourself about to over-share before you noticed.
