Reading a Baseline

First switch of domain, from persuasion to nonverbal reading — deliberately, so the two threads interleave rather than running six Cialdini lessons back to back. They'll keep alternating.

Here's a claim you've probably heard: crossed arms mean someone's closed off or defensive. It's one of the most repeated "facts" about body language — and it doesn't hold up. Some people cross their arms because they're cold, or their chair has no armrests, or it's just how they've always stood. Treating it as a universal signal is exactly the kind of claim professional behavior analysts don't use, because it fails the moment you actually test it against a real person.

What professionals — the panelists at The Behavior Panel, poker-tells author Zachary Elwood, and Ekman's own research — actually rely on isn't a dictionary of gestures. It's a method. It has four steps, and the first one is the one everyone skips.

The method

  1. Baseline — before you can spot a change, you need to know what "normal" looks like for this specific person. Watch them for a minute or two during easy, low-stakes small talk: how much do they gesture, how fast do they talk, do they hold eye contact, do they fidget. This is entirely individual — someone's baseline "nervous" might be someone else's baseline "relaxed."
  2. Deviation — when a specific topic, question, or moment arrives, does their behavior change from their own baseline? Not from some universal norm — from the baseline you just watched them establish thirty seconds ago.
  3. Cluster — one changed gesture, alone, is close to noise. A voice-pitch shift and a posture change and a pause in speech, landing together right as one topic comes up — that's a cluster, and clusters are what professional readers actually weight.
  4. Context — before concluding anything, ask what else could explain the deviation. Uncomfortable chair, unrelated bad news, a cold room, a stiff shoulder. Rule out the boring explanation first.

Even the most literally "hidden" signal — a microexpression — is rare in real interactions (roughly 2% of expressions, per Porter & ten Brinke's research) and shows up at similar rates whether someone is lying or telling the truth. Treat this whole domain as a way to know where to look closer, never as a lie detector.

Put together: a deviation cluster is a flag, not a verdict. What it tells you is "something changed right here, worth a closer look" — via a follow-up question, not a conclusion you silently reach and act on. (More on the actual follow-up question — the calibrated question — in an upcoming negotiation lesson.)

Try it: real signal or myth?

Four claims. Decide whether each is a Real application of the baseline method, or a Myth — an absolute, no-baseline-needed claim of the kind that doesn't hold up.

A coworker who normally talks with their hands the whole meeting suddenly goes still and folds them in their lap the moment the budget overrun comes up — worth a follow-up question.

Real. This is exactly baseline (talks with hands) → deviation (goes still) → tied to a specific topic. Framed as a flag worth asking about, not a verdict.

Crossed arms always mean someone is closed off or being defensive.

Myth. No baseline, no context — just a universal claim about one gesture. Could be cold, could be a bad chair, could be how this person always stands.

If someone won't make eye contact while talking to you, they're lying.

Myth — and a particularly persistent one. Eye contact norms vary enormously by culture, personality, and individual habit; treated as a universal deception cue, it fails constantly.

Your poker opponent, chatty and loose all night, suddenly goes quiet and stares at their chips right after a big raise — a deviation cluster worth noting, not proof of a bluff.

Real. Baseline (chatty, loose) → deviation (quiet, staring) → tied to one moment (right after the raise) — and correctly stops short of calling it proof of anything.

Today's real-world practice

Pick one person you'll talk to today — coworker, barista, friend, family member. Spend the first minute or two of easy conversation just building their baseline: how they gesture, how fast they talk, their usual eye contact. Later in the conversation, watch for one moment where something shifts from that baseline, and note what topic was on the table when it happened. Don't decide what it means — just log the observation.
