Credible Commitments

New domain: game theory and strategic thinking, via Thomas Schelling. New reference doc starts here.

Thomas Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict centers on a paradox: "in bargaining, weakness is often strength... to burn bridges behind one may suffice to undo an opponent." A general who burns the bridges behind his own troops has just made his own army weaker — no possibility of retreat. And that's exactly what makes his position stronger: the enemy now knows there's no scenario where this army backs off, so they have to plan around an opponent who literally cannot fold.

During the Cuban Missile Crisis, President Kennedy made the U.S. demand for missile removal public, in front of the press and the world. That single move tied his own hands — backing down quietly would now cost him enormously in credibility, not just strategically but personally. And that's precisely what made the Soviets take the demand seriously: a private threat can be walked back with no one the wiser; a public one can't.

The mechanism

A commitment only constrains the other side if breaking it would cost you something real and visible. Not "I really mean it" — an actual cost, verifiable by someone other than you, that would kick in if you reversed course. That's the whole difference between a threat that works and a bluff that gets called.

This is a different move from Lesson 3's Commitment & Consistency. That lesson was about someone else using your past small commitment as a lever on you. This lesson is about deliberately, publicly constraining your own future options, on purpose, to make your own position stronger in front of someone else. Same underlying force, opposite direction.

It's also compatible with, not contradicted by, Lesson 6's reserve clause. You can hold zero emotional stake in an outcome (reserved, composed, unreadable) while still making a hard, visible, external commitment that structurally limits your own future moves. One is about your internal relationship to the outcome; the other is a public structural fact about your options.

Your defense: reading someone else's commitment

When someone tells you "this is absolutely final" or "I will not move on this," ask: what would it actually cost them, visibly, to go back on that? If the answer is nothing — no contract, no public statement, no reputational stake, nothing a third party would notice — you're looking at a stated position, not a real commitment. Treat it as one more data point in the negotiation, not a wall.

Try it: locked or hollow?

A company publicly announces to shareholders and the press that it will not lay off staff this year.

Locked. Public, verifiable by press and shareholders, with a real reputational and possibly legal cost to reversing it.

A coworker tells you privately, just between the two of you, that they "definitely won't" take on the extra project.

Hollow. Private, unverifiable, no cost to quietly changing their mind — you'd have no way to even confirm it happened.

A supplier signs a contract with financial penalty clauses for late delivery.

Locked. Enforceable, with a real and specific cost attached to breaking it.

The other side says "this is absolutely our final offer," verbally, in a back-and-forth where they've already revised their "final" offer twice before.

Hollow — and the repeated pattern (see Lesson 12 on Pattern vs. Episode) is itself the evidence. A "final" that's moved twice isn't final.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one commitment today — yours or someone else's — and ask whether it's Locked (real, verifiable, costly to break) or Hollow (a stated position with no actual cost to reversing). If it's your own commitment and you want it to hold, try making it more Locked: say it publicly, or attach a real cost to breaking it.
