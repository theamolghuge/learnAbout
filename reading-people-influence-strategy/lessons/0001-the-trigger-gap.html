Lesson 1 · Self-Regulation

The Trigger Gap

Why start here, and not with body language or negotiation? Because every other skill in this workspace — reading a tell, resisting a pitch, holding your line in a negotiation — depends on you noticing your own reaction before it drives your behavior. This is the one piece everything else is built on top of.

Around 55 AD, a formerly enslaved Stoic teacher named Epictetus opened his handbook with one sentence that the rest of this workspace quietly assumes is true:

"Some things are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Not in our power are body, property, reputation, command, and, in a word, whatever are not our own actions."
— Epictetus, Enchiridion, Ch. 1

Translated out of 55 AD: someone else's words, someone else's move, the traffic, the market price — none of that is yours to control. What is yours to control is what you do next. The gap between the two is where every skill in this course lives.

Three words, one gap

To make that gap easy to catch in real time, this workspace splits any tense moment into three parts. Precise definitions are kept in the Glossary; here's the short version:

  • Trigger — the external event. Something said, something done, a situation you walk into. Not yours to control.
  • Reaction — the automatic spike that follows, before you've thought about it: heat in the face, a jump in pulse, the urge to snap back. Barely yours to control — but noticeable, with practice.
  • Response — what you actually do or say next. Fully yours to control. This is the only lever you have.

The phrase "between stimulus and response there is a space" is often attributed to Viktor Frankl, but Frankl scholars have never located it in his actual writing — treat it as a popular paraphrase, not a citation. Epictetus's text above is the real, sourced original.

Almost everyone can name their Trigger. Most people skip straight from Reaction to Response without noticing the seam between them — the snap comment, the frozen silence, the cave-in, all fired off before any actual choice happened. The entire skill here is widening that seam until you can feel it happen, in the moment, and pick your Response on purpose instead of by default.

Try it: name the gap

Four short scenes. For each, decide whether the underlined moment is the Trigger, the Reaction, or the Response.

In a team meeting, your manager says your report was “sloppy” — in front of everyone. What is that comment itself?

The comment is the external event — something someone else said. That makes it the Trigger, full stop, regardless of how fair or unfair it was.

Before you've said a single word back, your jaw tightens and your ears go hot. What is the jaw and the heat?

That's the automatic spike, arriving before any thought — the Reaction. It happened to you; you didn't choose it.

You take a breath and say, “Can you say more about what wasn't landing for you?” instead of arguing back. What is that question?

That's a deliberately chosen move made after the spike, not instead of noticing it — the Response. It's the one part of this whole sequence you actually authored.

Later, negotiating a car price, your counterpart crosses her arms and looks away the moment you name your number. What is her crossed arms and turned head?

A Trigger doesn't have to be spoken — her body language is an external event too, and it's about to become a Trigger for your Reaction. (More on reading that signal itself in a later lesson on body language.)

Today's real-world practice

Three times today, when you notice a spike of feeling — irritation, anxiety, the urge to over-explain, the need to fill a silence — pause for two seconds and mentally log all three: what was the Trigger, what did the Reaction actually feel like in your body, and what Response did you choose (or default into)?

You don't need to change your behavior yet. The entire assignment is noticing — the seam between Reaction and Response only gets wider with repeated observation, not with willpower.

Jot each one below as it happens (or right after) — one line per moment is plenty: Trigger → Reaction → Response.

Saved locally in this browser, to this file only — nothing leaves your machine. Bring what you notice back to your next conversation with your teacher; that's what turns raw noticing into an actual learning record.
