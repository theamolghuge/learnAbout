Greene's Lens: Reading Power Plays

Read this one the way MISSION.md frames it: a field guide to what's sometimes done to you, not a personal code of conduct to adopt. Several of Greene's 48 Laws are ethically ugly by design — the goal here is naming a pattern, never practicing it.

In 1661, Nicolas Fouquet, finance minister to young King Louis XIV, threw what may have been the most lavish party in French history — hoping to impress the king into naming him prime minister. It worked too well. Louis, famously vain about being the center of attention, couldn't tolerate being outdone in splendor by his own minister. Fouquet was arrested the next day on a fabricated charge and spent the rest of his life in prison.

Robert Greene uses this story to illustrate what he calls Law 1: Never Outshine the Master. The underlying pattern, stripped of the court intrigue: people in positions of authority often feel less secure in that position than they appear, and some will turn against anyone who makes them look or feel inferior — even someone who did excellent, well-intentioned work, with zero intention of threatening anyone.

Why this is worth naming, defensively

This explains a specific, otherwise-confusing experience: doing genuinely good work and getting punished or frozen out for it instead of rewarded. Without this pattern named, it's easy to conclude you did something wrong. Often you didn't — you were simply visible in a way that threatened someone whose sense of authority depends on staying the most visible person in the room.

This is not a universal law of all leaders. Plenty of genuinely secure people actively want the people around them to shine — that's what a real mentor looks like. Apply Lesson 12's Pattern-vs-Episode discipline here too: one cold reaction after one win might be nothing; a repeated shape, specifically following your visible successes, is the signal worth taking seriously.

What to actually do with it

Recognizing the pattern isn't a license to dim your own work — it's information for a decision you get to make deliberately instead of being blindsided by: whether to keep investing trust in that relationship, whether to make sure your contributions are visible to more than one person (so your standing doesn't rest entirely on one insecure gatekeeper's goodwill), or whether it's simply time to look elsewhere.

Try it: threat or mentor?

Your manager was warm and encouraging until your presentation got praised by senior leadership. Since then, you've been quietly excluded from meetings you used to be part of, with no stated reason.

Threat — the exact shape of the Law 1 pattern: warmth reversed right after your visibility increased, with no other stated cause.

Your manager credits your work by name in front of their own boss, and pushes for you to present it yourself next time.

Mentor — actively amplifying your visibility rather than feeling threatened by it. This is what security in one's own position looks like.

After you solve a problem your team lead had been stuck on for weeks, they stop looping you into decisions you used to be part of.

Threat — visible competence followed immediately by quiet exclusion. Worth watching for repetition before drawing a firm conclusion.

A mentor introduces you to their own industry contacts and encourages you to build a reputation independent of them.

Mentor — actively building your independent standing, the opposite of guarding their own position against you.

Today's real-world practice

Think of one moment, recent or ongoing, where someone's visible success was met with either warmth or a cooling from someone above them (could be you, could be someone you've observed). Journal it as Threat or Mentor — and apply the Pattern-vs-Episode check: is this a one-off, or a repeated shape?
