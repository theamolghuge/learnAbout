The First Weapon: Reciprocity

This is the first of a one-per-lesson series on Cialdini's principles — see the running quick-reference table, which fills in one row per lesson rather than all at once.

In the late 1960s, sociologist Phillip Kunz mailed Christmas cards to 600 complete strangers, picked at random out of a city directory. He'd never met any of them. Around 20% mailed a card back — to a stranger. Cialdini opens Influence with that story for a reason: reciprocity is one of the strongest, most automatic pulls in human behavior, and it fires even when the "favor" was unrequested, unequal, or from a total stranger.

Every human culture studied has some version of this norm. Anthropologists think it's load-bearing for how any species that isn't purely self-sufficient manages to cooperate at all — you can extend trust to someone before you know if they'll ever pay it back, because the norm itself supplies the enforcement. That's exactly what makes it exploitable.

The lever

The move, used by everyone from Hare Krishna fundraisers (who'd press a free flower into your hand before asking for a donation — and saw donations rise even from people who threw the flower straight in the trash) to a car salesman who gets you a coffee, follows the same shape:

  1. Give something small, unrequested — a favor, a sample, a concession, a compliment.
  2. The norm of Reciprocity creates a felt debt in you, often without your noticing it happened.
  3. Ask for something back — frequently much larger than what was given.

The asymmetry is the whole trick: the norm doesn't require the repayment to be proportional, so a one-dollar flower can trigger a twenty-dollar donation. Cialdini calls this letting someone else "dictate the terms of the trade" — you gave them the opening move, and the norm does the rest.

Reciprocity itself isn't the problem

Notice this cuts both ways. A genuine favor — a friend covering your shift, a colleague vouching for you with no angle — also triggers Reciprocity, and repaying it is exactly how healthy relationships and trust get built. The skill isn't to become someone who refuses all favors. It's to notice, in the moment, whether the favor was honest or rigged — and only the second one needs a defense.

Cialdini's own suggested defense, from the book: accept a favor as a favor. If later evidence shows it was rigged specifically to extract a bigger ask, you're allowed to redefine it as a trick — and a trick, unlike a genuine gift, creates no debt at all.

Try it: honest or rigged?

Four short scenes. For each, decide whether the favor described was offered Honest or Rigged.

A software vendor gives your team free "premium" access for a month, then calls two days before it ends to schedule a contract-renewal meeting.

Rigged. The free trial exists specifically to create a sense of debt (and loss aversion) right before the renewal ask — that's the shape of the lever, not a coincidence.

A coworker covers your on-call shift last month because you had a family emergency, no discussion of payback at the time.

Honest. Given with no engineered follow-up ask in mind — repaying this kind of favor is exactly how trust between people actually gets built.

A stranger at a kiosk hands you a free sample of hand cream, then immediately asks if you'd like to hear about a "special today-only" skincare bundle.

Rigged — this is the Hare Krishna flower move in a mall kiosk costume. The sample's entire purpose is to open the door for the pitch that follows it.

A mentor spends an hour giving you unsolicited, genuinely useful feedback on a project, then goes back to their own work.

Honest — no ask followed. If you later feel moved to return the favor unprompted, that's Reciprocity working exactly as it should.

Today's real-world practice

Notice two times today someone offers you something — a favor, a free sample, a compliment, a concession in a conversation, even just "let me get that for you." For each one, don't decide on the spot whether to reciprocate. Just log: what was offered, and your best read on Honest or Rigged — and what you'd want your Response (from Lesson 1) to be if it turns out to be Rigged.
