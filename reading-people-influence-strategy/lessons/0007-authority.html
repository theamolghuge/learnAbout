Lesson 7 · Persuasion

Authority

Fourth row of the Cialdini reference table. Back to the persuasion thread after Lesson 6's self-regulation detour.

Before running his famous 1963 study, Stanley Milgram asked psychiatrists to predict how many ordinary people would keep administering what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to a stranger, simply because a man in a gray lab coat told them to continue. The prediction: about 0.1%. The actual result: 65% went all the way to the maximum, 450-volt switch.

The most telling detail isn't the headline number — it's what happened when the researchers changed one thing. When the same instructions came from an "ordinary member of the public" in regular clothes instead of a lab coat, obedience collapsed to 20%. The instructions were identical. The substance behind them hadn't changed at all. Only the costume had.

The lever

Deferring to real expertise is usually correct — you don't have time to independently verify your surgeon's medical training before an operation, and you shouldn't need to. Authority is the principle that lets us function at all in a world with more expertise than any one person can personally verify. The lever appears the moment someone realizes the trappings of authority — a title, a uniform, technical jargon, a confident tone, a name-drop — move people almost as well as the real thing, and far more cheaply.

That's the whole trick behind a supplement ad's "Dr." with a credential from an unaccredited program making claims outside any real specialty, a phishing email impersonating your CEO, or a scammer's confident use of insider jargon. None of it requires actual expertise — it only requires looking and sounding like it.

Your defense

Two separate questions, both required: is this person's authority real, and is it relevant to this specific claim? A genuine expert speaking outside their actual specialty still isn't evidence — a celebrity endorsing a supplement is real fame, wrong relevance.

Separate the costume from the substance. Ask: can I verify this person's expertise independent of how they've presented themselves, and is it actually relevant to the specific claim being made — not just impressive-sounding in general?

Try it: earned or staged?

A cardiologist with 20 years' experience recommends a specific treatment for your heart condition, and walks you through the reasoning when you ask.

Earned. Real, relevant, verifiable expertise — and reasoning you can actually check, not just a title asserted at you.

An email claiming to be from your company's CEO, sent from a slightly misspelled domain, urgently requesting a wire transfer.

Staged — the authority is entirely impersonated. (More on this exact pattern in a later social-engineering lesson.)

A supplement ad features someone in a white lab coat, titled "Dr." from an unaccredited online program, making health claims outside any real specialty.

Staged — the costume (coat, title) is doing all the work Milgram's lab coat did, with no verifiable substance behind it.

Your mechanic, who's serviced your car honestly for a decade, tells you the brakes need replacing now.

Earned — a real, relevant track record you've personally observed, not a symbol asserted at you for the first time.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one claim today made more persuasive by a title, uniform, credential, or name-drop. Journal what the claim was and whether the authority behind it was Earned (real, relevant, and verifiable) or Staged (just the costume).
