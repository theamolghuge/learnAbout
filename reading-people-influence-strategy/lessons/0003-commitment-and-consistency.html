Commitment & Consistency

Second row of the Cialdini reference table. Same series as Lesson 2: Reciprocity — a different lever, same "spot it in the wild" method.

Gordon's, a restaurant in Chicago, had a no-show problem: about 30% of reservations just didn't show up. Its receptionists had always ended calls with "please call if you have to cancel." The owner changed exactly two words. Instead of a statement, the receptionist asked a question, then paused for an answer: "Will you please call if you have to cancel?" Customers said "yes, of course" — and no-shows dropped from 32% to 10%, on that wording change alone.

Nothing about the cancellation policy changed. What changed is that the customer spoke a commitment out loud, in their own voice, and people work hard to stay consistent with commitments they made themselves — especially ones made publicly, in writing, or with some effort attached.

The lever

Stanford researchers Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser ran the classic version of this in 1966. They asked homeowners to let a team spend two hours in their house inventorying household products — a big, invasive ask. Only 22% agreed. But when a different group was first asked a tiny, unrelated favor three days earlier — filling out a short survey about soap — 53% agreed to the big ask later. One small "yes" more than doubled compliance with a completely separate, much larger request. This is foot-in-the-door: get a small commitment first, then the desire to stay consistent with your own past "yes" carries you into the bigger one.

The lever works because the small commitment doesn't feel like a trade (that's Reciprocity's territory) — it feels like discovering something about yourself. Once you've said "yes" to something small, refusing the next related ask means admitting you were wrong, careless, or not really the kind of person your first "yes" implied. Most people would rather comply than have that thought.

Your test: would past-you choose it again?

Cialdini's own suggested defense isn't "never commit to anything" — obviously untenable, and dishonest to how healthy commitments work (a promise to a friend, a New Year's resolution, showing up for training). The test is narrower: when a past commitment is being used to pull you into something bigger, ask whether you'd make that same original commitment again, knowing everything you know now.

If the honest answer is "yes, I'd still agree to that" — the consistency pull is doing its normal, healthy job. If the honest answer is "no, I only said that because it seemed small/harmless/polite at the time" — you're allowed to stop. A commitment extracted through a trick doesn't deserve the same loyalty as one you'd choose in full knowledge.

Try it: honor or waive?

Four scenes. For each, apply the test — would past-you make the same call again, knowing what present-you now knows — and decide whether to Honor the commitment or Waive it.

Weeks ago you told your manager, "sure, I can help organize the offsite" — sounded like a couple hours. It's now consumed a month of your evenings with zero extra support offered.

Waive — or at least renegotiate. You agreed to "help," not to a part-time unpaid job. Past-you would not have said yes to this scope; you're not bound to a blank check just because you said yes to something else with the same name.

A salesperson asks, "if the price were right, you'd be interested, right?" You say "sure" to be polite. Ten minutes later they're pointing back at your "yes" to push a product you don't actually want.

Waive. Your "sure" was never a real evaluation of the actual offer — it was a scripted trial close designed to manufacture a "yes" to point back at later. Past-you wasn't actually agreeing to buy anything.

Months ago you publicly committed to running a charity 5k your close friend organizes. Training has been going badly and you don't feel like it this week.

Honor. Knowing everything you know now, you'd still make this commitment — it's aligned with an actual value (supporting your friend), not a trick. "I don't feel like it today" isn't the same as "I was manipulated into this."

A canvasser gets you to agree, "wouldn't it be great if everyone had clean water?" — then leverages your nod into a request for a large on-the-spot donation to an organization you'd never heard of five minutes ago.

Waive the leap, not the value. Agreeing that clean water is good doesn't commit you to this specific ask, from this specific stranger, at this size. You can hold the value and still say no to the ask built on top of it.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one moment today where you're asked a small "would you agree that…" or "you'd want X, right?" question — from a salesperson, a coworker, an ad, even a friend setting up a favor. Don't answer automatically. Run the test: would you make that exact same small commitment again, in full view of where it's headed? Journal what the question was, your gut answer, and whether the eventual ask (if there was one) deserved Honor or Waive.
