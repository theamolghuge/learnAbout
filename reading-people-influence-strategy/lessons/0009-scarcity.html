Lesson 9 · Persuasion

Scarcity

Sixth row of the Cialdini reference table.

In 1975, researchers Worchel, Lee, and Adewole showed people two identical glass jars of identical cookies — one holding ten, the other just two. Same cookies, same jars. People rated the cookies in the near-empty jar as more valuable. Then they ran it the other way: a jar that had gone from two cookies to ten — sudden abundance — got the lowest ratings of all. Nothing about the cookies ever changed. Only the count did.

Scarcity is the principle that limited availability signals value, even with zero other evidence. The underlying logic isn't crazy: if something's running out, maybe other people have already noticed something you haven't. That heuristic is often useful — until someone manufactures the scarcity on purpose.

The lever

"Only 3 left in stock." "Sale ends at midnight." "Limited to the first 50." Some of these are true. Many are cosmetic — a counter that resets, a "deadline" that reappears next week, an artificial cap on something with no real supply constraint at all. The lever works whether the scarcity is real or staged, because your reaction fires off the claim of scarcity, not an audit of the actual supply chain behind it.

Scarcity is at its most persuasive exactly when you have the least time to think — which is precisely the design goal of most "ends tonight" messaging. Notice the urgency itself as a signal to slow down, not speed up.

Your defense

Ask two things: is this scarcity real (can I verify it, does it recur suspiciously often, would it still be gone tomorrow), and — regardless of the answer — would I actually want this thing at this price if it weren't running out? Scarcity should never be the reason you want something; at most it's a reason to decide faster about something you already wanted.

Try it: real or staged scarcity?

A booking site shows "only 1 room left at this price" — and shows the identical message again tomorrow, and the day after.

Staged. Scarcity that never actually resolves into "sold out" isn't tracking real inventory — it's a permanent prop.

A small bakery sells out of a specific pastry most mornings by 9am, and today it's already gone when you arrive at 9:15.

Real — genuinely finite daily production, verifiable by just showing up, with a real chance of actually selling out.

An online course page has a countdown timer that, when you clear your browser cookies, resets to a fresh 24 hours.

Staged. A deadline that resets on command isn't a deadline — it's a countdown prop with nothing real behind it.

A concert venue has a genuinely fixed number of seats, and the show is verifiably sold out on the official ticketing site.

Real — a physical, verifiable, independently-confirmable constraint.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one "limited," "ending soon," or "only X left" message today. Journal whether it was verifiably Real or likely Staged — and, separately, whether you'd actually want the thing at all if the scarcity claim were removed entirely.
