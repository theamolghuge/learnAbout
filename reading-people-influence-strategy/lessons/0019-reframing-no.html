Lesson 19 · Sales & Objection-Handling

Reframing "No"

Combines Lesson 11's calibrated questions and Lesson 13's labeling into one applied skill: getting past a surface objection to the real one underneath.

Voss argues most people chase agreement in exactly the wrong direction. He splits "yes" into three kinds: Counterfeit (they plan to say no, but "yes" is the easier exit, or keeps you talking without committing to anything), Confirmation (an innocent reflex to a black-or-white question, no real promise attached), and Commitment — the only one that leads anywhere, a real agreement backed by real reasons.

Most quick "sounds good" responses to a pitch, a plan, or a favor are Counterfeit — an easy way to end an uncomfortable moment, with the real objection never actually surfaced. Voss's inversion: "No" makes people feel safe and in control, and is the start of a real conversation, not a rejection of one. An honest "no" that surfaces the actual concern is worth far more than a fast "yes" that evaporates later.

Stated objection vs. real objection

Here's where Lesson 11 and Lesson 13 combine into one move. When someone raises an objection, resist the urge to argue against it directly — the stated objection is very often not the real one. Instead:

  1. Label it: "It seems like the timing is the real sticking point here."
  2. Ask a calibrated question: "What about the timeline feels off?"
  3. Address whatever concern actually surfaces — which is often different from what was first said.

The Rule of Three

To check whether an eventual "yes" is Commitment or just Counterfeit, Voss suggests getting the same agreement reaffirmed at least three times, through different calibrated questions, summaries, or labels. It's hard to fake genuine conviction three separate times in three different phrasings — a real "yes" holds up; a counterfeit one starts to waver or gets vague.

Try it: fake or real?

You ask, "does this all sound good?" They say "Yeah, sounds good," quickly, and change the subject.

Fake — a classic Counterfeit Yes: fast, no specifics, and an immediate subject change to avoid any follow-up.

Asked three separate times, in three different ways, whether they're really on board, they give the same specific reasons each time for wanting to move forward.

Real — passes the Rule of Three. Consistent, specific reasoning repeated under different framing is hard to fake.

After you label their hesitation and ask what's really wrong, they say, "Sure, whatever, let's just say yes and move on" — without ever answering the question.

Fake — the real concern was never actually surfaced, just papered over with an agreeable-sounding brush-off.

After you label their hesitation, they explain the real concern (budget timing) themselves, then spontaneously bring up specific next steps and a date to finalize.

Real — the actual concern surfaced and got addressed, and the follow-through details came unprompted.

Today's real-world practice

Notice one moment today where someone says "yes" too quickly, or raises an objection that feels surface-level. Try labeling it and asking a calibrated question to find what's really underneath. Journal the stated objection, the real one you uncovered (if any), and whether the eventual answer felt Fake or Real.
