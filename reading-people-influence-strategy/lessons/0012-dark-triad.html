Dark Triad: Spotting the Pattern

Read this one carefully: the goal is protecting your own boundaries, never diagnosing a real person in your life. Nothing here qualifies you to label anyone — see MISSION.md's explicit scope note.

In 2002, psychologists Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams named a cluster of three overlapping personality traits the Dark Triad: Narcissism (grandiosity, constant need for admiration, thin skin toward criticism), Machiavellianism (strategic manipulation, a cynical view of others, willingness to deceive for self-interest), and Psychopathy (callousness, impulsivity, thrill-seeking). Everyone has some measure of each — these are dimensions, not categories you either belong to or don't.

The one detail that actually matters for you

Here's the practically important finding, from a large meta-analysis of empathy across the three traits: narcissism is linked to low affective empathy (not feeling moved by someone else's pain) but is not reliably linked to low cognitive empathy (accurately understanding what someone else is feeling and why). Machiavellianism shows deficits in both. Psychopathy shows the strongest deficit specifically in the affective side.

Cognitive empathy intact, affective empathy absent, means: they can read you accurately and still not care. Don't mistake "they clearly understand exactly how I feel" for "they're on my side" — those are two separate systems, and the gap between them is exactly where manipulation operates.

This directly matters for everything else in this workspace. A lot of the reading-people skill you're building — baselines, deviations, calibrated questions — is exactly the kind of accurate reading someone high in these traits is often unusually good at themselves. The traits don't make someone bad at reading people. They remove the part of most people that would feel bad about exploiting what they read.

Your defense: pattern, not episode

You are not qualified to diagnose anyone, including yourself, from a checklist — and trying to is itself a bad habit to build. What you can do is notice repeated, cross-context patterns and let them inform how much trust you extend, without ever needing a label:

  • Charm that's reliably deployed while they need something from you, and reliably withdrawn once they don't
  • Little to no discomfort or remorse after being shown clear evidence of harm they caused
  • Rules and consequences that seem to apply to everyone except them
  • Accurately reading what upsets you, and using it anyway

The discipline is the same either direction: a single bad day is not a pattern, and don't let a friend's one rough week get filed under any of the above. A pattern is the same shape, showing up repeatedly, across different situations and over time — that's the bar.

Try it: pattern or episode?

A colleague is warm and helpful whenever they need something from you, then cold and dismissive the moment they don't — and this has happened three separate times this year.

Pattern — the same shape, repeated across multiple separate occasions. Worth adjusting how much you extend, without needing to label anyone.

A normally reliable friend snapped at you once, during a week they were dealing with a family emergency, and apologized sincerely the next day.

Episode. One occurrence, real context, genuine remorse — exactly what shouldn't get filed away as a character judgment.

Someone consistently takes credit for others' work and shows no visible discomfort when confronted with clear evidence, observed independently by several coworkers across different projects.

Pattern — same behavior, multiple independent witnesses, multiple contexts. This is what "repeated and cross-context" actually looks like.

A friend forgot your birthday once this year, seemed genuinely embarrassed about it, and made a real effort to make it up to you.

Episode. A single lapse with real embarrassment and repair attached — the opposite of the no-remorse pattern to actually watch for.

Today's real-world practice

Think of one behavior you've noticed in someone in your life that bothered you. Journal it honestly: is this actually a repeated pattern across time and context, or is it really a single episode you might be over-weighting? Either answer is fine — the point is training the distinction, not building a case against anyone.
