Calibrated Questions

First dedicated negotiation lesson — new domain, new reference doc that'll grow alongside it.

Former FBI lead hostage negotiator Chris Voss calls one particular question "the greatest": "How am I supposed to do that?" Not sarcastic, not rhetorical — asked as a genuine request for help, tone included. Someone demands something unreasonable; instead of refusing (which invites a fight) or complying (which gives away your position), you hand the problem back to them, and let them solve it for you.

That's a calibrated question: open-ended, starting with "What" or "How," designed to give the other side a feeling of control while you're actually steering. Two things it deliberately avoids:

  • "Why" questions — even asked innocently, "why" tends to land as an accusation ("why would you think that's fair") and triggers defensiveness.
  • Closed (yes/no) questions — "can you lower the price?" hands the other person a one-word exit. There's nothing to solve, just something to refuse.

Why this fits here

Notice what a calibrated question does not require: revealing your position, arguing your case, or reacting to pressure. You're asking, not asserting — which means there's nothing for the other side to push back against, and nothing in your tone for them to read as a tell. This is the negotiation-table version of the composure work from Lesson 1 and Lesson 6: it lets you stay level while still actively steering.

This cuts both ways. Someone skilled might ask you a calibrated question to extract information or soften your position — recognizing the shape ("What"/"How," landing right at a point of leverage) doesn't mean refusing to answer, just choosing deliberately how much to reveal.

A small starter kit

  • "How am I supposed to do that?" — when a demand is unreasonable
  • "What about this is important to you?" — to surface the actual interest behind a position
  • "How does this affect the rest of your team?" — to widen the frame beyond the person in front of you
  • "What's the biggest challenge you're facing here?" — to find the real obstacle instead of guessing

Try it: steers or blocks?

Four questions someone might ask in a negotiation. Decide whether each one Steers the conversation productively, or Blocks it (invites defensiveness or a flat refusal).

"Why would you ever think that's a fair price?"

Blocks. A "why" question aimed at their reasoning reads as an accusation, and invites a defensive dig-in rather than an explanation.

"What about this price feels off to you?"

Steers. Open "What" question, no accusation, invites them to hand you the actual objection you need to address.

"Can you just lower the price?"

Blocks. A closed yes/no question hands them a free, effortless "no" with nothing further to discuss.

"How am I supposed to explain this budget increase to my boss?"

Steers — Voss's classic move. It hands your actual problem to the other side without demanding anything or revealing your bottom line.

Today's real-world practice

In one real conversation today where you want something, swap a closed or "why" question for an open "What" or "How" calibrated question. Journal what you asked, and what changed in the response you got compared to how that conversation usually goes.
