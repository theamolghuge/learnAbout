Cialdini's Principles of Influence

This table grows one row at a time, as each principle gets its own lesson with real spotting-practice. Half-taught rows are marked "pending" rather than filled in early — a cheat sheet you haven't earned yet just teaches you to skim.

From Robert Cialdini's Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion — six principles compliance professionals (salespeople, fundraisers, recruiters) lean on, plus a seventh Cialdini added later. Full citation in RESOURCES.md.

PrincipleOne-line ideaYour defense
Reciprocity Receiving something creates a felt obligation to give something back — even an unrequested, unequal favor. Accept the favor as a favor. If it turns out to be rigged (offered specifically to extract a bigger ask), you're free to redefine it as a trick — a trick creates no debt.
Commitment & Consistency Once you commit to something — especially aloud, in writing, publicly, or with effort — you feel pulled to stay consistent with it, even into much bigger asks. Before honoring a leveraged commitment, ask: would I make that same commitment again, knowing everything I know now? If not, waive it.
Social Proof When uncertain, we treat similar people's behavior as evidence of what's correct — the closer the "similar people" (guests in this room beats "most people"), the stronger the pull. Ask whether the crowd is real (verifiable reviews, people you know) or manufactured (static counters, bought followers, staged lines). Weight manufactured proof at zero.
Authority A title, uniform, credential, or confident tone pulls trust toward a claim, whether or not the authority is real or relevant to that specific claim. Separate the costume from the substance: is this authority verifiable, and actually relevant to this claim — not just impressive-sounding?
Liking We agree more easily with people we like, via similarity, compliments, familiarity, or the halo effect — regardless of whether the case has any other support. Strip away how much you like the messenger. What's actually left of the argument?
Scarcity Limited availability signals value on its own, whether the scarcity is real or manufactured (a resetting timer, a "few left" counter that never runs out). Verify the scarcity is real, and separately ask if you'd want the thing at all without the countdown.
Unity Shared identity (family, team, nation, alma mater) pulls trust even from total strangers — distinct from Liking, which needs actual personal acquaintance. Ask whether this claim or deal would be equally credible from someone outside your group. If not, the identity is substituting for evidence.
