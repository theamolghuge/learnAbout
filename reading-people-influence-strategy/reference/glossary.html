Glossary

Terms are added here only once you've actually used them correctly in a lesson — this is a record of what you know, not a dictionary to cram. Newer lessons should use these exact words rather than inventing synonyms.

The canonical vocabulary for this workspace. Every lesson uses these terms consistently — if a lesson seems to use a word differently, that's a bug, not a nuance.

Self-Regulation

Trigger:
The external event or stimulus — something someone says or does, a situation you walk into. Outside your control entirely.

Reaction:
The automatic, involuntary spike that follows a Trigger — heat in the face, a jump in pulse, the urge to snap back — before any conscious thought has happened. Not fully in your control, but noticing it is a trained skill.
Avoid: "gut feeling," "instinct" (too vague — Reaction specifically means the pre-thought physiological/emotional spike).

Response:
What you actually do or say next. The only one of the three fully within your control — this is the whole point of the Trigger → Reaction → Response distinction.

Dichotomy of Control:
Epictetus's founding distinction (Enchiridion, Ch. 1) between what is "up to us" (our judgments, our Response) and what is not (other people's actions, outcomes, a Trigger). The root idea behind this workspace's whole approach to composure.

Reserve Clause:
Seneca's practice of silently attaching "fate permitting" to any intention whose outcome isn't fully in your control — full effort toward the goal, with the emotional stake decoupled from the part of the Dichotomy of Control that was never yours to guarantee. Applied before a Trigger arrives, unlike the Trigger/Reaction/Response check, which is applied after.

Negative Visualization:
Briefly and deliberately imagining a loss or difficulty before it happens — Marcus Aurelius's morning rehearsal of the difficult people he'd meet that day. Pre-loads your emotional response so a real Trigger arrives with less surprise, and therefore a smaller Reaction. Distinct from rumination: brief, bounded, and calming, not looping and anxiety-inducing.

Strategic Thinking

Forcing move:
A move (in chess, poker, or a conversation) that requires the other side to reply in a specific way, or drastically narrows their realistic options — a check, a bet, a Calibrated question, a Credible Commitment. Easiest to calculate precisely because it leaves the other side few branches to consider.

Passive move:
The opposite of a Forcing move: harmless, doesn't lose anything, but doesn't narrow anything either. The other side stays free to do whatever they want next — usually a wasted turn.

Persuasion

Commitment & Consistency:
The pull to behave in ways that match commitments you've already made — especially ones spoken aloud, written down, made publicly, or that took some effort. The mechanism behind foot-in-the-door: a small "yes" makes a much larger, related "yes" far more likely later.

Would-past-you test:
This workspace's defense against Commitment & Consistency: before honoring a past commitment being leveraged into something bigger, ask whether you'd make that same commitment again, knowing everything you know now. If yes, Honor it. If no, you're free to Waive it — a commitment extracted through a trick doesn't earn the same loyalty as one freely chosen in full knowledge.

Social Proof:
The pull to treat other people's behavior — especially people similar to you, in your exact situation — as evidence about what's correct or normal, strongest when you're uncertain. Manufactured versions (bought followers, staged lines, static "X viewing now" counters) borrow this pull without any real crowd behind them.
Avoid: "peer pressure" (too narrow — Social Proof works even among strangers who'll never interact, not just people pressuring you directly).

Authority:
The pull to trust a claim more because of the messenger's title, uniform, credential, or confident tone — whether or not that authority is real, or relevant to the specific claim. Milgram's 1963 studies showed obedience fall from 65% to 20% when the same instructions came from someone in ordinary clothes instead of a lab coat — the substance was identical; only the costume changed.
Avoid: "expertise" as a synonym — Authority is the perceived pull, which real expertise earns but a costume can borrow without any.

Liking:
The pull to agree with people we like, triggered by similarity, compliments, familiarity, cooperation, or the halo effect (one positive trait, often attractiveness, making someone seem more trustworthy and competent across the board with no actual evidence).

Scarcity:
The pull to value something more simply because it appears limited or about to disappear — fires whether the limitation is real or manufactured (a countdown that resets, a "few left" counter that never runs out).

Unity:
Cialdini's seventh principle (added in Pre-Suasion, 2016): the pull of a shared identity — family, team, nation, religion, alma mater — distinct from Liking because it requires no personal acquaintance at all, only membership in the same group. Its most damaging form is affinity fraud: scams that specifically target people who share a group identity with the scammer.

Reciprocity:
The near-universal norm that receiving something creates a felt obligation to give something back — the first of Cialdini's principles of influence. The favor and the repayment don't have to be equal size for the pull to work.
Avoid: "quid pro quo" (that implies an explicit, negotiated trade — Reciprocity is the automatic, often unconscious pull that happens even with an unrequested, unequal favor).

Rigged favor:
A favor offered specifically to trigger Reciprocity toward a later, disproportionate ask — as opposed to an honest favor, given with no expectation of repayment. The favor can look identical from the outside; what differs is the giver's intent and what happens if you don't reciprocate.

Negotiation

Calibrated question:
An open-ended question starting with "What" or "How," designed to hand a problem back to the other side while giving them a feeling of control — never "Why" (reads as accusation) and never a closed yes/no question (easy to refuse outright).

Mirroring:
Repeating the last one to three words the other person said, as a question, with zero opinion of your own attached. Reliably invites elaboration — the same mechanism behind waitstaff earning larger tips by verbally mimicking customers' orders.

Labeling:
Naming an emotion you observe in someone else — "It seems like…" / "It sounds like…" — without claiming to feel it yourself. Defuses a named negative emotion, amplifies a named positive one.
Avoid: "I understand," "I feel for you" (claims about yourself, which invite disagreement; a Label is an observation about them, easy to confirm and hard to argue with).

Counterfeit Yes / Confirmation Yes / Commitment Yes:
Voss's three kinds of agreement: a Counterfeit Yes is an easier exit than saying no, with no real commitment behind it; a Confirmation Yes is an innocent reflex to a black-or-white question; a Commitment Yes is the real deal, backed by real reasons and durable under repeated questioning (the Rule of Three).

Stated objection (vs. Real objection):
The objection someone voices first, which is very often not the actual concern underneath. Surfaced by combining a Label with a Calibrated question, rather than argued against directly.

Power & Strategy

Credible Commitment:
Schelling's paradox: voluntarily and visibly removing your own ability to back down makes your position stronger, because the other side must now treat it as fixed. Only works if breaking it carries a real, third-party-verifiable cost — otherwise it's a stated position, not a commitment. The opposite direction of Commitment & Consistency (that's someone else's lever on you; this is your own lever on the situation).

Never Outshine the Master:
Greene's Law 1: people insecure in their own authority sometimes turn against whoever makes them look or feel inferior, even in good faith. Read defensively (spotting the Pattern against you), never adopted as personal strategy.

Manipulation Awareness

Dark Triad:
Three overlapping personality dimensions named by Paulhus & Williams (2002): Narcissism, Machiavellianism, and Psychopathy. Everyone has some measure of each — these are dimensions, not categories, and not a diagnostic checklist for labeling real people.

Pattern (vs. Episode):
A behavior repeated across time and different contexts, as opposed to a single bad episode explained by circumstance. This workspace's core discipline for judging trust: adjust based on Patterns, never on one Episode.

Elicitation:
Extracting information without raising suspicion that specific facts are being sought — via feigned ignorance, flattery, a deliberate false statement ("bracketing"), or manufactured rapport. Each is a Cialdini principle aimed at information instead of a sale.

Pretexting:
Fabricating a believable identity or scenario (IT support, an auditor, a vendor) to gain trust and compliance without independent verification.

Active Listening & Cold Reading

Barnum/Forer effect:
Vague, universally-true statements feel personally accurate even when given to everyone identically. Forer's 1948 study: identical generic profiles, rated 4.26/5 accurate on average by every recipient.

Sharp (vs. Vague):
Whether a statement is tied to something specific and verifiable about this one person (Sharp), or would land equally well on almost anyone (Vague, i.e. Barnum). The test for telling genuine insight from cold reading, regardless of how personal either one feels.

Nonverbal Reading

Baseline:
How a specific person normally moves, speaks, and holds themselves in a low-stakes moment. Entirely individual — there is no universal baseline, which is why single-gesture "dictionaries" (crossed arms, eye contact) don't hold up.

Deviation:
A change from someone's own Baseline, tied to a specific topic or moment — not a change from some general norm.

Cluster:
Multiple channels (voice, posture, gesture, pacing) deviating at once. Weighted far more heavily than a single deviation, which is close to noise on its own.

Deviation cluster:
A Cluster of Deviations from someone's Baseline, landing together at one specific moment. A flag worth a follow-up question — never, on its own, a verdict about lying or hiding something.

Timing (in tell-reading):
The same ostentatious behavior correlates with weakness when it happens early, before much is at stake, and with strength when it happens later, right after a genuinely costly move. The context step of Baseline → Deviation → Cluster → Context, made concrete — see Elwood's correction to the "weak means strong" poker myth.
