Lesson 4 of 5 · ~10 min · The vital few

Choosing Your Few Things

Lessons 1–3 taught you how to focus once you've sat down. This lesson is about a different, higher-stakes kind of focus: deciding what's even allowed to compete for your attention in the first place.

This is the lesson closest to what you actually asked for: getting your attention back from too many things, so a few of them can become great instead of all of them staying mediocre.

Why spreading out costs more than it looks like

You already know from Lesson 1 that switching between tasks within a single session is expensive — attention residue, ~23 minutes to recover focus. The same mechanism operates at a larger scale, over weeks and years, for a reason that's less obvious: goals don't just compete for your time, they compete for the same limited pool of attention and willpower even when you're not actively working on either of them.

A peer-reviewed meta-analysis on goal conflict found that when two goals draw on the same finite resources — time, energy, attention — progress on one measurably comes at the expense of the other, and the psychological toll (rumination, stress) is real, not just a productivity footnote. "Get better at my craft" and "chase five different side projects" aren't neutral toward each other. They're actively competing, all the time, even when you're only consciously thinking about one of them.

Depth compounds. Breadth doesn't.

Cal Newport's career capital theory (from So Good They Can't Ignore You) makes the positive case for the same idea: rare, valuable skill comes from deliberate practice concentrated in one domain over a long stretch of time. Skill in one area accumulates — each hour builds on the last. Skill spread across five areas doesn't accumulate the same way; you're perpetually a beginner in four of them while barely intermediate in the fifth. "Jumping around and not doing any," in your words, isn't just a focus problem — it's a compounding problem. Time spent switching domains is time where nothing is accumulating.

Greg McKeown's Essentialism gives this a name: "the disciplined pursuit of less but better." Not doing more things faster — deliberately doing fewer things, chosen well, so each one can actually reach the bar you're capable of. His test for whether something belongs on your list: if it's not a clear yes, it's a no. Not "maybe," not "if I have time" — those are how a list of a few things quietly becomes a list of fifteen.

The exercise: force the ranking

Knowing "I should focus on fewer things" doesn't tell you which ones. The tool below is a popular prioritization exercise, often (probably wrongly) attributed to Warren Buffett — the origin story about his pilot has been debunked as most likely fabricated. Whether or not Buffett ever said it, the mechanism is sound on its own merits, so it's used here for what it does, not for who supposedly said it.

The mechanism: list everything currently pulling at your attention — projects, skills, ambitions, commitments. Then force yourself to pick only five as your real priorities. The uncomfortable part, and the actual point of the exercise, is what happens to everything you didn't pick: it doesn't become "someday." It becomes an explicit avoid-at-all-costs list — because a half-abandoned pursuit sitting in the background is exactly the kind of open loop that produces attention residue (Lesson 1) and goal conflict (above), even while you're not touching it.

Exercise — 10 minutes, be honest List everything currently competing for your attention below, one per line — be exhaustive, include the things you feel guilty about not doing as much as the things you're actively doing. Then select up to 5 as your real vital few. This saves in your browser.

      What this changes day to day

      The other three lessons now have a filter. Before a time block (Lesson 3) goes on your schedule, it should serve something on your vital-few list — if it doesn't, that's worth noticing, not just accepting. Before you say yes to a new opportunity, project, or course, check it against the avoid list you just wrote, not just against how interesting it sounds in the moment. Interesting is not the same test as essential.
