Attention Management: Quick Reference

Everything in this workspace, compressed. If you only ever read one page, read this one.

The four facts worth knowing

  1. Attention residue is real. Switching tasks before finishing leaves part of your mind stuck on the old task — you're never fully "in" the new one. (Leroy, 2009)
  2. Recovery from interruption is slow. On average it takes ~23 minutes to return to the depth of focus you had before an interruption. (Mark, UC Irvine)
  3. 90% of distraction starts inside you. Boredom, anxiety, loneliness, fatigue — the phone is the escape hatch, not the cause. Fixing only the external triggers (notifications) without addressing the internal ones is why willpower-only approaches fail. (Eyal)
  4. Attention span is trainable, not fixed. Controlled studies show measurable gains in sustained attention from as little as a few weeks of brief daily meditation practice. It is a skill, like a muscle — it responds to use and to disuse.

The system, top to bottom

1. Before you sit down — design the environment

  • Phone in another room, or in airplane mode, not just face-down.
  • Notifications for everything except true emergencies: off by default, not muted-until-you-remember.
  • One browser tab / one application open. Close everything else, don't minimize it.
  • A single, specific task written down before you start — not "work on the project."

2. Starting — use a ritual, not motivation

Motivation is unreliable; a rehearsed starting ritual isn't. Pick a short, fixed sequence (same location, same drink, same first two minutes) that you always do before deep work, so your brain learns to associate it with "focus starts now."

3. During — protect the session

  • Set a timer for a length you can actually sustain today (start at 25–50 min, not 3 hours).
  • When an internal urge to check something hits: name it ("that's an internal trigger"), then return to the task. Don't fight the urge — just don't act on it.
  • If a stray task or worry surfaces, write it on a capture list instead of switching to it. Closing that open loop on paper reduces the attention residue it would otherwise cause.
  • Match difficulty to your current skill. Too easy → boredom → you reach for your phone. Too hard → anxiety → same result. Adjust the task, not just your willpower.

4. Ending — close the loop deliberately

Don't just stop. Spend two minutes reviewing what's unresolved and writing the next concrete step. This is what prevents the task from generating attention residue that follows you into the rest of your day.

5. Between sessions — actually recover

Directed attention depletes with use. A walk outside, or any low-demand environment with "soft fascination" (things that hold interest without effort — moving water, greenery, even a fish tank), restores it faster than another screen-based "break" does. Screen breaks between focus blocks usually aren't breaks at all.

The single highest-leverage change If you only do one thing: put your phone in a different room during focus blocks. It removes both the external trigger (you can't see it) and raises the cost of acting on the internal trigger (you have to get up), which is exactly where implementation-intention research says friction works best.

If-then plans worth pre-committing to

Generic intentions ("I'll focus more") don't survive contact with a bad moment. Specific if-then rules do. Adapt these:

  • "If I feel the urge to check my phone during a focus block, then I open my capture list and write the urge down instead."
  • "If I finish a sub-task mid-session, then I write the very next step before doing anything else."
  • "If it's 3pm and I haven't done my one deep-work block yet, then I do it now, before anything else on the list."
  • "If I'm interrupted, then I write one sentence about where I was before I respond to the interruption."

Choosing your few things

Session-level focus (above) only matters if it's pointed at the right target. Depth compounds — skill built in one domain over time becomes career capital; the same hours split across five domains barely move any of them. Goal-conflict research backs this up: goals competing for the same time and attention measurably slow each other down, even when you're only thinking about one at a time.

  • List everything currently pulling at your attention — be exhaustive.
  • Force a pick of ~5 as your real priorities (see the exercise in Lesson 4).
  • Everything not picked becomes an explicit avoid-at-all-costs list — not "someday," off-limits.
  • Before a new time block, project, or opportunity gets a yes: check it against the list. If it's not a clear yes, it's a no (McKeown's essentialism test).
