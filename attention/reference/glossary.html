Glossary

The canonical vocabulary for this workspace. Every lesson uses these terms exactly as defined here — if a lesson seems to use a word loosely, this page wins.

Core concepts

Deep work

Cognitively demanding activity, performed without distraction, that pushes your abilities to their current limit and creates value that's hard to replicate. Contrast with shallow work.

Source: Cal Newport, Deep Work.
Shallow work

Logistical, low-concentration tasks (email, status updates, routine admin) that don't require much skill and are easy to replicate. Necessary in small doses, corrosive in large ones — it crowds out deep work without you noticing, because it feels productive.

Avoid: "busy work" (imprecise — some busy work is unavoidable; the point is the cognitive-demand distinction, not the tedium).
Attention residue

The cognitive lag left behind when you switch from Task A to Task B before A is finished or consciously closed — part of your attention keeps processing A, leaving fewer resources for B. Strongest when A was unfinished, under time pressure, or emotionally engaging.

Source: Sophie Leroy (2009). See Quick Reference.
Switch cost

The measurable performance hit — in time and accuracy — that comes from changing tasks, distinct from attention residue. Even a "quick check" carries a switch cost on both the way out and the way back in.

Flow

A state of complete absorption in an activity, where self-consciousness fades and time distortion sets in. Produced when challenge and skill are closely matched (see challenge-skill balance) plus clear goals and immediate feedback.

Source: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. Avoid: "the zone" (fine casually, but this workspace uses "flow" for precision).
Challenge-skill balance

The condition, necessary for flow, where a task's difficulty is matched to your current ability — too easy produces boredom, too hard produces anxiety, either one blocks flow.

Directed attention

Effortful, voluntary attention — the kind deep work requires — as opposed to attention captured involuntarily (a loud noise, a notification). Directed attention is a finite resource that depletes with use and needs deliberate recovery.

Source: Rachel & Stephen Kaplan, Attention Restoration Theory.
Attention restoration

The replenishment of depleted directed attention, most effectively triggered by soft fascination — environments (classically, nature) that hold interest effortlessly without demanding directed attention. A deep-work day needs restoration blocks, not just work blocks.

Source: Rachel & Stephen Kaplan, Attention Restoration Theory. See Lesson 5.
Soft fascination

An environment or stimulus that captures attention involuntarily and gently — moving water, greenery, clouds — without requiring effortful, directed attention. The mechanism ART identifies as most effective for restoring a depleted directed-attention tank. Contrast with screen scrolling, which feels passive but still demands continuous micro-decisions.

Source: Rachel & Stephen Kaplan. Avoid: treating any non-work activity as restorative — only low-demand, choice-free engagement counts.

Mechanisms of distraction

Internal trigger

An uncomfortable internal state — boredom, loneliness, fatigue, anxiety, uncertainty — that creates the urge to distract yourself. Responsible for roughly 90% of distraction episodes; the phone is the escape route, not the cause.

Source: Nir Eyal, Indistractable.
External trigger

An outside cue — a notification, a ringing phone, someone walking by — that pulls attention away. Accounts for a minority of distraction, but the easiest category to engineer away.

Traction

Nir Eyal's term for any action that moves you toward what you actually planned to do, as opposed to distraction (anything that moves you away from it). Traction and distraction are defined by intention, not by the activity itself — scrolling can be traction if you deliberately planned downtime.

Practices & techniques

Time blocking

Assigning every part of your working day to a specific block of time for a specific task in advance, rather than working from an undifferentiated to-do list. Converts vague intentions into scheduled, defended commitments.

Implementation intention (if-then plan)

A pre-committed rule in the form "when situation X happens, I will do Y" that moves a response from effortful, in-the-moment willpower to an automatic, rehearsed reaction. More effective than a general intention like "I'll try to focus more."

Source: Peter Gollwitzer (1999).
Shutdown ritual

A fixed end-of-day sequence (review open loops, note next actions, say a closing phrase) that gives your brain permission to stop processing work — a deliberate countermeasure to attention residue carrying over into personal time.

Pact

A pre-commitment device that makes distraction harder in the moment: an effort pact adds friction (app blockers), a price pact adds a cost to failure (money on the line), an identity pact ties the behavior to self-image ("I am someone who finishes what they start").

Source: Nir Eyal, Indistractable.
Body doubling

Working alongside another person (in person or over video, even in silence) purely for the accountability and social pressure of being watched — reliably lowers the activation energy to start a task.

Digital minimalism

A philosophy of intentionally choosing which technologies earn a place in your life based on whether they strongly serve something you value, rather than adopting tools by default and hoping the benefits outweigh the costs.

Source: Cal Newport, Digital Minimalism.

Choosing what deserves your attention

Career capital

Rare and valuable skill, built through sustained deliberate practice concentrated in one domain, that gives you leverage to shape your work on your terms. Accumulates with depth; doesn't accumulate the same way when effort is spread across many domains.

Source: Cal Newport, So Good They Can't Ignore You.
Goal conflict

The measurable cost, in both progress and psychological well-being, that occurs when two or more goals compete for the same finite resources (time, attention, energy) — even when you're only consciously working on one of them at a time.

Source: meta-analysis, Goal Conflict and Psychological Well-Being.
Essentialism

Greg McKeown's term for "the disciplined pursuit of less but better" — deliberately narrowing to a small set of priorities via a strict test (if it's not a clear yes, it's a no) rather than trying to do everything a little.

Source: Greg McKeown, Essentialism. Avoid: "minimalism" (a related but distinct idea about possessions, not priorities).
Vital few / avoid-at-all-costs list

The output of a forced-ranking exercise: a short, explicit list of the priorities that get your real attention (the vital few), paired with an equally explicit list of everything else you've decided not to pursue right now (avoid-at-all-costs) — so the un-chosen items stop quietly competing for attention in the background.
